# Split the 90% target into what code, critics and Chadwick each own

A pipeline can reach 90% or better on the parts of Chadwick's standard that are written rules, but only by moving checking out of the author model's single pass. It cannot reach 90% on "Chadwick accepts this with small edits" by process alone, and nobody can honestly claim that number without about 30 consecutive accepted drafts from a frozen process. The benchmarks are blunt: frontier models reliably hold about three simultaneous constraints, and an author prompt carrying a long rule file lands in the 60 to 75% range for full compliance. The fixes that work all add information the author did not have. Those are a deterministic linter, a fresh-context critic that checks one numbered rule at a time, a factored evidence check that looks up `reference/` without seeing the draft's framing plus Chadwick's own approved passages and written standard. Same-session "review and improve" loops are the weakest option and can make things worse through self-preference. On content, design hiring managers grade visible judgment: named decisions, trade-offs, what failed, who owned what and where people disagreed. That material is exactly what a model cannot invent, so the ceiling on case study quality is set by what Chadwick supplies and approves, not by the loop. The practical answer is a three-part split. Code owns the mechanical rules at near 100%. A calibrated critic owns evidence tracing and structural checks at a measured rate. Chadwick owns voice and judgment, and his accept or reject record is the only honest source of a headline number.

## Same-model review is the weakest loop and new information drives every real gain

The positive results in the self-correction literature share one variable: something enters the loop that the author did not already know. Reflexion reaches **91% pass@1 on HumanEval against 80% for the GPT-4 baseline**, but the signal is executed unit tests ([Shinn et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366)). CRITIC gains come from search, code interpreters and a toxicity API, and its authors call external feedback of "crucial importance" ([Gou et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11738)). Remove that and the effect collapses. Intrinsic self-correction on reasoning tasks fails, and performance sometimes degrades after the model reviews itself ([Huang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798)). A TACL survey found no successful self-correction from prompted model feedback alone outside tasks unusually suited to it, and flagged that many positive studies used unfair setups such as oracle stop signals ([Kamoi et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01297)).

Self-Refine is the optimistic outlier at **about 20% absolute average improvement** across seven tasks ([Madaan et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17651)), but those were short tasks judged partly on preference with GPT-4 era models. It is not a rule-compliance effect size. The study closest to prose found that self-refinement improved fluency and understandability while amplifying self-bias across six model families, and that only larger models and accurate external feedback produced real gains ([Xu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11436)). Multi-agent debate adds nothing once compute is matched. On GSM8K, debate scored 83.0% against 88.2% for self-consistency at nine responses ([Huang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.01798)). Five debate methods across nine benchmarks generally failed to beat chain of thought, though mixing different models consistently helped ([Zhang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08788v3)).

Two designs carry over well to Chadwick's case. The first is per-constraint critique. DeCRIM decomposes the instruction into a constraint list, has a critic name which constraints fail and revises only those. It added **7.3 points on RealInstruct and 8.0 on IFEval** for an open model, against a baseline where GPT-4 fails at least one constraint on over 21% of real multi-constraint requests ([Ferraz et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06458)). Instruction-specific yes/no checklists raised judge agreement with humans from 46.4% to 52.2% and gave 7.8 points from checklist-guided refinement ([Cook et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03608)). The second is draft-blind verification. On long-form biographies, Chain-of-Verification scored FactScore 55.9 at baseline, **60.8 when verification questions were answered alongside the draft, 63.7 when each was answered in a separate prompt without it and 71.4 with that plus an explicit cross-check revise step** ([Dhuliawala et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.11495)). The joint verifier repeated the draft's errors because it was conditioned on them.

That is the same mechanism as self-preference. Model judges recognize their own text, and recognition strength correlates linearly with how much they favor it ([Panickssery et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)). Anthropic reports that a separate model instance screening output "tends to perform better" than one call doing both jobs ([Anthropic, Building Effective Agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). So the worst configuration is the one most sessions default to: the same model, in the same context, reviewing the draft it just wrote with its own reasoning still in view. A critic in a fresh session with only the numbered rules and the draft removes the shared reasoning. A different model family removes some of the shared taste. Expect single-digit to low double-digit gains from either, and cap loops at one or two rounds because gains concentrate in the first pass and self-bias grows with iteration. No study measures these loops on long-form portfolio prose against a large style guide, so the effect size in this exact setting is unknown.

## Compliance falls past three constraints, so mechanical rules belong in code

Chadwick's rule set (evidence tags on every claim, no quotes, no colleague names, no em dashes or Oxford commas, Mondai framing kept out, product terms in a set order, a voice pass, writing for a stranger) sits far beyond the range models hold reliably. On FollowBench, GPT-4's rate of meeting every constraint fell from **84.7% with one constraint to 61.9% with five**, and the authors conclude top models handle "about three constraints" ([Jiang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.20410)). In a business report writing task with 500 simultaneous instructions, the best of 20 models reached 68%, with a bias toward instructions that appear earlier ([Jaroslawicz et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11538)). Material in the middle of long contexts is used less reliably than material at either end ([Liu et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172)). Across 200,000 simulated conversations, every top model performed **39% worse in multi-turn sessions** than single-turn, mostly through rising unreliability and early assumptions models never recover from ([Laban et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120)). Skipped steps and broken rules in a long drafting session are the expected baseline, not a sign of a bad prompt.

This is why benchmark builders themselves switch to programs. IFEval restricted itself to verifiable instructions so a script, not a judge, decides compliance ([Zhou et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911)). ComplexBench had to add rules to its LLM evaluators because model judgment alone was not enough ([Wen et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.03978)). Practitioners give the same order of operations: code assertions first, then a specialized judge only for what remains, validated against expert labels ([Husain, Using LLM-as-a-Judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)). Anthropic's eval guidance says to use deterministic checks where possible, model graders where needed and humans sparingly ([Anthropic, Demystifying evals for AI agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)).

Applied to the portfolio, the rules split cleanly. A linter can enforce em dashes and double hyphens, the Oxford comma pattern, quotation marks, a denylist of colleague names, an evidence tag on every numeric claim and the first-occurrence order of a fixed product term list. A script plus a lookup can confirm that each claim cites a path that exists in `reference/`. Whether the claim actually matches what that file says is a judge's job, and a reference-guided one is where judges do best: supplying a reference answer cut GPT-4's math judging failures **from 70% to 15%** ([Zheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685v4)). Voice, Mondai framing creep and "would a stranger follow this" resist code entirely. A linter wired as a hook enforces its rules on every run because it does not depend on the model remembering to run it. That near-100% figure is an inference from how deterministic checks work, not a measured result. Regex will false-positive on product nouns that double as names, so a check that flags for review is safer than one that silently blocks. The repo's em dash hook already proves the pattern. Extending it to the rest of the mechanical list takes a large share of the standard out of the probabilistic regime for good.

## His edits teach rules, his examples teach voice and his own statement beats both

The draft and edited-draft pairs already in `reference/` are the signal the calibration research uses. In PRELUDE, an agent infers a short written preference from each user edit and carries it forward. The best method, CIPHER, cut cumulative edit distance on email writing **from 31,103 with no learning to 8,391**, about 73% lower. Simply showing the model past edits scored 30,949 to 32,405, no better than nothing ([Gao et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.15269v3)). Dumping the diff history into context is not the method. Distilling it is.

The same paper holds the most important number for this question. Handing the model the true preference outright brought email edit distance down to **1,851, more than four times better than the best learned rules**, and on summaries 6,573 against 32,974 ([Gao et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.15269v3)). Learned rules came out generic, missed brevity and short sentences and struggled with subtle traits like parallel structure. PROSE (Apple, ICML 2025) attacked the generic-rule problem by refining each inferred rule and checking it against several samples from the same writer, and reported 33% better generation than CIPHER ([Aroca-Ouellette et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23815)). Inside PROSE, plain examples beat learned rules on structure, learned rules won on nuanced tone and **the two together beat examples alone by about 9%**; human raters preferred the combination 69.4% of the time against examples alone ([PROSE](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23815v1)). Learned rule quality also correlated only weakly with output quality, so a rule that reads right is not evidence the drafts will land.

Both studies use simulated users on short summaries and emails, so the link to a real senior designer writing long-form case studies is inference. The direction is still consistent. AI-abstracted rules from his corrections are the weakest input. Rules distilled from a diff, verified against several of his other edits and readable by him are useful. His approved passages carry voice better than any description of it, and Anthropic's own prompting guide calls examples "one of the most reliable ways to steer" tone, recommending three to five varied ones and explaining why a rule exists instead of issuing a bare prohibition ([Claude prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). Text continuation from a writer's own opening was the strongest imitation format in one comparison, though that figure comes through a secondary summary ([Jemama and Kumar 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24930v1)). Starting a section from his approved first sentence is a cheap way to use it.

The oracle gap argues for something the repo has deliberately left empty: `docs/VOICE.md` written by Chadwick in his own words. The research also says when. Criteria drift is real: people discover their criteria by grading outputs, and the EvalGen authors state it is impossible to fully specify criteria before judging real drafts ([Shankar et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272)). So the statement should be written after a grading round, not before, and revised in the open when it moves.

Two failure modes keep a human in the final seat on voice. A pre-registered study of 81 writers found that post-editing LLM drafts left the result closer to LLM style than to their unassisted writing, while participants believed the edited text was in their own voice ([arXiv 2604.24444](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.24444)). And a growing rule list is itself a risk, because adherence drops with count and earlier rules get favored ([Jaroslawicz et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11538)). Prune, merge and attach reasons rather than append. Comparing drafts against his unassisted writing gives a second signal his own read cannot.

## Hiring managers grade visible judgment, which only Chadwick can supply

The content standard is clear, and it is the part no pipeline manufactures. Julie Zhuo puts designers who "recount projects that seem like polished little case studies" in the okay column, and credits excellent ones who say what didn't work, "which tradeoffs shaped the final outcome" and "what they versus other people owned and where they disagreed" ([Julie Zhuo](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer)). NN/g's survey of 204 hiring professionals asked for the messy process and "what isn't in the design and why" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)). Emily Backes calls narration without a position "a diary" ([Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)). Lucas Rappart lists "No single decision is named" and "The work is too clean" among senior portfolio mistakes, and prescribes three named decisions per case study: what was designed versus not, what was sacrificed and the tension between teams ([Lucas Rappart](https://www.lucasrappart.com/blog/portfolio-mistakes-senior)).

Ownership should be legible by role, not by name. Backes' model is "I did X, as part of a team that also did Y and Z" ([Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)), and Leslie Yang asks for "who else was on the team" alongside role and duration ([Leslie Yang](https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/what-a-hiring-manager-looks-for-in)). No source found recommends quoting stakeholders, which fits the existing no-quotes and no-colleague-names rules.

The research also corrects the earlier report `reports/What gets designers interviewed.md`. It used Zhuo's "core message" to support a one-sentence takeaway for each case study. Read in full, that line describes the portfolio as a trailer; her case study criteria point the other way. The "argument" practitioners accept is the hiring argument at portfolio level, made with dense evidence of several decisions per case study. No named practitioner supports reducing a single case study to one repeatable point. A case study can still open with a clear frame, since one former Apple design leader advises leading with a concrete result ([Verified Insider](https://verifiedinsider.substack.com/p/the-playbook-to-finding-a-design)), without compressing its body.

This is where the AI question and the content question meet. Practitioners flag AI-written case studies by symptom, not detection: fluent paragraphs that "describe actions, but never explain intent or impact" ([UXfolio](https://blog.uxfol.io/ux-portfolio-ai-case-study-workflow/), a vendor with an AI product to sell) and work that is generic, polished and "missing decision logic" ([Muzli](https://muz.li/blog/how-to-build-a-ux-portfolio-that-actually-gets-you-hired-2026/)). Backes made the same point before AI: sameness is what the reader screens against ([Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)). Rejected options, real constraints and owned disagreements are the content a model is least able to invent and the content reviewers most want. So the pipeline's job is to arrange and phrase decisions Chadwick has already surfaced in `reference/`, and the evidence rule (every claim traces or it does not ship) is doing quality work as well as honesty work. It turns out to be the anti-generic rule too. For framework work like CS2, the guidance is thin and mostly about component systems, but Zhuo's "think in systems" and NN/g's "what isn't in the design" both point to scope boundaries and sequencing as the decisions to show.

## Thirty straight acceptances before "90%" is an honest claim

"90% confidence" and "90% pass rate" are different statements, and the target needs one of them pinned down. The useful one is: across N independent drafts, the 95% lower bound on the acceptance rate clears 0.90. Run to run variance makes small samples nearly meaningless. Even at temperature 0 with a fixed seed, five models across eight tasks showed **accuracy swings up to 15% between runs**, rarely produced identical outputs and were less stable on longer outputs ([Atil et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04667v5)). Anthropic recommends multiple trials per task and notes per-task success can range from 90% on one task to 50% on another ([Anthropic, Demystifying evals for AI agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)).

The arithmetic below uses the Wilson score interval, which NIST recommends over the normal approximation for small samples, and the exact binomial as the conservative check ([NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section2/prc241.htm)). The figures were computed from those formulas, not quoted from a paper.

| Accepted drafts | 95% lower bound on true rate (exact, one-sided) |
|---|---|
| 2 of 2 | 0.22 |
| 5 of 5 | 0.55 |
| 10 of 10 | 0.74 |
| 20 of 20 | 0.86 |
| 30 of 30 | 0.90 |
| 50 of 50 | 0.94 |

To clear 0.90 at 95% confidence takes **29 consecutive acceptances with zero rejections, about 46 runs if one is rejected and about 61 if two are** (exact one-sided; Wilson one-sided gives 25, 42 and 58). An observed 90% never proves a 90% floor: 18 of 20 has a two-sided interval of 70% to 97%, and 27 of 30 has 74% to 97%. A process that is truly about 92% will almost never be provable as at least 90% at Chadwick's volume, and one at a true 97% clears a 30-run test only about 40% of the time.

Per-case-study reliability compounds. Anthropic defines pass^k as the chance that all k trials succeed and recommends it when consistency matters ([Anthropic, Demystifying evals for AI agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). At a true 90% per section, five sections all pass only 59% of the time. "90% per draft" still means better than a one in three chance that some section in a five-section case study comes back.

The counting rules matter as much as the count. Runs must be independent: fresh sessions, one frozen process version and a spread of section types, because twenty retries of one easy section overstate reliability on hard ones. Any change to the process resets a strict claim, so a fixed set of section briefs is needed to compare before and after. "Small edits" needs a written threshold, such as no paragraph rewritten and no claim rewritten, or the line will drift with his mood and the record will mean nothing.

Judges need the same treatment. Agreement with humans is near human-human level on chat tasks, **85% for GPT-4 against 81% human-human** ([Zheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685v4)), but drops toward zero on expert creative writing judgment, where LLM assessors including Claude showed correlations with experts "close to zero" ([Chakrabarty et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14556v2)). Raw agreement misleads when failures are rare: a judge that always passes scores 95% agreement on a set where 5% fail, so true positive and true negative rates must be reported separately ([Husain, Using LLM-as-a-Judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)). One evaluator reached over 90% agreement with its expert in three prompt iterations using binary verdicts and written critiques ([Husain](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)), but personal-preference calibration studies range from 48% to 66% alignment on early attempts ([Shankar et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.12272v1)). Binary checklists beat scales: CheckEval raised inter-evaluator agreement by 0.45 over Likert protocols across 12 models ([Lee et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.18771v2)). The cheapest valid test of a critic is to plant known violations in drafts and count how many it catches.

### What is reachable by process and what is not

| Part of the standard | Owner | Realistic level | How it is shown |
|---|---|---|---|
| Em dashes, Oxford commas, quote marks, colleague names, evidence tag on every number, product term order | Linter as a hook | Near 100% (inference, not measured) | Hook blocks or flags on every run |
| Cited path exists in `reference/` | Script plus lookup | Near 100% | Same hook |
| Claim matches what the reference says | Fresh-context, draft-blind critic with the reference | High but measured, not assumed | Catch rate on planted errors, TPR and TNR on his labels |
| Mondai framing creep, structure, named decisions present, role legible | Per-rule binary critic, different model or new session | Single to low double-digit gains over author alone | Same validation, about 100 labels per failure mode for a tight estimate |
| Voice, writing for a stranger, whether the decisions are the right ones | Chadwick | Not reachable by process | His accept or reject record |
| "Accepted with small edits at 90% or better" | Chadwick plus measurement | Provable only after about 30 consecutive acceptances on a frozen process | Observed count with its 95% interval |

## Conclusion

The question as posed bundles two different targets, and separating them changes what to build. The checkable half of the standard is an engineering problem with a known solution: code for the mechanical rules, a fresh-context per-rule critic for evidence and structure and a loop capped at one or two rounds. Most of the reliability gain available to Chadwick comes from expanding the existing hooks, not from smarter prompting, because a rule enforced by a script stops competing for the author model's limited attention. The judgment half is not a pipeline problem at all. What hiring managers reward is the specific decisions, trade-offs and disagreements in his material, and the strongest calibration input in the research is his own stated standard. That points to a sequence. Grade a batch of drafts with a one-line reason each, cluster the reasons into a failure taxonomy, write `docs/VOICE.md` from that in his words, convert every failure category that can be checked by code into a hook and only then freeze the process and start counting.

Until that count reaches roughly 30 consecutive acceptances, the honest claim is the observed record with its interval, for example "19 of 20 accepted, 95% interval 76% to 99%." That is not a weaker claim. At Chadwick's volume of a few case studies and perhaps 10 to 20 sections, it is the only claim the arithmetic supports. Treating every rejection as a new rule appended to a growing file would push the system back toward the constraint-overload regime that caused the failures. Each rejection should become a hook, a pruned and reasoned rule or an approved example, in that order of preference.
