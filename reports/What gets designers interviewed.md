# End Haven on the teen, not the method

A hiring manager decides to interview after finding, in a scan of a few minutes, visible judgment the designer personally owns: a real constraint, the decision it forced, evidence that proved the designer's own first idea wrong and an honest read of what is still uncertain. For concept work with no launch and no metrics, that decision trail is the whole case, and reviewers treat honest status as integrity rather than weakness. Most reviewers never reach the end of a case study, so the ending is not where the interview is won; it serves the already interested reader, and its job is to leave one sentence about the designer standing, then hand off. For Haven that sentence is "he designs for the risk his user carries and changes his own design when evidence says to." The ending should therefore return to the teen the page opened on, show them at the Identity launch in the moment Haven was built for and stop on the one question the controlled sessions could not answer, with an optional line of genuine reflection only Chadwick can write. That is about 70 to 90 words with one display sentence and no lists, counts, proxies, timeline or slogan. The bigger levers on interview odds sit above the ending: Haven's generic opening line, template section headings and a strongest moment (the Confidante reversal) buried mid page.

## Interviews come from judgment a skimmer can find in minutes

No rigorous study measures time per case study, but named practitioners converge on two stages: a scan of **10 to 30 seconds** that decides whether a case study gets read, then **2 to 3 minutes** on one or two case studies if it earns it ([Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review); [Matej Latin](https://adplist.substack.com/p/only-30-seconds-to-reject-your-portfolio)). Mollie Cox gives herself "about 2-3 minutes per portfolio," reads the first case study for headline, then "product, problem, outcome," then skims again "for numbers" ([Mollie Cox](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/molliecox_hiring-manager-pov-my-portfolio-review-activity-7158534013253545984-Pgmm)). Julie Zhuo describes managers scanning "40 or 50 or maybe 100 URLs" in an hour ([Julie Zhuo](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer)). NN/g's survey of **204 hiring professionals** says they have little time per portfolio and want scannable case studies ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)).

What converts in that window is less "is this good work" than "can I see how this person thinks." NN/g's hiring managers want to see how the designer "started with an opportunity and produced real value," including elements that did not make the cut ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)). Facebook's method judged the idea, usability and craft, and prized a candidate for whom "every decision they've made has a purpose" ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/an-inside-look-at-facebooks-method-for-hiring-designers/)). Backes says what advances a candidate is "A number. A decision. A trade-off you'd make differently," and that designers who name what went sideways are the ones she remembers; **zero visible friction reads as over-edited** ([Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)). Zhuo adds that the best portfolios carry "a really strong core message or concept" and work like a trailer for who the designer is ([Julie Zhuo](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer)).

The red flags are equally consistent: final screens with no path to them ([Indeed Design](https://indeed.design/article/ux-design-portfolio-advice-from-hiring-managers/)), unclear ownership ([Leslie Yang](https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/what-a-hiring-manager-looks-for-in)), generic phrasing and checklist section titles ([Open Doors Careers](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-recruiters-and-hiring-managers-actually-look-at-your-portfolio)) and the textbook linear story. Latin estimates from 1,000+ reviews that about 90% of portfolios fail on cookie cutter research to UI narratives that read like "school homework"; that is one reviewer's estimate, not a survey ([Matej Latin via ADPList](https://adplist.substack.com/p/90-of-designers-are-unhirable)). Backes names the real screen: "Competence isn't the bar... Sameness is what this reader is screening against." The interview itself then probes the same ground. Facebook asked "If you had two more months, what would you have done differently?" ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/an-inside-look-at-facebooks-method-for-hiring-designers/)), and Tom Scott reports the same question in 2026, with a gap between articulation and execution as the red flag ([Tom Scott](https://verifiedinsider.substack.com/p/faq-design-hiring-in-2026)). A case study that names its decisions, its rejected alternative and one unresolved question plants the conversation the interviewer already wants to have.

## Concept work wins on the decision a test overturned

Bootcamp work carries a real penalty that no ending removes. Indeed's Corey Chandler would "rather see a smaller product that actually shipped," because concept work avoids real challenges ([Indeed Design](https://indeed.design/article/ux-design-portfolio-advice-from-hiring-managers/)). What offsets it is visible friction. NN/g advises building realistic constraints into student projects and acknowledging what is unrealistic ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)); MentorCruise's bar is a decision trail documented "as rigorously as a shipped project would" ([MentorCruise](https://mentorcruise.com/blog/product-design-portfolio-how-to-build-one-that-shows-business-impact/)); Rhonda Gilligan at Indeed wants validation testing and "the 'before' if there was one" ([Indeed Design](https://indeed.design/article/ux-design-portfolio-advice-from-hiring-managers/)). With no outcome data, weight shifts to a sharp problem, one pivotal decision with its rejected alternative (ideally changed by testing), constraint handling and craft as a threshold. That ranking is an inference from these sources; nobody has published a quantitative one.

Haven has more of that friction than most concept work. Judged against the criteria above, three moments would start an interview conversation. The strongest is **Confidante selection moving after the intro chat**: testers did not feel confident choosing without getting to know a Confidante, hesitated, went back and forth and some undid their pick, so the step moved ([HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md](../reference/haven/HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md)). It is the only place on the page with a before, a conflict and a resolution, and it shows the designer's own flow proven wrong. Second is the **privacy setup** (disguised icon, app switcher hiding, notifications off, PIN with Face ID), which is threat modelling for a teen whose phone may be seen by family and invites the discretion versus re-entry trade-off conversation ([HAVEN-ENDING-BRIEF.md](../reference/haven/HAVEN-ENDING-BRIEF.md)). Third is the **consent constraint** that made the core user unreachable, which is rare in bootcamp work and documented outside it: in published research **75% of sexual and gender minority teens** would be unwilling or unsure to join a study requiring parental permission ([Mustanski et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5768203/)). Its interview value is the judgment behind the proxy choice, and it already lives in the research section.

The same criteria mark what reads as scaffolding: the opening line about belonging being "a difficult and isolating experience," which says nothing only Haven could say (Latin's boring opening trigger); the identity quiz and scheduling listed without a reason tied to the teen's risk; bare counts such as 6 interviews, 5 insights, 6 tests and 4 insights, which MentorCruise calls process narration rather than evidence ([MentorCruise](https://mentorcruise.com/blog/usability-testing-what-your-portfolio-entry-says-about-you-as-a-researcher/)); and template section names. The three strong moments share one idea, and it is the core message Zhuo asks for: Haven is designed around the teen's exposure risk, and the designer changed his design when evidence said to.

## The ending's job is to leave that one sentence standing

Few readers arrive. NN/g's eyetracking found **81% of viewing time in the first three screenfuls** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)), and reviewers "very rarely" read word for word ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)). The reader who reaches Haven's ending is already interested and deciding whether to spend an hour in an interview. Put plainly, **the ending's job is to leave that reader with the designer's judgment about Haven and its teen, in one short beat, then hand off to the next case study.** It is not the place to prove anything new, because the proof is above it.

The single takeaway should be: **he designs for the risk his user carries and changes his own design when evidence says to.** The two halves map to what concept work must prove without launch data: product judgment for a specific person and willingness to be wrong. The competing candidate, "he does careful research under hard constraints," is weaker. It is about method rather than judgment, and it is exactly the thread that dragged earlier rounds into proxy groups. The ending should leave the takeaway standing, not restate it, since Susan Orlean's advice is to avoid summary conclusions and not explain what the story means ([Nieman Storyboard](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2026/01/16/how-to-end-a-story/)).

That job dictates what the ending holds. It holds one image that resolves the opening, which is Leonard Pitts' return to the beginning ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/educators-students/2016/want-a-strong-ending-to-your-story-here-are-3-tips/)): the page opens on a teen isolated without an affirming environment, so it should close on that teen at the Identity launch, met in the way Haven was designed to meet them. It holds one honest open question, because Backes remembers designers who name what is uncertain and Facebook flagged candidates who called their work "perfect" ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/an-inside-look-at-facebooks-method-for-hiring-designers/)). The truthful one for Haven is whether the design holds at home, where it is used, which a controlled session cannot show ([HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md](../reference/haven/HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md)). The next test appears at most as a clause attached to that question. It holds a reflection only if it is real, since a stock "what I learned" is the generic phrasing reviewers spot instantly ([Open Doors Careers](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-recruiters-and-hiring-managers-actually-look-at-your-portfolio)). And it holds no invitation copy, because the next case study card and footer already do that job.

Everything the owner rejected fails one of these tests, which is why the rejections were right rather than a matter of taste. A tally of findings or counts is a recap. Proxy groups and consent logistics restate the research section and make the ending about method. The slogan "a profile gives information, a conversation gives trust" explains the story's meaning and flattens nine findings into one. A repeated MVP timeline re-shows a visual the reader already decoded. Static rows on a page whose rows are interactive cost trust at the moment the page should feel finished, since weak signifiers cost users **22% more time** in NN/g's testing ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/flat-ui-less-attention-cause-uncertainty/)). This also corrects the earlier reports in this folder: their recommended bookend on "the teens the study could not reach" and their six block ending with a hypotheses table both centered research and measurement, and they should not be revived.

## Four changes above the ending matter more than the ending

The highest leverage change is the **opening line**. It is what the 30 second reviewer reads and it is currently generic. A line only Haven could say would do more for interview odds than any ending: the teen's phone being visible to family, or the Trevor Project finding that **40% of LGBTQ+ young people found home affirming against 68% who found online spaces affirming** ([Trevor Project 2024](https://www.thetrevorproject.org/survey-2024/)), which is the cleanest single argument for a digital product. The second is **surfacing the Confidante reversal near the top**, in the overview or first outcome line, so the skimmer who never scrolls still sees a decision testing overturned; Cox reads "product, problem, outcome" first ([Mollie Cox](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/molliecox_hiring-manager-pov-my-portfolio-review-activity-7158534013253545984-Pgmm)). The third is **headings that state findings instead of template names**, so Research, Usability testing and Solutions stop pattern matching to the bootcamp sequence reviewers screen against, while the non formulaic content inside them carries into the scan. The fourth is **giving each feature its reason at the point of the feature**: text-only chats, the identity quiz and scheduling each need one clause tying them to the teen's risk, or they read as a feature inventory. Two smaller items follow: the MVP section should say why Identity must come before Community for this user, and if session recordings survive, recounting how many testers hesitated or undid their pick turns "some" into a defensible "x of 6" in the testing section, never in the ending.

## Recommended ending: the teen at home, and the question still open

**Concept, in one sentence:** close on the teen the page opened with, at the Identity launch, able to hide Haven and talk to a Confidante before trusting one, and on the one thing the designer still does not know about whether that holds where they live.

| Beat | What it holds | Type and length |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The teen, met | A present tense picture of the Identity launch from the teen's side: Haven kept behind a disguised icon, a conversation before any choice of Confidante. It resolves the opening's isolation and carries both halves of the takeaway without naming them | One statement at 40px, about 20 words |
| 2. The open question | What testing could not show: whether the flow holds at home. The next test rides on it as one clause | One paragraph at 20 to 24px, about 35 words |
| 3. Reflection (only if real) | One sentence in Chadwick's words on what designing Haven changed in how he works | One sentence, same paragraph style, about 20 words; drop it if there is no true answer |
| Handoff | The next case study card, the only interactive element in the zone, at the standard section gap | Existing component |

Total: about 55 words without the reflection and 70 to 90 with it, three text elements at most, one sentence at display size, no rules, containers, arrows or rows. Draft copy for beats 1 and 2, built only from the evidence and brief:

> At the Identity launch, a teen can keep Haven behind a disguised icon and talk with a Confidante before choosing one.
>
> Testing in a controlled session changed how that choice works. It could not show whether the flow holds at home, where Haven is actually opened, so that is the next test, with 18 and 19 year olds who still live with family.

This is an ending beat rather than a section because it resolves the page's opening instead of adding to its body, and it stops on an owned uncertainty instead of trailing into future work. The next test clause is the only research element, it is the owner's chosen step from 30 September and it can be cut to "It could not show whether the flow holds at home, where Haven is actually opened" if it still reads as logistics. Two alternatives were weighed and rejected. Ending on the Trevor finding that one accepting adult is associated with **40% lower odds** of a suicide attempt ([Trevor Project](https://www.thetrevorproject.org/research-briefs/accepting-adults-reduce-suicide-attempts-among-lgbtq-youth/)) is emotionally strong but makes the last word a case for the product rather than a read on the designer, and a mortality statistic as a closing flourish risks feeling used; it belongs near the opening if anywhere. Ending on reflection alone is the highest upside version but depends entirely on an answer that does not exist yet.

The owner has to supply three things before this ships:

| Input | Why only Chadwick holds it | What happens without it |
|---|---|---|
| A true sentence on what designing Haven changed in how he works | It is his experience; the brief lists it as an open fact | Beat 3 is dropped; the ending still works at about 55 words |
| Whether the takeaway sentence matches how the homepage and the other two case studies position him | Positioning is his call | The ending copy stays as drafted, but the takeaway may need a word change to stay consistent |
| Approval of the beat 1 wording, including "talk with" versus "chat with" and whether "choosing" or "trusting" names the decision | Voice is his, and `docs/VOICE.md` is deliberately empty | Draft copy stays a draft |

## Conclusion

The research shifts where Haven's effort should go. The ending cannot win the interview, because the people who decide in 30 seconds never see it; the opening line and the first screen can. What the ending can do is make the interested reader's last thought the right one, and the right one is about Haven's teen and the designer's judgment on their behalf, not about how the research was run. Every failed round shared a single cause: the ending kept answering "how do you know?" when the reader at that point already believes the work and is asking "who is this designer?"

That reframing also explains why an honest open question beats any results substitute. For a concept with no launch, naming precisely what the work cannot yet prove, in terms of the user's real life rather than the study's method, is the most senior move available, and it is the question an interviewer would ask anyway. Handing it to them is the invitation.
