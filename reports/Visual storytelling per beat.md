# Pick the form before the template

Walls of text come from choosing a layout before choosing what the reader should see. Every editorial desk and research paper in these notes uses the same order: name the one point a beat must land, pick the visual form that shows that point, then pick the layout that serves the form. For CS2's mostly qualitative beats, the chart choosers designers usually reach for (FT Visual Vocabulary, Data to Viz) cover almost nothing. The usable references are Bach et al.'s data comics patterns for how beats relate, Lengler and Eppler's Periodic Table and the Evergreen and Lyons Qualitative Chart Chooser for conceptual forms, plus Tversky's research on what the marks in a diagram mean. A handful of design write-ups (Linear, Figma, Stripe, Duolingo, Basecamp) show the practice: each claim gets one isolating figure. The still test is well supported by evidence. Readers don't reliably interact with graphics, and animation has not beaten good static diagrams across studies, so a beat that fails as a flat image is unfinished, not waiting for motion. On the Haven grid (12 columns, 40 margins and gutters), the line length rule alone keeps body text at 5 or 6 columns. That leaves 6 or 7 columns beside it for a figure and turns width changes into the pacing device. The step should go into `CS2_PROCESS.md` as process text for loop step 5. It should borrow two named parts of installed skills and treat one community skill as reading. No installed or community skill picks a form per beat for qualitative stories, and his standing rule rules out writing one.

## Every source puts the reader's point before the picture

The NYT graphics desk's working method, as Sergio Peçanha described it for the 2014 Ebola coverage, was to read reporters' drafts for points about trends, places or processes. The desk sketched rough ideas, killed what did not survive and let one editor own form and placement ([Source](https://www.storybench.org/?p=972)). The Pudding's Ilia Blinderman gives the same order. First think through the points you need to communicate, then ask which visualization expresses each. Static drafts get tested in Keynote or Figma before any code ([Source](https://pudding.cool/process/how-to-make-dope-shit-part-2/)). Jonathan Corum calls sketches "not commitments" and would rather have an ugly sketch with one clear thought than a polished one that misses ([Source](https://style.org/stdp3/)). **No desk publishes an outline to graphics template**, but every one runs idea first, form second, layout last. Nobody starts from a layout.

The research literature gives that order a formal shape. Lee, Riche, Isenberg and Carpendale define a visual data story as a set of story pieces. Each piece is visualized for one intended message and annotated so it cannot be misread without a presenter. The pieces are then put in a meaningful order ([Source](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/StorytellingProcess-CGA2015.pdf)). They place static infographics in the asynchronous, low participation setting, where annotation has to carry the message. A portfolio sits in exactly that setting. Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic's practitioner version adds two tests that fit a case study page. Horizontal logic means the action titles alone, read in order, tell the story ([Source](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2013/12/horizontal-logic)). Vertical logic means everything inside one page reinforces its single point ([Source](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2013/12/vertical-logic)). Her sticky note storyboard (brainstorm the pieces, then group, choose a structure and discard) is the beat list in physical form ([Source](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2018/11/1/swdchallenge-sticky-notes)).

The first fork in the decision is whether the beat needs a picture at all. Reuters Graphics puts it flatly: if a story does not benefit from a graphic, they don't make one ([Source](https://onlinejournalismblog.com/2020/12/16/striking-the-balance-between-graphic-design-and-data-journalism-design-is-a-conversation/)). After the NYT cut back on interactives, Archie Tse reported the desk was writing and editing a lot more text ([Source](https://garciamedia.com/?p=2509)). Text only is therefore a legitimate form for a beat, chosen on purpose. What causes walls of text is ending up with prose by default, never having asked whether the reader could see the point instead. The SRCCON newsroom panel adds a review test: a fresh pair of eyes either gets the chart or doesn't, and WSJ's Becky Bowers called a chart that leaves you unable to explain its point a chart failure ([Source](https://2015.srccon.org/docs/transcripts/reportergraphics/)).

## Qualitative beats need diagram catalogues, not chart choosers

**The well known choosers ask about numbers.** The FT Visual Vocabulary sorts more than 70 forms into nine relationships (deviation, correlation, ranking, distribution, change over time, magnitude, part to whole, spatial, flow). Its only non numeric forms are Venn, network, chord and the Priestley timeline ([Source](https://github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/blob/main/visual-vocabulary/README.md)). Datawrapper's 2025 guide is current but numeric only ([Source](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/chart-types-guide)). Critics add that chooser trees oversimplify and that most guidance reduces to "some channels work better than others" ([Source](https://filwd.substack.com/p/choosing-chart-choosers-with-nick)). For CS2, the FT covers the rare numeric beat and nothing else. It has no hierarchy, process, cycle, two by two or decision category.

Three catalogues do cover conceptual beats. **Lengler and Eppler's Periodic Table of Visualization Methods** (2007) lists 100 methods, and its concept, strategy and metaphor groups name forms no chart chooser has: cycle diagram, cause effect chain, decision tree, swim lane, layer chart, concentric circles, concept skeleton, magic quadrant and strategy canvas. Each is tagged as process or structure, overview or detail, convergent or divergent ([Source](https://www.visual-literacy.org/periodic_table/periodic_table.pdf)). It is unmaintained since 2007, and its consulting forms can read as cliché in a design portfolio. **Evergreen and Lyons' Qualitative Chart Chooser 3.0** opens with the question of what story you are trying to tell. Its rows are hierarchy, flow, comparison, cluster and comment, with forms such as concept mapping, journey map, flow or path diagram, spectrum display and "change photos". That last one is the only explicitly non numeric before and after form in any chooser reviewed ([Source](https://stephanieevergreen.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/Qualitative-Chooser-3.0.pdf)). Ribecca's **Data Visualisation Catalogue** has concept, hierarchy and "how things work" functions, but its conceptual entries are thin ([Source](https://datavizcatalogue.com/search/concepts.html)). For naming the beat type itself, Microsoft's SmartArt taxonomy (list, process, cycle, hierarchy, relationship, matrix, pyramid, picture) matches qualitative beats almost one to one. Cite it for the type and a richer catalogue for the form, because SmartArt reads as PowerPoint ([Source](https://support.microsoft.com/en-US/Office/graphics-visuals/choose-a-smartart-graphic)). Dan Roam's six ways of seeing pair who or what with a portrait, how much with a chart, where with a map, when with a timeline, how with a flowchart and why with a multivariable plot ([Source](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-the-back-of-the-napkin/)). That mapping comes from secondary book summaries; Roam's own text was not read.

**The closest thing to a beat type to form table is Bach et al.'s data comics design space.** It crosses six content relations (narrative, temporal, faceting, visual encoding, granular, spatial) with nine panel layouts, from large panel through annotated, tiled, grouped, grid, parallel, network and branched to linear. Named patterns sit in the cells: exposé for opening a context, before/after and time sequence for change, contrast and alternatives for options, build-up for introducing a structure part by part, overview+detail and the-larger-picture for zooming. Space-walkthrough narrates across one big image ([Source](https://aviz.fr/~bbach/datacomics/Bach2018designpatterns.pdf)). Segel and Heer's seven genres (magazine style, annotated chart, partitioned poster, flow chart, comic strip, slide show, film) work as a coarser per frame menu. The authors refuse to map message to genre, saying there is no right answer in advance ([Source](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/narrative.pdf)). Applying Bach's rows to qualitative beats is an inference. Neither paper tested non data stories.

**The marks themselves carry meaning, and that decides between forms.** Barbara Tversky's review shows boxes contain, lines connect and arrows assert sequence or cause, though arrows are ambiguous. Up reads as more or better. Time runs left to right. Cycles are read clockwise from twelve o'clock. Glyphs help when their natural meaning matches the idea and interfere when it doesn't ([Source](https://www.tc.columbia.edu/faculty/bt2158/faculty-profile/files/2011_Tversky_VisualizingThoughts.pdf)). Larkin and Simon explain why diagrams win at all. Two representations can hold the same information, but a diagram puts what an inference needs in one place ([Source](https://mechanism.ucsd.edu/bill/teaching/F12/cs200/Readings/larkin.whyadiagramissometimesworth.1987.pdf)). The practical consequences for CS2 are concrete. A tree implies a way through it, so a "hierarchy with no way through it" beat should use nested containers with no arrows. A beat about falling behind should show lateness horizontally, because a downward line reads as failure. A two by two only earns its place when a real second axis exists, because renaming the axes changes the whole argument ([Source](https://www.strategy-business.com/article/05110)). Concept maps need labelled links, because an unlabelled line states no proposition ([Source](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~paritosh/papers/sketch-to-models/Novak-Canas-TheoryUnderlyingConceptMapsHQ.pdf)).

Sequencing across beats has its own evidence. Hullman et al. found professional linear stories change one dimension at a time between views. Readers strongly prefer single change transitions and rank temporal transitions above comparison and comparison above granularity. Sequences with perfect parallelism were remembered significantly better ([Source](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2013-StorySequence-InfoVis.pdf)). Nicky Case's rule for explanation is the narrative version: link ideas with "therefore" and "but", never "and then" ([Source](https://ncase.me/StanfordTalk/transcript.html)). Stolper et al. add the cheapest device there is for tying copy to a figure: color the term in the text with its color in the diagram ([Source](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/MSR-TR-2016-14-Storytelling-Techniques.pdf)).

## A still has to carry the point on its own

The still rule is a finding, not a production shortcut. Tse's Malofiej rules were blunt. If you make a tooltip or rollover, "assume no one will ever see it." If the reader has to do anything but scroll, something spectacular has to happen ([Source](https://garciamedia.com/?p=2509); event coverage, [Source](https://www.niemanlab.org/2016/03/at-the-malofiej-infographics-world-summit-the-best-form-of-storytelling-is-often-static/)). A forum claim that 85 percent of readers ignored tooltips is attributed to his slides but could not be verified there, so it stays out of any argument. Russell Samora's Pudding guidance says to stack standalone charts when each step reads clearly as its own chart. It also says to replace hover with fixed annotation and to keep scroll driven transitions only when the transition itself carries meaning ([Source](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/)). The cognitive evidence agrees. Tversky, Morrison and Betrancourt found animation had not proved superior to good static graphics, with many apparent wins coming from animations that simply carried more information ([Source](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf)). Mayer's principles say the same about animation and add segmenting and signaling, which favour one step per frame with the new element cued ([Source](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)). Hohman et al.'s Distill survey finds animation helps with state transitions and causality, but whether it beats a well designed still is contested ([Source](https://distill.pub/2020/communicating-with-interactive-articles/)).

**Annotation is what lets a still stand alone.** Amanda Cox calls the annotation layer critical and says most NYT graphics can stand on their own ([Source](https://simplystatistics.org/posts/2012-06-01-interview-with-amanda-cox-graphics-editor-at-the-new/)). Corum calls annotation the most important thing the desk does. He labels directly on the data, removes keys and uses one consistent arrow style ([Source](https://style.org/ku/)). John Burn-Murdoch says a title that makes a point gets a stronger reaction than a descriptive one ([Source](https://eyemagazine.com/feature/article/conversation-with-john-burn-murdoch)). Cox's "graphs are stronger when they say something" exists only in attendee notes and is flagged secondhand.

For sequences, the still form is the comic strip or small multiples. Bach's data comics work notes that panel layout itself encodes the reading order and needs no interaction ([Source](https://aviz.fr/~bbach/homepage/topics/datacomics)). Tufte's parallelism (repeat the frame, change one thing) and smallest effective difference (make the change the only loud mark) are the drawing rules. Both come from secondary reviews of Visual Explanations, not the book itself ([Source](https://www.intrust.org/in-trust-magazine/autumn-1999/words-in-pictures); small multiples, [Source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_multiple)). McCloud's closure explains why this works. The reader supplies what happens in the gutter between frames, so a cause has to be drawn in the earlier frame or the reader cannot infer it ([Source](https://hoodedutilitarian.com/?p=67985)). Bret Victor's ladder of abstraction gives the two frame pair that suits a framework story: one concrete instance beside the abstraction that holds all instances ([Source](https://worrydream.com/LadderOfAbstraction/)).

Put together, the still test has four checks, synthesized from these sources rather than published as a single rule. Look at the figure with no surrounding copy and see whether the point is visible. Check that it changes only one thing from the previous beat's figure. Check that its headline states a claim, not a label. Check that annotation sits on the graphic, not in a caption block or a hover. Samora's mobile first point gives a fifth: if the figure cannot survive at phone width, the idea is not yet singular ([Source](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/)). Motion added later should only animate the difference between two approved stills, the case Samora and ProPublica's David Sleight both describe, where the transition itself shows a state change ([Source](https://nightingaledvs.com/the-past-present-and-future-of-scrollytelling/)).

## Measure and width changes turn templates into pacing

**Line length sets the text column before anything else does.** Butterick recommends 45 to 90 characters per line ([Source](https://practicaltypography.com/line-length.html)). ProPublica's 2021 redesign dropped the fixed sidebar so supporting media could take any shape or size around a centered text block ([Source](https://www.propublica.org/article/2021-article-page-redesign)). Rob Weychert's composition system for it gave editors a small set of spatial parameters per element on a 4 to 14 column grid ([Source](https://v7.robweychert.com/projects/propublica/)). No newsroom found publishes figure widths in grid columns, so the span table below is arithmetic, not a published rule.

The arithmetic, marked as an estimate: on an assumed 1440 pixel artboard, the Haven grid's 12 columns with 40 margins and 40 gutters leave 1360 pixels of content. Eleven gutters take 440, so each column is about 76.7 pixels. Spans come to about 427 pixels at 4 columns, 543 at 5, 660 at 6, 777 at 7, 893 at 8 and 1360 at 12. At roughly 9 pixels per character for 18 pixel body type, 4 columns holds about 47 characters, 5 about 60, 6 about 73 and 7 about 86. **Body text therefore belongs at 4 to 6 columns. 7 is the ceiling, and any template running body copy across 8 or more columns produces a wall by measure alone, whatever its image count.** If the Haven artboard or body size differs, recompute; the ratios hold.

| Width tier | Haven span (inference) | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Text | 5 or 6 columns, for example text cols 1 to 5 | Body copy within measure |
| Figure beside text | 6 or 7 columns, for example figure cols 7 to 12 | Evidence for the paragraph it sits beside |
| Wide figure | 8 to 10 columns | A standalone diagram that needs room |
| Small multiples | 3 or 4 panels at 4 columns each across 12 columns | Sequences and options with identical frames |
| Full grid or bleed | 12 columns, or 12 columns full bleed to the artboard edge | Pause beats, dense diagrams, hero screens |

Reading order follows placement. A figure beside its paragraph reads as proof of that paragraph. A figure that breaks out to wide or full width after it reads as a new beat. That makes width changes, not template swaps, the pacing device. Mike Bostock's rules point the same way: prefer scrolling to clicking, keep some content scrolling normally and don't autoplay against text ([Source](https://bost.ocks.org/mike/scroll/)). The carousel (MediaSliderAndText) is a stepper. Kosara favours discrete steppers for discrete stories ([Source](https://eagereyes.org/blog/2016/the-scrollytelling-scourge)), but Tse and Samora report readers don't click through them. So a carousel can hold breadth that is nice to have, never a step the reader needs. The two scrollytelling layouts that matter, sticky graphic beside steps and steps over a full width graphic, both have still equivalents: the same split with one figure state per text block, or a full bleed image with annotation baked in ([Source](https://pudding.cool/process/scrollytelling-sticky/)).

The Case study layouts templates are AI generated scaffolds. The LEDGER notes they came from Metalab's 6 column modules, so each one has to be refit to the Haven grid once chosen. The spans in the step 5 table say where each family should land after that refit.

## Strong case studies give each claim one isolating figure

The admired design write-ups almost never present a framework as prose. **Linear** marks its inverted L navigation on a screenshot before showing any change. It carries a whole iteration in one Before / Concept / After strip and demonstrates a parameter by showing contrast at 30 and 100 ([Source](https://linear.app/now/how-we-redesigned-the-linear-ui)). **Figma's UI3 post** captions rejected options with the reason they were cut. It shows three slider variants as labelled small multiples and moves from a wide shot of a crit file to one comment thread ([Source](https://www.figma.com/blog/our-approach-to-designing-ui3/)). **Stripe** draws a constraint as a shaded region of impossible colors and a before and after as two sets of curves ([Source](https://stripe.com/blog/accessible-color-systems)). **Duolingo** sets the old skill tree beside the new linear path, the closest published analogue to a curriculum structure changing shape ([Source](https://blog.duolingo.com/new-duolingo-home-screen-design)). **Basecamp's hill chart** teaches one progress model in about eight figures, each adding one idea, which is the build up pattern in practice ([Source](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/3.4-chapter-13)). Counts from the pages run at about one visual per two paragraphs in Linear and Figma. Portfolio case studies are shorter than these posts and probably need a higher ratio, but that is an inference.

Hiring managers' published guidance points the same way. NN/g says reviewers scan rather than read and asks to see directions that did not make it ([Source](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)). Taylor Palmer asks for screenshots annotated to point at what is different and headings that carry a finding. He calls exploration walls with no tie to what changed low value ([Source](https://www.uxtools.co/blog/5-principles-of-exceptional-case-studies-in-ux-portfolios)). No source in the notes shows a published design case study using a two by two or a formal state diagram, so one in CS2 would be the case study's own move rather than a borrowed pattern. Simon Pan's Uber study and Airbnb's visual language post could not be read.

The CS2 map already matches the best of this evidence. One base diagram that gains one element per section is Bach's build-up pattern ([Source](https://aviz.fr/~bbach/datacomics/Bach2018designpatterns.pdf)) and Segel and Heer's consistent visual platform with a progress tracker ([Source](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/narrative.pdf)). It follows Hullman's one change per transition and Basecamp's figure sequence. Its known risk is crowding by section 6. The fix is Tufte's smallest effective difference: earlier elements fade so the newest one is the loudest mark.

> 5. **Layout and visuals.** Done beat by beat, after the voice pass, before any building. A beat is one point the reader has to come away with; a section usually has two to four. For each beat, in this order. Source: Lee et al. [4], NYT graphics desk [22].

- **(a) What the reader must understand.** One sentence, written as a claim, not a topic ("nothing connected the pieces", not "the existing product"). Read the claims for a section in order: they should tell the section on their own. If two beats make the same point, merge them. Source: Lee et al. [4], Knaflic horizontal and vertical logic [25][26].
- **(b) Classify the beat, then pick the form and name its reference.** First ask whether a sentence says it as well. If yes, the beat is text only and that is the form. Otherwise classify it with the table below and pick the form named there, writing the reference beside it (for example "options: labelled options grid, Figma UI3; Bach contrast"). Use one figure per claim. Draw the difference rather than both whole states. Label every arrow or leave it out. Numeric beats only: if it is one number, it is a stat, not a chart. Source: Reuters [21], Bach et al. [3], Tversky [6], dataviz and artifact-diagramming skills (resource sourcing notes).
- **(c) Still test.** Look at the figure alone, with no copy around it, as a flat image. It passes only if the point is visible without the paragraph, the figure changes one thing from the previous beat's figure, the headline above it states the claim, labels sit on the figure (no legend or caption block doing the work) and it still reads at phone width. Nothing the reader needs may live in a hover, a carousel slide past the first or an animation. Motion comes later and only animates the change between two approved stills. Source: Tse [17], Samora [18], Cox [19], Corum [15], Tversky et al. [7].
- **(d) Template and spans.** Use the table to pick the Case study layouts family that fits the form. State it on the Haven grid: 12 columns, 40 margins and gutters. Body text sits at 5 or 6 columns and never wider than 7. The figure takes what is left beside it, or breaks out wide or full when the beat is a pause or a sequence. Write the spans out, for example "text cols 1 to 5, figure cols 7 to 12". The template is a starting point. It is refit to the grid after it is chosen, never chosen because it is already on the grid. Large image slots take real desktop screens. Smaller slots take the map state or a diagram. All image slots stay empty for the imagery he chooses. Source: Butterick [28], ProPublica [29][30], grid arithmetic in this report (an inference, not a published rule).
- **(e) Share per beat before building.** Bring the section as a short list: per beat, the claim, the beat type, the form with its reference, the still test result and the template with spans. He corrects it there, because that is the cheap stage. Building starts only after his yes, and a correction goes back to (a) for that beat, not to the last build. Source: Corum on sketches [16], Knaflic storyboard [27], his global design rule (CLAUDE.md).
- **Across beats.** Change one thing between neighbouring figures. Default to time order. When parts are parallel (several demands, several options), give each the same internal pattern. Link beats with "but" or "therefore". Colour a term in the copy the same colour it has in the figure. Never put two text only blocks at the same width back to back. Source: Hullman et al. [2], Case [49], Stolper et al. [5].
- **The map.** Each section's map state is the previous state plus one element, the new element loudest and the earlier ones faded. It sits in a small slot beside the real screen and passes the still test on its own. Source: Bach build-up [3], Segel and Heer progress tracker [1], Basecamp hill chart sequence [39].

**Beat type to form to template.** Spans assume the Haven grid and are an inference from measure plus arithmetic. Source: Bach et al. [3], Lengler and Eppler [10], Butterick [28].

## Conclusion

Templates were never the cause of the walls of text. The order of decisions was. A template picked first gets filled with the copy already written. A form picked first forces the question of what the reader should see, and the template then only has to hold it at the right width. The still test is the other half. A visual that cannot carry its point as a flat image is prose with a decoration, and the research gives no reason to expect motion to rescue it.

The open risk sits with the references, not the method. Every qualitative mapping in the table transfers research built on data stories to framework beats, and no study has tested that transfer. The beat list he reviews in (e) is the actual test. If a form keeps getting corrected for the same beat type, the table row is wrong and should change, not the beat.
