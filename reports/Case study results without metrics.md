# Let Haven's evidence prove the judgment

Haven does not need outcome metrics to earn a hiring manager's trust, and inventing any would cost it more than their absence. Across hiring manager interviews and portfolio guidance, the accepted substitute for launch data on concept work is a visible trail from evidence to decision: what testing showed, what changed because of it, how success would be known after launch and what was deliberately left unmeasured. Haven already has the single strongest artifact that trail can hold: testing showed participants hesitating, going back and forth between Confidante profiles and undoing their choice, and the design responded by moving selection from step 3 to after the intro chat at step 5. The honest numbers are small and that is fine: 3 plus 3 proxy interviews, 6 moderated sessions, 2 proxy groups and counts reported as "x of 6", never as percentages. The research constraint is itself a finding worth stating, because the guardian consent a minor needs to join a study is the disclosure Haven exists to prevent, and published research shows most sexual and gender minority teens would refuse or hesitate under that requirement. External Trevor Project data can quantify the problem credibly as long as its odds ratios are written as associations. The ending section should close on judgment (a status line, finding to change, hypotheses with a status column, a "chose not to measure" list and guardrails), not on projected impact.

## Hiring managers read results as proof that evidence changed the design

Reviewers want to know something changed because of the designer's work. Sarah Doody puts it plainly: "People don't just want to know what you did, they want to know the impact of what you did," and she lists outcomes and lessons as a required part of every case study ([Career Strategy Lab](https://www.careerstrategylab.com/how-to-write-a-ux-case-study/)). Indeed's UX leaders ask for impact, iterative design based on feedback and "the 'before' if there was one" ([Indeed Design](https://indeed.design/article/ux-design-portfolio-advice-from-hiring-managers/)). For junior and concept work, the guidance converges on a broader reading of outcome: **validated learning, improved usability or a clarified direction** counts as long as the case study shows how something changed because of the work ([UXfolio](https://blog.uxfol.io/ux-case-study-structure/)). CareerFoundry, whose own course produced Haven, tells students without impact data to "lay out how you would measure the impact" and to show next steps ([CareerFoundry](https://careerfoundry.com/en/blog/ux-design/ux-case-study/)).

The skeptics are real and Haven should not pretend otherwise. Indeed's Corey Chandler would "rather see a smaller product that actually shipped," because concept projects skip the trials of real delivery ([Indeed Design](https://indeed.design/article/ux-design-portfolio-advice-from-hiring-managers/)). A results section cannot erase that bias. What it can do is show the trials Haven did face: an unreachable core user, a safety model that had to survive privacy constraints and a test that broke an assumption about how teens choose a trusted adult. MentorCruise's framing is the right bar: "Speculative projects are acceptable when the case study documents the decision trail as rigorously as a shipped project would," and reviewers spend **3 to 5 minutes** on a cold portfolio scanning for problem, constraints, choices and outcome evidence ([MentorCruise](https://mentorcruise.com/blog/product-design-portfolio-how-to-build-one-that-shows-business-impact/)). That source has a commercial interest in portfolio coaching, but its advice matches the independent guidance.

The distrust triggers are specific. Senior reviewers flag round, implausibly large percentages with no baseline, results that cannot follow from the design change and research that did not happen. Their conclusion: **"honest qualitative evidence beats fabricated quantitative"** ([UX Companion](https://uxcompanion.co.uk/ux-portfolio-mistakes)). MentorCruise adds "using numbers habitually rather than defensibly" and warns that process narration such as "I ran 5 moderated tests" is not evidence; the reviewer wants the consequence of the study ([MentorCruise](https://mentorcruise.com/blog/usability-testing-what-your-portfolio-entry-says-about-you-as-a-researcher/)). For Haven that rules out "83% of users" from six people and any projected retention or adoption figure. It also turns the proxy labeling into an asset: saying plainly that interviewees were proxies, not the target teens, is exactly the honesty reviewers say they reward.

## Six sessions support counts, never percentages

Six moderated sessions are a qualitative study. Nielsen Norman Group defines qualitative testing as 5 to 8 participants producing observed problems, used formatively; quantitative benchmarking needs **30 or more participants** under controlled conditions ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/quant-vs-qual/)). Its reporting rule is direct: give individual values such as "Only 1 out of 6 participants completed the task successfully," never "16.7% of participants succeeded" or an average task time ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/metrics-qualitative/)). MeasuringU takes a more permissive line and allows percentages at any sample size if a confidence interval is attached ([MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/should-you-report-numbers-with-small-n/)). The conservative choice is NN/g's, and the arithmetic shows why. Using MeasuringU's adjusted Wald method, the 95% intervals at n = 6 are wide enough to make any percentage misleading ([MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/wald/)):

| Observed | 95% adjusted Wald interval | What it honestly supports |
|---|---|---|
| 2 of 6 | 9% to 70% | The problem exists; frequency unknown |
| 4 of 6 | 30% to 91% | Unlikely to be rare |
| 5 of 6 | 42% to 99% | Very unlikely to be rare |
| 6 of 6 | 56% to 100% | Affects at least a large minority |

The one inferential claim six people can carry is prioritization: a problem seen in 4, 5 or 6 of 6 is very unlikely to be rare in the wider population. Nielsen's five user rule is about finding problems, not measuring rates, and it assumes one homogeneous group; with distinct groups NN/g recommends **3 to 4 per group** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/)). Haven's 3 and 3 split fits that guidance for discovery, which is worth one clause in the method note. It also means SME and student results should never be compared numerically; describe contrasts in words.

What Haven can derive now depends on what was captured then. Attitudinal instruments such as the Single Ease Question and SUS exist only if participants answered them in the session; SEQ is asked immediately after each task ([MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/seq10/)). Any SEQ or SUS figure for Haven that was not collected would be invented. Behavioral counts (task success by level, profile backtracking, undoing the Confidante choice) can be counted after the fact from recordings or timestamped notes, but not from memory, because users and observers forget encountered issues without video support ([Følstad et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13673-017-0100-y)). Task success should be reported in word levels (complete, minor issues, major issues, failure), never averaged as numbers ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/success-rate-the-simplest-usability-metric/)). Severity is a researcher judgment and can be assigned afterward if labeled as the designer's single rating; Nielsen's 0 to 4 scale recommends averaging three evaluators precisely because one rating is unreliable ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-to-rate-the-severity-of-usability-problems/)).

| Measure | Honest from recordings or notes | Honest from recollection only |
|---|---|---|
| SEQ, SUS, confidence ratings | Only if asked and logged | No |
| Task success by level | Yes | Coarse claim only, labeled as recollection |
| Profile backtracking, undoing the choice | Yes, per participant | Descriptive only ("several participants") |
| Time on task | Yes, from video timestamps | No |
| Hesitation | Needs a definition, such as a pause before selecting | Descriptive only |
| Severity rating | Yes, labeled as designer rating | Yes, labeled as designer rating |

Haven's own evidence file settles which column applies. It records that some participants undid their Confidante choice and that no count was recorded, so no number may be stated ([HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md](../reference/haven/HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md)). Unless the session recordings or notes still exist and are recounted, Haven reports the Confidante finding descriptively and lets the design change carry the weight. That is not a weakness to hide. The evaluator effect research found that four evaluators reviewing the same sessions agreed on only a minority of problems, so "problems I observed" is the accurate phrasing whatever the count ([Hertzum and Jacobsen](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/200552976_The_Evaluator_Effect_A_Chilling_Fact_About_Usability_Evaluation_Methods)).

Without a second round there is no before and after measurement. The honest "after" is the design response, so the copy says the move "targets" the hesitation, never that it "reduced" it. The strongest visual is a participant by issue matrix (dots per participant, which shows n) with a frequency column, a separate severity column and a design change column. MeasuringU keeps frequency and severity as independent dimensions because a rare problem can still be critical, and defines minor severity as something that "causes some hesitation or slight irritation" ([MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/rating-severity/)). Undoing a trust decision in a safety product arguably rates higher than that, and the case study should say why in one sentence.

## An unlaunched concept frames outcomes as hypotheses, guardrails and deliberate omissions

Google's HEART framework gives the measurement plan a credible shape. Rodden, Hutchinson and Fu published it at CHI 2010 as five user centered categories (Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention and Task success) paired with a Goals, Signals, Metrics process for mapping product goals to measures ([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/measuring-the-user-experience-on-a-large-scale-user-centered-metrics-for-web-applications/)). Teams pick only the categories that matter ([Lyssna](https://www.lyssna.com/blog/google-heart-framework/)). The fit for unshipped work is that the Goal and Signal columns are pure design reasoning the designer owns outright; only the Metric column waits on launch. Adding a status column (observed in testing, testable after launch, not measurable by design) turns missing results into visible judgment.

Hypothesis statements supply the vocabulary. Jeff Gothelf's Lean UX format reads "We believe [this]. We will know we're right when we see [qualitative feedback] and/or [quantitative feedback] and/or [KPI change]" ([University of Edinburgh summary of Lean UX](https://blogs.ed.ac.uk/website-communications/lean-ux-requirements-hypotheses/)). Amplitude's North Star model adds the leading and lagging split: a North Star metric is a leading indicator of long term value with 3 to 5 input metrics beneath it ([Amplitude](https://amplitude.com/blog/map-your-metrics)). For Haven, lagging outcomes such as wellbeing or suicide risk cannot ethically be attributed to an app without a formal study, so the case study should say so and claim only leading signals a product team could own. A value based North Star (my proposal) is the share of teens who report feeling understood after a first conversation with a Confidante.

Comparable services show how sensitive products measure success, and none of them uses time in app. Crisis Text Line relies on an optional post conversation survey: **86.5%** found conversations helpful, with a stated **22.5% response rate** and self-selection bias named as a limitation ([Gould et al., PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9322288/)). TrevorSpace evaluated itself with a voluntary on-platform survey of 631 respondents using adapted Search Institute instruments, finding 89% felt a sense of community ([The Trevor Project](https://www.thetrevorproject.org/blog/exploring-positive-youth-development-in-online-spaces-3-key-insights-to-create-thriving-positive-outcomes-for-lgbtq-young-people/)). The digital mental health literature argues for "effective engagement" rather than more engagement, noting engagement is not proportional to clinical improvement ([Saleem et al., JMIR Mental Health](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8726056/)), and warns that social media style engagement design can put retention ahead of benefit ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12003299/)). Haven's privacy features sharpen the point: an analytics event that logs "user opened the identity quiz" is itself sensitive data. Aggregate, non identifying counts plus skippable in-app self report is the ethically consistent position, and stating it is a design decision, not a caveat.

That context is where the three ruled out metrics belong, shown as a decision. Identity quiz completion in a controlled session says nothing about completion at home or in public; passcode and disguise icon adoption depend on how safe a teen feels in their own surroundings, which a lab cannot reproduce; intro chat to booked session conversion sat after the intro chat and outside the tested MVP ([HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md](../reference/haven/HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md)). After launch, the first two would still be poor success measures because they track feature usage in a context the product cannot observe safely. The third is closer to a genuine leading signal and could be counted in aggregate later, but it measures funnel health, not whether a teen found a safe person. A short "chose not to measure" list naming these alongside time in app and streaks reads as seniority; guardrails (privacy exposure reports, safety reports per 1,000 conversations with time to resolution, Confidante response time) read as seniority too, because they define what must not get worse.

The research constraint also deserves a citable sentence rather than an apology. Federal human subjects rules let an IRB waive parental permission only where it "is not a reasonable requirement to protect the subjects," with substitute protections ([45 CFR 46.408(c), Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/46.408)). In online focus groups with 74 sexual and gender minority teens aged 14 to 17, about **46% were not out to at least one parent** and **75% would be unwilling or unsure** to join a study requiring parental permission ([Mustanski et al., PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5768203/)). Another cited survey of 198 adolescent boys found 62% willing to join a study in general but only 27.7% willing when parental permission was required ([Klitzman et al., PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12240674/)). This evidence base comes from sexual health research, not consumer apps, so the case study should cite it as the documented consent problem, not as a study of Haven's users. It explains why college students and SMEs were the right proxies and names their bias: students recall their teen years from a safer vantage point and consenting families would already skew affirming.

## Trevor Project data quantifies the problem when written as odds

Trevor's figures come from nonprobability samples of LGBTQ+ people aged 13 to 24 recruited online, and its "lower odds" results are adjusted odds ratios from cross sectional surveys. The safe phrasing is "had 40% lower odds of reporting a suicide attempt," never "cut attempts by 40%." Survey names also lag their data by a year (the 2024 survey is 2023 data), so cite by survey name consistently.

| Use in the case study | Statistic | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Problem: help runs through adults | 50% of LGBTQ+ young people who wanted mental health care could not get it; 37% of those did not want to need parental permission; 24% were not out and afraid of being outed | [Trevor Project 2024 Survey](https://www.thetrevorproject.org/survey-2024/) |
| Problem: home is not the safe place | 40% found home affirming vs 68% who found online spaces affirming | [Trevor Project 2024 Survey](https://www.thetrevorproject.org/survey-2024/) |
| Problem: many are not out | 71% had disclosed their sexual orientation to a parent or caregiver; 52% of trans and nonbinary youth had disclosed their gender identity (so roughly 29% and 48% had not, by subtraction) | [Trevor Project, Sept 2023 brief](https://www.thetrevorproject.org/research-briefs/acceptance-from-adults-is-associated-with-lower-rates-of-suicide-attempts-among-lgbtq-young-people-sep-2023/) |
| Design choice: text first | 77% likely to use text, chat or IM in a crisis vs 43% phone; 68% of digital users chose it as more confidential | [Trevor Project, April 2019 brief](https://www.thetrevorproject.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Trevor-Project-Digital-Support-Research-Brief-April-2019.pdf) |
| Ending: why one Confidante matters | At least one accepting adult: 40% lower odds of a past-year suicide attempt | [Trevor Project, June 2019 brief](https://www.thetrevorproject.org/research-briefs/accepting-adults-reduce-suicide-attempts-among-lgbtq-youth/) |
| Ending: why one safe space matters | At least one LGBTQ-affirming space: 35% reduced odds of a past-year suicide attempt | [Trevor Project, Dec 2020 brief](https://www.thetrevorproject.org/research-briefs/lgbtq-gender-affirming-spaces/) |

The accepting adult result replicated in later data: a parent or caregiver's acceptance was associated with about 40% lower odds and a doctor's with 45% lower odds ([Trevor Project, Sept 2023 brief](https://www.thetrevorproject.org/research-briefs/acceptance-from-adults-is-associated-with-lower-rates-of-suicide-attempts-among-lgbtq-young-people-sep-2023/)). That matters for Haven because a Confidante is a non-parent adult; the data says acceptance from adults outside the parent role is also associated with lower risk. The 68% online vs 40% home gap is the cleanest single argument for a digital product. The 2025 Trevor survey and the CDC's 2023 YRBS figures could not be verified at the primary source in this research, so they should not ship until checked; the YRBS is the stronger choice if one stat must survive a skeptic, because it is a nationally representative sample.

## A ready-to-use ending for Haven

The recommended ending runs about 250 to 300 words in six short blocks, in this order: status, what testing changed, why the core user was not tested, how success would be known, what was not measured and what comes next. It stops at the Identity launch (Phase 1), per the evidence file. Bracketed items need Chadwick's wording or a recount before shipping; everything else traces to reference material or cited sources.

> **Where Haven landed**
>
> Haven is a concept. It was designed and tested through Phase 1, Identity, and has not launched.
>
> **What testing changed**
>
> The original flow asked teens to pick a Confidante at step 3, from profiles alone. In six moderated sessions, participants hesitated over that choice, went back and forth between profiles and some undid their pick after making it. [If recounted from recordings: "4 of 6 compared profiles more than once; 2 of 6 undid their choice."] Profiles gave them information but not trust. So selection moved to step 5, after a short intro chat, letting a teen meet a Confidante before committing. This move targets the hesitation I observed; it has not been retested.
>
> **Who I could not test with and why**
>
> Anyone under 18 needs a guardian's consent to join a study. For a teen who is not out at home, that consent is the disclosure Haven exists to prevent. In published research, 75% of sexual and gender minority teens said they would be unwilling or unsure to join a study that required it. I interviewed two proxy groups instead: three subject matter experts and three LGBTQIA+ college students. Both see the problem from a safer distance than a closeted 15 year old, and I treat their input that way.
>
> **How we would know it works**
>
> | We believe | We will know when | Status |
> |---|---|---|
> | Teens trust a Confidante they have spoken to more than one they have only read about | Fewer reversed selections; first chats rated "felt safe" | Problem observed in testing; fix untested |
> | A text-first intro lowers the barrier to reaching out | Most first chats rated "felt understood" in a one-tap, skippable prompt | Testable after launch |
> | Disguise and passcode features keep Haven invisible at home | Zero privacy exposure reports | Measurable after launch, in aggregate only |
>
> **What I chose not to measure**
>
> Identity quiz completion, passcode adoption and intro-to-booked conversion cannot be read from a controlled session, because how safe a teen feels at home or in public drives all three. After launch, Haven would count only aggregate, non-identifying signals and skippable self-report. No time in app, no streaks, no logging of quiz answers. For this user, engagement data is exposure risk.
>
> **What comes next**
>
> Retest the intro-chat-first flow. Then validate with teens themselves, through a study designed with a parental permission waiver and substitute protections of the kind teens in that research asked for: clear confidentiality and comprehension checks. The goal stays small on purpose: one accepting adult is associated with 40% lower odds of a suicide attempt among LGBTQ young people. Haven is built to find that one person.

Three details in that draft are deliberate. The recount placeholder exists because the evidence file forbids a number unless one is recovered; if no recording survives, delete the bracket and the descriptive sentence stands. The hypotheses table carries the owner's ruled out metrics as a decision rather than a gap. The final line uses the odds phrasing and "associated with," which is exactly what the Trevor data supports.

These are the numbers Haven can honestly show, with the evidence tag each carries under the repo's rules:

|---|---|---|
| 3 SME interviews and 3 LGBTQIA+ college student interviews (2 proxy groups) | CONFIRMED | Owner stated; check against the Figma master frame |
| 6 moderated usability sessions | CONFIRMED | Confirm whether sessions used the same 3 and 3 split before saying so |
| Confidante selection moved from step 3 to step 5 | CONFIRMED | Owner stated; match to the flow in the Figma master frame |

Numbers Haven must not show: any percentage from the six sessions, any SEQ, SUS or NPS score, any projected adoption, retention or wellbeing effect and the project's duration.

## Conclusion

The absence of launch data is less of a gap for Haven than it looks, because the most persuasive thing a results section can show is a decision that evidence overturned, and Haven has one. Moving selection behind the intro chat reframes the whole product insight: for this user, a profile is information but a conversation is trust. A reviewer who sees that finding, the honest limits on its measurement and a plan that refuses engagement tracking on privacy grounds is reading the reasoning of someone who would measure a shipped product responsibly.

The consent constraint is the part most portfolios would bury and Haven should lead with. Portfolio guidance rarely addresses it at all. It is backed by federal regulation and published data. It shows that the proxy research was a reasoned response to an ethical wall rather than a shortcut. If the session recordings still exist, a recount is the single highest value hour left on this case study: it is the only way to turn "some participants" into a defensible "x of 6".
