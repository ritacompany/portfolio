# Move plan verification outside the author

The most effective way for an agent to audit its own plan is to stop it from grading itself. Have the plan checked by something that does not share the author's context. In order of strength, that means a deterministic check against evidence, then a reviewer from a different model family, then narrow verification questions answered without sight of the draft, then one fresh-session review. Asking the same agent in the same context to "double-check the plan" is the weakest option, and controlled studies show it often makes things worse: GPT-4's GSM8K accuracy fell from 95.5 to 89.0 over two rounds of self-review ([Huang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.01798v2)). Errors are hard to find, not hard to fix. Once given the error's location, the same models repair it well. Pre-mortems, debate and assumption checks are good at generating candidate failures, but they are not verdicts, and every candidate needs triage against evidence. One caveat: no controlled study compares plan-review methods on open-ended plans. The research covers math, factual recall and formal planning, and the Claude Code tooling evidence is practitioner self-report.

## Self-critique in the same context fails at detection

The core negative results agree with each other. Huang's team found that prompting a model to review its own answer lowered accuracy across tasks. GPT-3.5 on CommonSenseQA dropped from **75.8 to 41.8**, and when models changed an answer they more often turned a right one wrong ([Huang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.01798v2)). Earlier reported gains came from oracle labels telling the model when to stop, or from a weak first prompt ([Kamoi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01297)). On planning tasks it gets worse. GPT-4 on graph coloring scored 16% alone, **2% with self-critique** and 38% with a sound external verifier. As its own verifier it rejected 95.8% of correct answers ([Stechly et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.08115v2)). Checking its own Blocksworld plans, it **accepted 38 of 45 invalid plans** ([Valmeekam et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.08118)).

The bottleneck is locating the error. GPT-4 found the first mistake in a reasoning trace only **39.8%** of the time, but given the true location, backtracking added 18 to 44 points ([Tyen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.08516v3)). Authorship makes this worse in two ways. Models fixed an error when told it was the user's and missed the same error **64.5%** of the time when it was their own ([Self-Correction Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02778)). LLM judges also recognize their own text (GPT-4 at 73.5%) and favor it, and self-recognition drives self-preference ([Panickssery et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076)).

## Gains come from an outside signal or a hidden draft

Every robust positive result has a source of truth the author lacked. In the LLM-Modulo framework the LLM proposes plans and sound external critics decide validity. Back-prompting with a plan validator took Blocksworld to **82% within 15 rounds**, against about 12% for GPT-4 alone ([Kambhampati et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01817v3)). CRITIC's own ablation shows most of its gain disappears when search and code tools are removed ([Gou et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.11738v4)). Even a strong external LLM is not a sound critic: one falsely accepted up to 89.5% of a small model's wrong answers ([SIEVE](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/77914)).

Chain-of-Verification is the best evidence for verification by the model itself, and it works by isolation. The model drafts, writes short verification questions, answers each in a fresh prompt that cannot see the draft, then revises. List precision roughly doubled (**0.17 to 0.36**) and biography FactScore rose from 55.9 to **71.4**. The factored variant beat the one that could see the draft, because that one repeated its own hallucinations ([Dhuliawala et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.11495)). Newer preprints point the same way but are small and single-author. A cross-family reviewer raised olympiad-math accuracy from **52% to 64%**, while same-model review falsely rejected 35% of correct answers against 2% ([Tanveer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04270)). A fresh-session review beat a repeated same-session review but not a single one ([Song](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12123)).

| Check | Why it works | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Deterministic check against files, sources or constraints | Ground truth with no shared blind spot | Strong in formal domains |
| Different model family | Uncorrelated errors, no self-preference | Moderate, small samples |
| Factored questions answered without the draft | Small checks are easier than generation; no copying | Strong for facts, untested on plans |
| One fresh-session review, same model | Removes some anchoring | Weak |
| Same-session self-review, repeated | Shares every blind spot | Negative |

## Debate, pre-mortems and checklists find candidates, not truth

Same-model debate is mostly majority voting with extra steps. At nine responses it scored 83.0 on GSM8K against **88.2 for self-consistency** ([Huang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.01798v2)), and correct agents switch to wrong answers under peer pressure ([Wynn et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.05396v2)). Plans have no majority answer, so debate's one working mechanism barely transfers.

The pre-mortem (assume the plan failed, explain why) reliably lowers overconfidence and produces more failure reasons. In a 178-person study it cut confidence in a flawed plan more than standard critique ([Veinott, Klein and Wiggins](https://core.ac.uk/works/49608374)). The famous "30% better" figure comes from a 1989 study about explaining events, not about plans ([citation record](https://colab.ws/articles/10.1002%2Fbdm.3960020103)), and the method can invent threats. Structured assumption analysis is weaker than its reputation. ACH showed no advantage over no technique (N=227), and trained analysts skipped steps ([Karvetski and Mandel](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/20/200430/jdm200430.html); [Dhami et al.](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/69049/)). The lesson for agents is to force an output for each step, because skipped steps are the same failure in people and models. Fixed yes/no checklists make judges more consistent, not more correct: TICK raised human agreement only from **46.4% to 52.2%** ([Cook et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03608v1)), and a checklist catches only what it was written to catch.

Unbounded review creates its own error. Anthropic warns that a reviewer told to find gaps "will usually report some, even when the work is sound" ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). A 2026 multi-round study found that repeated fresh re-reviews scored worst, apparently because later reviewers invent findings; it was seen only through a search summary ([arXiv 2603.16244](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.16244.pdf)). One pass with a severity bar beats several.

## Claude Code tooling checks execution more than plan logic

Anthropic's guidance separates planning from execution through plan mode and recommends a verification subagent so "the agent doing the work isn't the one grading it". That reviewer sees only the diff and the criteria, never the reasoning ([best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). No official plugin critiques the plan itself. The most adopted workflow, obra/superpowers (about **299k stars**), front-loads quality through questioning and granular plans, then reviews each task for spec compliance ([obra/superpowers](https://github.com/obra/superpowers)). A wrong plan passes spec compliance. Matt Pocock's grilling skill questions the user one decision at a time ([skills.sh](https://skills.sh/mattpocock/skills/grilling)). It is the only common method that catches bad premises, because only the human holds those facts.

Plan-level cross-model review exists only as community tooling. A hook on `ExitPlanMode` sends the plan to Codex read-only before approval ([codex-skill](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/gh/cathrynlavery/codex-skill@main/README.md)). Crucible's Claude and Codex loop reported 55 findings over five rounds, converging 26, 15, 12, 2, 0 ([Crucible](https://github.com/chaseai-yt/crucible)). OpenAI's official plugin reviews diffs, not plans ([codex-plugin-cc](https://github.com/openai/codex-plugin-cc)). Simon Willison reports that cross-vendor review "really does work" in practice ([simonwillison.net](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/5/sqlite-utils-fable/)). None of this is measured.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "how should the agent review its plan" to "what does the reviewer know that the author does not". A plan audit that adds no new information (no files checked, no different model, no hidden draft, no human answer) is theatre, and the evidence says it can lower quality. The practical process follows from that. Write each load-bearing claim and assumption as a separate checkable line. Check it against the actual files or sources. Answer each one in a context that cannot see the plan's reasoning, ideally from a different model family. Run one pre-mortem to surface candidates and triage them against evidence. Send premise questions to the human. Stop after one pass, flagging only issues that break correctness or a stated requirement.

The biggest open gap is that nobody has tested any of this on open-ended plans. A useful next step for any team running plan reviews is to log which findings were real, because without that record a reviewer that always finds something cannot be told apart from one that finds the right thing.
