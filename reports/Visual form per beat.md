# Name the relationship, then draw the beat

A section turns into a wall of text when the designer opens a template before deciding what the reader has to understand. Every source in this research, from newsroom graphics desks to the visualization research canon, works in the opposite order. First, write the beat's point as one complete sentence. Second, name the relationship that sentence rests on: a change, a comparison, a gap, a structure, a process, a cause or a position. Third, pick the least elaborate form that shows that relationship. Fourth, lay the form out so the words sit next to the thing they describe. If no relationship can be named, the beat stays as prose, and that is a valid outcome. For a portfolio made of text, product screens and conceptual material, the chart choosers built around datasets (From Data to Viz, Datawrapper, Abela) mostly do not apply. The references worth naming are the FT Visual Vocabulary for the vocabulary of reader questions, the Periodic Table of Visualization Methods for conceptual diagrams and Dan Roam's six questions as the plain-language bridge between them. On layout, the strongest evidence (Mayer's contiguity and signalling effects and Hullman's sequencing studies) works just as well on a static page. A Figma still that obeys those findings is not a weaker version of the later motion build. It is where the understanding comes from, and motion mainly adds engagement. The one structural change the research forces on the existing templates is this: carousels hide frames behind a click, so any sequence the argument depends on has to be unrolled into a visible row.

## Graphics desks choose the relationship first and the chart last

The newsroom teams that describe their process agree that the story and the reader's question come first and the form is fitted to the message afterwards. The FT writes this into its tooling. Its Visual Vocabulary sorts charts by **the relationship the reader needs to see, in nine groups: deviation, correlation, ranking, distribution, change over time, part to whole, magnitude, spatial and flow**. You choose the group from the message and only then a chart inside it ([FT Visual Vocabulary](https://github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/tree/main/visual-vocabulary)). Each group comes with a one-line rule that works as a question to put to a beat. Ranking applies "where an item's position in an ordered list is more important than its absolute or relative value". Deviation emphasises variation from a fixed reference point. A map is justified only when precise location matters more than anything else. Correlation carries a warning that readers assume causation unless told otherwise.

The WSJ graphics team reorganised around story rather than output. Graphics editors sit with news desks so they are "helping craft the story instead of being the cherry on top", and the team dropped "lower-value dashboards and data dumps". For "Portraits of the Parties" the team started from a text script and then brainstormed visuals for each medium. The online version animated people moving between groups across slides, while print used a single splash of several views, with "very little overlap" between the two ([OpenNews Source](https://source.opennews.org/articles/wsj-graphics-team)). This is the closest documented example of a per-beat storyboard. It also shows that the sequence is designed for the medium that ships, and a long Figma page behaves like print.

Reuters supplies the filter for whether a beat gets a visual at all. Simon Scarr says the team takes on stories "where the visual aspects are essential to the understanding", and that a big story is not automatically a visual one. That quote reached the notes secondhand through Design Week, whose page returned an error ([Design Week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/issues/27-january-2-february-2020/in-house-teams-how-reuters-graphics-visualises-catastrophic-world-events/)). Applied to a case study, a beat about motive, a judgement call or a trade-off with no spatial, structural or comparative shape fails the Scarr test and should stay as prose.

John Burn-Murdoch at the FT supplies the text layer. He says **"Text is where people's attention goes first"**. He writes titles that answer the reader's two questions, "What is this showing me? Why does it matter?", and labels lines directly instead of using legends so readers are not "checking back and forth". His COVID trajectory chart kept the same data and geometry and became "significantly improved... as a piece of communication" through changes to the title, direct labels, colour grouping and a reference line alone ([iMEdD reprint of GIJN](https://lab.imedd.org/en/from-data-to-storytelling-concept-and-design-tips-from-the-financial-times-john-burn-murdoch/)). His animated excess deaths chart, documented by Flourish, reveals one thing per slide and ends on the annotation that explains the shaded gap ([Flourish](https://flourish.studio/blog/masters-reveal-line-john-burn-murdoch/)). The lesson for a portfolio is that fixing the title and annotation of a visual you already have often does more than switching to a new form.

The NYT position on interaction comes from Archie Tse's 2016 Malofiej talk. It is reported secondhand as "If you make a tooltip or rollover, assume no one will ever see it" ([Future of Coding thread](https://linen.futureofcoding.org/t/22673047/i-recall-coming-across-some-opinions-against-interactivity-i)). Gregor Aisch's rebuttal clarifies that the often-quoted 85% referred to readers who did not click one prominent button. His own conclusion still agrees on the core rule: "you should not hide important content behind interactions" ([vis4.net](https://www.vis4.net/blog/in-defense-of-interactive-graphics)). A Figma case study has no hover at all, so everything the reader must take away has to sit on the surface as a title, an annotation or a caption. Some gaps remain. No primary account of NYT per-beat form choices by Amanda Cox or Steve Duenes was retrieved. The widely circulated "annotation layer" quote could not be verified and is not used here.

## The research canon says state the point, then change one thing per step

**Segel and Heer (2010)** coded 58 professional examples into **seven genres: magazine style, annotated chart, partitioned poster, flow chart, comic strip, slide show and film/video/animation**. Genres differ by the number of frames and the ordering of elements, and they "can function like building blocks" ([Segel and Heer](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/narrative.pdf)). Their author-driven profile is linear ordering, heavy messaging and no interactivity, which they find works best "when the goal is storytelling or efficient communication". A portfolio read once by a skimming reviewer sits at that end. Their martini glass (an authored stem that opens into reader exploration) suits a case study that ends on a dense whole-system image or a link to the prototype. Their tactics are structural rather than numeric, so they transfer directly. Visual structuring includes the establishing shot, the checklist, a consistent visual platform and a progress indicator. Highlighting uses colour, framing and size. Messaging covers headlines, captions, labels and annotations. They found messaging under-used and named repeating key points, introductory text and final summaries as missing tactics. Their best example works through text and graphic "providing related but different information". That gives a test for any caption: if it repeats the paragraph, it wastes the image.

**Hullman and colleagues (2013)** provide the only tested sequencing rules. In 42 professional slideshow-style pieces, each transition was "a single change in one dimension" between views. Transitions fell into six types: dialogue (question then answer), temporal, causal, granularity (general to specific), comparison and spatial proximity. In a study of 143 participants, people were **much less likely to choose transitions that changed more than one thing**. At equal cost they preferred **temporal over comparison over granularity**. Sequences with **parallel structure** (the same local pattern repeated across sections) produced significantly better memory for order ([Hullman et al. 2013](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2013-StorySequence-InfoVis.pdf)). A 2017 follow-up found people group views by shared properties and then order within groups ([Hullman, Kosara and Lam](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf13194)). For non-numeric beats the types translate cleanly. Temporal is the project timeline. Granularity is system overview to one component. Comparison becomes the same criterion walked across options, or one option judged on several criteria. Dialogue is a question raised in one section and answered in the next. Causal is a decision followed by its consequence. Granularity ranked lowest, so a long overview-to-detail drill needs a second anchor such as time. All of this was measured on simple charts with crowd workers, so applying it to diagrams and prose is an inference.

**Amini and colleagues (2015)** coded 50 data videos using Cohn's visual narrative categories. An **Establisher** gives context, an **Initial** sets events in motion, the **Peak** is where "the most important things happen" and a **Release** carries the aftermath. The most common pattern was establisher, initials, then a single peak around the middle followed by several release units, which give "ample time for conveying the take away message" ([Amini et al.](https://hci.cs.umanitoba.ca/assets/publication_files/DataVideoStorytellingCHI2015_Revision_Final.pdf)). For a case study, that arc puts the key decision or reframe near the middle and spends the closing sections on what changed. **Kosara and Mackinlay** define a story as "an ordered sequence of steps", where each step can be text, image or visualization. They argue for building complex views "up gradually" and for opening with "a static view that provides a teaser", which they compare to a lede ([Kosara and Mackinlay](https://media.eagereyes.org/papers/2013/Kosara-Computer-2013.pdf)).

The practitioner books supply the process rules. **Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic's Big Idea** "must articulate your unique point of view", "must convey what's at stake" and "must be a complete sentence". She adds that "each slide should have a clear Big Idea" ([Storytelling with Data blog](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2014/02/whats-big-idea)). Her form chooser starts with simple text and tables before any graph ([WIT library TOC](https://vufind.wit.edu/Record/w2038185/TOC)). That makes her the only canonical source who explicitly offers "no chart" as an answer. **Alberto Cairo's** governing idea is that "function constrains the variety of forms that the data can adopt". Name what the reader has to do with the point, then choose a form whose encoding supports it ([Peachpit interview](https://www.peachpit.com/articles/article.aspx?p=1951176); [eagereyes review](https://eagereyes.org/blog/2012/review-alberto-cairo-functional-art)). **Edward Tufte's** principles, reached here through secondary summaries, are to show comparisons, show causality and structure, integrate words and images and remember that "content counts most of all" ([jez.io notes](https://blog.jez.io/tufte-course/); [University of Michigan notes](https://websites.umich.edu/~mmmc/516/notes/TuftePrinciples.pdf)). His case against bullets applies directly to a wall of fragmented case study text: they are "base-touching grunts, which show effects without causes, actions without actors" ([Tufte, PowerPoint does rocket science](https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/powerpoint-does-rocket-science-and-better-techniques-for-technical-reports/)).

The canon disagrees in two places that matter here. The first is minimalism. Tufte's data-ink position runs against **Bateman et al.**, who found embellished charts were read about as accurately as plain ones and were **recalled significantly better two to three weeks later** ([Bateman et al.](https://vis.csail.mit.edu/classes/6.859/readings/pdfs/Bateman-UsefulJunk.pdf)). Wang and colleagues likewise found data comics improved understanding and recall compared with infographics ([TU Delft record](https://research.tudelft.nl/en/publications/comparing-effectiveness-and-engagement-of-data-comics-and-infogra)). The practical position is to cut whatever does not carry the point while allowing a memorable visual anchor. The second is framing. **Hullman and Diakopoulos** show that titles, annotations and omissions "prioritize particular interpretations", and that omission is the technique least likely to be disclosed ([Hullman and Diakopoulos](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2011-VisRhetoric-InfoVis.pdf)). A case study is persuasive by nature, so an annotation should point at evidence that is really there and never invent emphasis the material does not support.

## Layout carries the story when every frame stands alone

No single taxonomy of scrollytelling exists, but the usable ones combine well. The NZZ graphics team (Oesch, Roth and Renner) grouped 50 examples into **five techniques: graphic sequence, animated transition, pan and zoom, moviescroller and show and play** ([Benjamins catalogue](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/idj.22005.oes); [data.europa.eu](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/scrollytelling-introduction)). The Pudding describes the core mechanic as a sticky graphic whose state changes as text steps scroll past, inside a bounded section, because the scrolly part "is not the entire story" ([The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/process/how-to-implement-scrollytelling/)). Mike Bostock prefers ordinary scrolling with position-fixed graphics over snap-to-section steppers. He argues that "making content visible by scrolling is almost always better than hiding it behind a click" ([Bostock](https://bost.ocks.org/mike/scroll/)). The Pudding's responsive guidance supplies the still-first test. Keep the scroll only where transitions are "truly meaningful", such as change over time or spatial movement. Otherwise stack the steps as static charts, and in particular **when each step reads well as a standalone chart** ([The Pudding, responsive scrollytelling](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/)).

Every pattern below has a still form. The still forms are recommendations built from those definitions, not claims made by the sources.

| Pattern | Works as a still | Still form on the 12 column grid |
|---|---|---|
| Graphic sequence (NZZ) | Best of all | Discrete frames in a row or stack, same crop and scale (Hullman parallelism) |
| Sticky graphic with stepping text (Pudding) | Well | Storyboard strip of 3 or 4 key states at 3 or 4 columns each with the step text as the caption under each frame. Alternatively, one fully annotated final state |
| Pan and zoom (NZZ) | Well | Full-width overview with numbered callouts, then 2 or 3 detail crops keyed to the numbers |
| Animated transition (NZZ) | Partly | Before and after, or first, middle and final states. Drop the in-between frames and keep only the states that carry meaning |
| Stepper or slideshow (Bostock, McKenna) | Yes, if unrolled | The carousel template, opened into a visible row |
| Reverse martini glass | Yes | Open on the whole-system image, then sections that each zoom into one part |
| Martini glass (Segel and Heer) | The stem only | Narrative sections first, one dense whole-system image near the end |
| Moviescroller, show and play (NZZ) | Weakly | Filmstrip of 4 to 6 meaningfully different frames, or one poster frame with a caption stating what happens |
| Drill-down story (Segel and Heer) | Poorly | Lay out the one branch the argument needs and list the others in a small index |

The comprehension evidence favours stills. **Mayer's multimedia principles** report replicated effects for spatial contiguity (median **d = 0.79**), signalling (d = 0.41 to 0.46, supported in 24 of 28 tests), coherence (d = 0.70 to 0.86) and segmenting (d = 0.70). Figures differ by review ([Mayer 2020](https://ugc.futurelearn.com/uploads/files/7d/d6/7dd6188d-c343-4311-b064-ac98d2c95abc/Multimedia_Principles._R._E._Mayer__2020.pdf); [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://resolve-he.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/CD5B7AE1279A9AB81F8EEBB53DBEC86E); [Dartmouth summary](https://services.dartmouth.edu/TDClient/1806/Portal/KB/PrintArticle?ID=171655)). Each of these works the same way on a static page. The evidence on scrolly formats themselves is about engagement, not comprehension. McKenna and colleagues (240 participants) found that visuals and navigation feedback affect engagement, while the level of control may not ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/visual-narrative-flow-exploring-factors-shaping-data-visualization-story-reading-experiences/)). Kosara's report of the same work says steppers and scrollers both beat text with static visuals on engagement, with no difference between the two ([eagereyes](https://eagereyes.org/blog/2017/eurovis-2017-thursday)). A July 2026 preprint with 25 designers found interactive formats raised engagement and guideline adherence but **not comprehension** ([arXiv 2607.03023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.03023)). The well-made still therefore carries the understanding, and motion added later is an engagement layer. No study was found that tests a storyboard still against its scrolly original directly.

Attention data sets the pacing. NN/g's 2018 eyetracking found **57% of viewing time above the fold and 74% in the first two screenfuls** ([NN/g scrolling and attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). It also found that the **layer-cake scan**, where readers fixate headings and then read the section they need, is "by far the most effective" scanning pattern, while most users "will read very little from a wall of text" ([NN/g text scanning](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/)). Editorial practice guides add alternation. Uniformly dense spreads fatigue readers and uniformly airy ones feel thin. Pull quotes work as re-entry points when they sit on the same page as their source text ([Made Good Designs](https://madegooddesigns.com/magazine-design-principles/); [WOU magazine layout](https://people.wou.edu/~visuanod/magazine_layout/pullquote.html)). These are practice guides rather than studies. No source gives an evidence-based ratio of text to image for long pages.

For the existing templates, these findings become the following recommendations. The title block's subhead should state the finding, not the topic, because it is what the layer-cake scanner reads. Text plus one image is the contiguity template. Run text across 4 or 5 columns and the image across 7 or 8, with the caption beside the image rather than below the fold. Text plus two or three images is the parallelism template. Use equal cells (two at 6 columns or three at 4) with identical crop and scale and labels in the same position on each frame, so the eye reads the change and not the layout. Full-bleed or 12 column moments earn their space only when the image is itself the evidence, when wholeness or scale is the point (a system map) or when they mark a turn between acts. Where a section runs past roughly a screen of text, a pull quote or big number at 6 to 8 columns gives the scanner a way back in. That threshold is a judgement, not a sourced figure. The carousel stays for supplementary galleries only, because a click-gated frame is the stepper problem that Bostock, The Pudding and Tse all describe.

## Name FT for the question, the Periodic Table for the drawing

The catalogues index form in three different ways, and only one of them fits mostly conceptual material. **Data-first** choosers ask "What kind of data do you have?" From Data to Viz starts from six data types ([data-to-viz.com](https://www.data-to-viz.com/)). That fails when there is no dataset to classify. **Relationship-first** choosers ask what you want to show. These include the FT's nine groups, Abela's four purposes (comparison, distribution, composition and relationship) ([FlowingData](https://flowingdata.com/2009/01/15/flow-chart-shows-you-what-chart-to-use/)), Datawrapper's six sections ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/chart-types-guide)) and the Graphic Continuum's six groups ([PolicyViz](https://policyviz.com/2014/09/09/graphic-continuum/)). Their category names carry over as names for the reader's question even when nothing numeric is drawn. **Knowledge-first** catalogues ask what kind of thinking is involved. Only these were built for non-numeric content.

The **Periodic Table of Visualization Methods** (Lengler and Eppler, 2007) catalogues about 100 methods in six groups: data, information, concept, strategy, metaphor and compound. It marks each method as **structure or process**, as overview or detail and as convergent or divergent thinking ([Lengler and Eppler](https://www.visual-literacy.org/periodic_table/periodic_table.pdf)). Concept visualization covers "mostly qualitative concepts, ideas and plans". Strategy visualization includes the strategy canvas and technology roadmap, and the paper also names the Minto pyramid, square of oppositions, Toulmin map, IBIS argumentation map and Rich Picture. The structure versus process split is the most useful single cut for case study material. A framework or a system is structure. A flow or a timeline is process. **Dan Roam's six Ws** map who and what to a portrait, how much to a chart, where to a map, when to a timeline, how to a flowchart and why to a multiple variable plot. His SQVID adds five choices, including quality versus quantity and delta versus status quo ([Buteau on Roam](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-dan-roam/); [thinkinsights SQVID](https://thinkinsights.net/consulting/sqvid)). **Nancy Duarte's Diagrammer** sorts relationships into flow, network, stack, segment and join ([Duarte](https://www.duarte.com/blog/duarte-diagrammer-thousands-of-free-diagrams-at-your-fingertips/)). The **Data Visualisation Catalogue** has a "Search by Function" covering 16 functions, including concepts, how things work and processes and methods. Every one of its 60 methods has its own page, which makes it the most readable source for a one-line definition ([datavizcatalogue.com search](https://datavizcatalogue.com/search.html)).

The naming rule follows. Use the FT group to name the reader's question (deviation, ranking, part to whole and so on), because it is the most widely recognised vocabulary. Cite the Periodic Table for any conceptual diagram, since it is the only source with peer-reviewed definitions of concept, strategy and metaphor forms. Use Roam when the beat is phrased as a reader question. Fall back on the Data Visualisation Catalogue for a plain definition of a single form. There is one real hole. **The 2x2 matrix, swimlane, customer journey map and before and after as a named form do not appear by those names in any catalogue researched**. Duarte's matrix is the nearest, and it was confirmed only through a secondary source ([Consultant's Mind](https://www.consultantsmind.com/2016/11/21/slideology-2/)). Treat a swimlane or journey map as a process form (Periodic Table process marker, Duarte flow) with a lane or stage axis added. On licensing, the FT vocabulary content is "all rights reserved" even though the repo's code is MIT licensed ([chart-doctor](https://github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor)). No licence was found for the other catalogues, so cite them by name and do not reproduce their images on a public page.

## The best case studies put the reasoning on the scan layer

Reviewers read in two passes, and the first pass is headings plus visuals. NN/g says hiring managers very rarely read a portfolio word for word. It says final screenshots "only tell part of the story", and that reviewers want to know "what isn't in the design and why" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)). In a live review written up by Uxcel, walls of text were "the most common red flag of the session". Reviewer Réka Nagy's rule of thumb is **"30% text and 70% visual in each section"**, where the visual "doesn't have to be a screen", because insight cards, interview quotes and simple diagrams all count. She also warns that a visual with no context "will be just a decoration" ([Uxcel](https://uxcel.com/blog/ux-portfolio-review-red-flags-green-flags)). Dominic Monn says "The Decisions section is what earns callbacks" ([MentorCruise](https://mentorcruise.com/blog/how-to-write-a-ux-case-study-that-passes-the-hiring-managers-cold-read/)). Timing figures circulate (60 seconds, 5 to 10 minutes) without traceable methods. Only the direction, scan before read, is well supported.

The strongest documented exemplars are product team design posts rather than studio pages. Linear's UI redesign post uses a **Before, Concept, After** strip and an annotated screenshot marking the "inverted L" navigation. It also shows exploration boards, an LCH versus HSL comparison that justifies a system choice and the same theme rendered at contrast 30 and 100 to show what one control does. Its prose names the question, the image follows and a short caption says where to look ([Linear](https://linear.app/now/how-we-redesigned-the-linear-ui)). Stripe's accessible colour post shows **a rejected approach that passed the contrast rule and still looked muddy**, side by side, so the reader judges the trade-off with their own eyes before reading the verdict. It also draws the system's constraint as a "thread the needle" plot ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/blog/accessible-color-systems)). Growth.Design holds one image steady across consecutive slides while short narration lines annotate it step by step. That is the sticky-graphic pattern done as a static sequence. It also opens on an everyday analogy before any product screen ([Growth.Design](https://growth.design/case-studies/labor-perception-bias)). Metalab's work listing leads with large screens, outcome numbers and service tags, and its reasoning is mostly implicit at listing level ([Metalab](https://www.metalab.com/work)). For a design leader writing about reasoning, the product team pattern is the closer model.

Three recurring devices cover most of what this portfolio needs to show. The first is a **framework or system**. Draw the model, then show it applied across real contexts in a grid, then vary one parameter to show it is generative, then show the tool or ritual that kept it running (Stripe, Linear). The second is a **rejected option**. Use a row of option thumbnails with the chosen one marked and a single "lost because" line under each rejected one, written as "X over Y because Z". Monn supplies the sentence shape and NN/g the reason to show it. The third is the **problem space or competitive landscape**. In the exemplars this appears as "existing approaches and why each falls short" (Stripe) or a single before screen (Linear), not a plotted quadrant. No verified portfolio example of a 2x2 positioning map was found. A 2x2 earns its place only when both axes encode the trade-off the design actually resolves, with the designer's position plotted and the reason that space was empty. No sourced exemplar diagrams a non-visual operating, scheduling or strategy framework, so a case study that does this well is in open territory rather than copying a pattern.

## A per beat procedure

Run this once for every section before opening a template. Each step comes from the sources above. Step 1 is Knaflic's Big Idea, steps 2 and 3 apply the FT, Cairo and Scarr, steps 5 and 6 apply Burn-Murdoch and Mayer, step 7 applies Hullman and step 9 applies The Pudding.

1. **Reader understanding.** Write the reader's question at this point and the answer as one complete sentence with a point of view and the stakes. That sentence becomes the section headline. If it cannot be written, the beat is not ready, and no layout will fix it.
2. **Relationship.** Name what the sentence rests on using the index below: change, comparison, gap, structure, process, cause, position, part of a whole, overlap or identity. If none applies (motive, judgement, a trade-off with no shape), keep the beat as prose and give it a short, well-headed text block. That is a valid outcome, not a failure.
3. **Essential test.** Ask whether the reader would misunderstand or miss the point without the visual. If not, cut it. A visual added for rhythm alone is decoration.
4. **Form, with a named reference.** Pick the least elaborate form in that relationship's row of the index and record its source name. Prefer a real product screen with annotation over a new diagram whenever the screen already holds the evidence.
5. **Text layer.** Write a visual title that states the finding. Add one annotation on the object at the exact place the claim lives, and keep every other part of the object muted. Write a caption that adds related but different information to the paragraph rather than repeating it.
6. **Layout.** Choose the template the form needs. Text plus one image is for explanation beside evidence. Text plus two or three images in equal cells is for comparison or sequence. Full width is for a whole system or a turn between acts. A title block holds the claim. The carousel is only for optional extras. Keep the words within reach of the visual they describe.
7. **Continuity with the previous beat.** Change one thing only: zoom level, time or subject. Where an earlier diagram or screen can return with new emphasis, reuse it instead of introducing a new picture. Where sections are parallel, such as three decisions or three user types, give each the same internal sequence.
8. **Pacing check.** Read the page as headings plus visuals only. The claim should survive, and the first two screens should carry the whole case. After a dense section, follow with an open one.
9. **Still test.** Check that every frame makes sense cropped out and seen alone. Note which transitions are meaningful (real change over time or movement), because those are the candidates for motion later.

| A process across roles or stages | Flow with lanes or stages added (swimlane, journey map) | No catalogue names these; cite as a process form (Periodic Table process marker, Duarte flow) | Full width, lanes running across the 12 columns |
| A framework or system's structure | Concept map, network, tree, stack, metro map | Periodic Table concept and metaphor groups, structure marker; Duarte network and stack; Data Viz Catalogue network and tree | Full width model diagram first, then a grid of it applied, then a one-parameter variant strip |
| How the parts make up a whole | Segment diagram, treemap, Venn | FT part to whole; Duarte segment; Data Viz Catalogue Venn | Text plus one image, the part that matters highlighted and the rest muted |
| How two things overlap or combine | Venn, join diagram | FT and Data Viz Catalogue Venn; Duarte join | Text plus one image |
| A decision | Decision tree, Minto pyramid, option comparison | Periodic Table concept group, Minto pyramid ([Lengler and Eppler](https://www.visual-literacy.org/periodic_table/periodic_table.pdf)) | Title block holding the decision, then text plus three images of the options |
| A rejected option and why it lost | Side-by-side of the rejected option meeting the rule and still failing; option row with a "lost because" line | Stripe ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/blog/accessible-color-systems)); NN/g ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)); Monn ([MentorCruise](https://mentorcruise.com/blog/how-to-write-a-ux-case-study-that-passes-the-hiring-managers-cold-read/)) | Text plus two or three images in equal cells, chosen one marked |
| A trade-off | Square of oppositions, 2x2 matrix, two-criteria scatter | Periodic Table square of oppositions; Duarte matrix (secondary, [Consultant's Mind](https://www.consultantsmind.com/2016/11/21/slideology-2/)); FT correlation | Text plus one image, both axes labelled with the actual tension |
| A position in a competitive landscape | Existing approaches and why each falls short; strategy canvas; positioning map | Stripe problem framing; Periodic Table strategy group; Roam "where" to map | Text plus three images (one per approach), or one full-width map with the empty space annotated |
| A cause, why something happened | Causal chain, argument map (Toulmin, IBIS), Rich Picture | Roam "why"; Periodic Table argument methods; Hullman causal transition ([Hullman et al.](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2013-StorySequence-InfoVis.pdf)) | Decision section followed by consequence section, same visual reused with the effect highlighted |
| A plan or roadmap | Gantt, technology roadmap, metro map | Periodic Table concept, strategy and metaphor groups | Full width |
| One fact to remember | Big number | Mayer signalling ([Dartmouth summary](https://services.dartmouth.edu/TDClient/1806/Portal/KB/PrintArticle?ID=171655)); editorial entry points ([WOU](https://people.wou.edu/~visuanod/magazine_layout/pullquote.html)) | 6 to 8 columns inside or right after its source text |
| A ranking or order of importance | Ordered list or ordered bar | FT ranking | Text plus one image, or a typographic ordered list |
| Motive, judgement or a trade-off with no shape | Prose | Knaflic simple text option ([WIT TOC](https://vufind.wit.edu/Record/w2038185/TOC)); Scarr filter | Short text block with a headline that states the finding |

## Conclusion

The walls of text are a sequencing failure, not a shortage of visuals. They appear when the template is picked before the reader's sentence and its relationship are named, so the image slot gets filled with whatever is to hand and the reasoning falls back into paragraphs. The research reverses that order, and for this portfolio the reversal has a consequence the templates do not yet reflect. Most beats will resolve to a small set of forms (annotated screen, paired or tripled frames, a model diagram reused with changing emphasis, an option row) plus a deliberate share of prose. The most useful tactic across the whole case study is reuse rather than variety: one diagram introduced early and returned to with a single change per section. That is the static form of the sticky graphic, the tested benefit of parallel structure and the move that keeps each still ready for motion later.

The open ground matters too. No source shows a non-visual operating or strategy framework drawn well in a portfolio, and no catalogue names the 2x2, swimlane or journey map. The framework case study will set its own visual precedent rather than borrow one. That raises the bar on the text layer. Every diagram that has no canonical form needs a finding-style title and one honest annotation to do the explaining a familiar chart type would otherwise do.
