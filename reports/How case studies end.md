# Strong case studies end short, then hand off

The strongest case study endings are small: one resolving beat of roughly 7 to 9 text elements and 20 to 45 words of prose, followed directly by a named next step. Across 30 studio case studies from 9 studios, the most common final section is a results block with 2 or 3 stats (13 of 30), but it clusters at Metalab and Fantasy; the rest end on a qualitative outcome statement, a credits block, the last feature section or a press wall, and none of the 30 admits that results are missing. Type only jumps at the ending when the ending is numeric (Metalab's 64px title and 88px digits, Huge's 146px "The numbers."); narrative endings stay at or below the page's section scale. Among individual designers, titled "Reflection" sections are the minority (4 of 12), and the best concept endings do three things in under 200 words: state the real status, name a concrete next step and give one specific observation. The UX evidence points the same way: few readers reach the end, working memory holds about four chunks, editors cut summary conclusions and static items styled like links cost users time and confidence. For Haven, that rules out a to-do list, a metrics grid and a slogan, and leaves three sound structures: a bookend that returns to the teens the study could not reach (recommended), a Metalab style tally of what actually happened and a short closing note in Chadwick's own voice.

## Nine studios converge on one short resolving beat

Across 30 case studies catalogued on 30 September 2026, the final content section takes six shapes. The table counts one category per page; Instrument pages also appear under credits because every Instrument page ends body, then credits.

| Final section before the handoff | Count | Where it shows up |
|---|---|---|
| Results block with numeric stats | 13 of 30 | Metalab (7 of 10), Fantasy (3 of 3), Collins Figma, Huge McDonald's, Instrument ServiceNow |
| Qualitative outcome or forward statement | 5 of 30 | Metalab Robinhood and The Atlantic, Koto Stack Overflow and Coinbase, Instrument Nike |
| Last feature section, no ending treatment | 5 of 30 | Clay Slack and Snapchat, Work & Co IKEA and Aesop, Ueno Slack |
| Credits or roles block | 5 of 30 | Pentagram (named people), Instrument (disciplines) |
| Press or awards wall | 3 of 30 | Work & Co MTA, Collins Spotify, Instrument Oura |
| Quote as the very last element | 2 of 30 | Metalab Windsurf, Ueno Reuters |

Metalab's template is the most codified and the most relevant to Haven's layout grammar. Atoms ends on a 64px title "Climbing the charts", a 12px "Results" label, one sentence of about 20 words and a row of three stats with hairline tops: **4.8, 4.6k and T10, set at 88px with 20px captions and no imagery** ([Metalab Atoms](https://www.metalab.com/work/atoms)). Calvin Klein, Headspace, Midjourney, Ro, Suno and Uber repeat that exact skeleton ([Metalab Headspace](https://www.metalab.com/work/headspace); [Metalab Uber](https://www.metalab.com/work/uber)). The three Metalab pages without a stat row show what happens when numbers are absent: Robinhood closes on leadership seeing "potential beyond the product", Windsurf on a 40px client quote of about 30 words and The Atlantic on an "Innovation Roadmap" paragraph about experiments "for years to come" ([Metalab Robinhood](https://www.metalab.com/work/robinhood); [Metalab Windsurf](https://www.metalab.com/work/windsurf); [Metalab The Atlantic](https://www.metalab.com/work/the-atlantic)). These observations come from local rebuilds that reproduce Metalab's live section order; the live Metalab next project handoff was not captured.

Other studios keep the ending even quieter. Koto ends on an 11px "Outcome" label, one 24px statement ("Far from becoming a relic, Stack and its community are an anchor of the AI era.") and one paragraph, with **no stats and the statement at the same size used everywhere else on the page** ([Koto Stack Overflow](https://koto.com/projects/stack-overflow)). Instrument's Nike design system page closes on "Vision for the Future", one paragraph and a single 33px statement that the system is "intended to evolve" ([Instrument Nike](https://www.instrument.com/work/nike-digital-design-system)). Work & Co moves its stats into the page header and lets the body end on process, with Aesop closing on an 18px "After Launch" heading and two run-in bold points inside paragraphs ([Work & Co Aesop](https://www.work.co/clients/aesop/)). Clay simply stops on its last feature ([Clay Slack](https://clay.global/work/slack)). Forward-looking content never gets its own section in this sample; it appears as a sentence inside the ending, as in ServiceNow's "the work is far from done" ([Instrument ServiceNow](https://www.instrument.com/work/servicenow-rebrand)). The sample is skewed: 10 of the 30 pages are Metalab, which inflates the stats count.

## Type climbs at the end only when the ending is a number

Endings are bigger than the page only in the numeric finale. Metalab's rebuilds set the stats title at 64px while every other section statement is 40px, with digits at 88px ([Metalab Atoms](https://www.metalab.com/work/atoms)). Huge's McDonald's page ends on a **146px "The numbers." against 43px section headings**, the largest type after its 175px H1 ([Huge McDonald's](https://www.hugeinc.com/case-study/mcdonalds)). Collins sets a 95px hero number above its 72px H1 but keeps the "Impact" heading at the standard 36px and outcome items at 16px ([Collins Figma](https://wearecollins.com/case-studies/figma/)). Everywhere else, the ending matches or undercuts the body: Fantasy's stat numbers equal its 40px section heads ([Fantasy Priceline Penny](https://fantasy.co/work/priceline-penny)), Koto holds 24px, Clay and Ueno keep every chapter at one size ([Ueno Slack, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20211002060011/https://ueno.co/work/slack/)) and Pentagram runs its body tail, credits and "Next Project" label all at 16px, with the only large text being the next project's title at the H1 size of 52px ([Pentagram Xsolla](https://www.pentagram.com/work/xsolla)).

| Ending type | Title or statement size relative to section heads | Prose held | Longest display-size sentence observed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Numeric finale (Metalab, Huge, Collins) | 1.6x to 3.4x larger | 1 to 2 sentences, about 20 to 45 words | Title only |
| Narrative statement (Koto, Instrument Nike, Metalab Windsurf) | Equal, or a single sentence at 33 to 40px | 1 statement plus 1 paragraph | About 30 words (Windsurf quote at 40px) |
| Credits or plain last feature | Equal or smaller | Varies | None |

One finding matters directly for Haven: **no studio in the sample sets more than about 30 words at display size**. Long endings drop to body type; display type is reserved for one sentence. Handoffs follow catalogue size. Studios with a linear order (Pentagram, Koto, Clay, Metalab) use one "next" card, while broad agencies show a 3 to 5 card grid ([Koto Coinbase](https://koto.com/projects/coinbase); [Fantasy BNY Eliza](https://fantasy.co/work/bny-eliza)). A three case study portfolio belongs with the single named card.

## Concept work ends on status, one real observation and the next test

Individual portfolios are more varied, and the experienced designers in the sample mostly had no titled ending at all. Gabriel Valdivia's Daylight ends on its design system section ([Valdivia](https://www.gabrielvaldivia.com/work/daylight)); Steph Parrott's shipped Box study ends on one factual sentence about the release ([Parrott Box](https://www.stephparrott.com/box/)). Only 4 of 12 studies had a titled reflection. The contrast inside Parrott's own portfolio is telling: her side project Plantd ends on "What's Next?" with its real status (beta paused by COVID), a next step and **one concrete observation, that beta users logged food with eggs or cheese, revealing that "plant-based" meant different things to different people**, in about 150 words ([Parrott Plantd](https://www.stephparrott.com/plantd/)). Bettina Bergendahl's unbuilt Malu does the same with an honest status (next is finding a developer) and a conversational contact line ([Bergendahl Malu](https://www.bettinabergendahl.com/work/malu-recipe-meal-planning-app)). The weaker concept pattern lists what the project never did: Travmates' "What I would do differently next time" reads as apology rather than judgment ([Yingrui Chen](https://www.yingruichen.com/travmates)).

Reviewer guidance lines up with those examples. IxDF puts the ideal conclusion at **4 to 5 sentences**, roughly matching the intro ([IxDF](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/how-to-write-the-perfect-conclusion-to-your-ux-case-study)). Erik Kennedy wants two actions at the bottom, read another case study or get in touch, and names "too much text" as a common mistake ([Learn UI Design](https://www.learnui.design/blog/great-ux-ui-portfolio-case-study.html)). The Crit asks designers to separate "measured results, observed behavior, approved direction, shipped work, and what remains untested" and to end on what changed in their thinking ([The Crit](https://thecrit.co/resources/complete-portfolio-guide)). Florian Bölter warns that hiring managers "spot generic phrasing instantly" and that template section titles read like a checklist ([Open Doors Careers](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-recruiters-and-hiring-managers-actually-look-at-your-portfolio)). No verified senior or staff hiring manager quote about the final section specifically was found, and no senior designer's bootcamp concept ending was found to compare against, so these are the best available signals rather than a settled standard.

## Reading research argues for one beat, a clean handoff and nothing that looks clickable

Most readers never arrive. NN/g's 2018 eyetracking study (120 participants) found **81% of viewing time in the first three screenfuls** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)), and Chartbeat data reported by Slate showed most readers stopping near the halfway mark ([Slate](https://slate.com/technology/2013/06/how-people-read-online-why-you-wont-finish-this-article.html)). The readers who reach Haven's ending are the self-selected ones deciding whether to keep going or get in touch, so the ending serves them while the top of the page carries the outcome for everyone else. Those readers often scan in layer cake mode, reading headings and first lines ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/)), so the ending has to make sense from its heading and first sentence alone.

The memory arguments are real but narrower than the UX folklore. The peak end rule comes from retrospective ratings of painful procedures ([Redelmeier and Kahneman, summarized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak%E2%80%93end_rule)), and a 174 sample meta-analysis found the average of the whole experience predicted evaluations about as well as peak plus end ([Alaybek et al.](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/All%E2%80%99s-well-that-ends-(and-peaks)-well-A-of-the-rule-Alaybek-Dalal/b3d5a40cd30e51cc709b942b759e0ca4a9d0979b)). Recency in recall vanishes after **15 to 30 seconds** of distraction ([Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/primacy-recency.html)). The defensible use is modest: the last beat colours the overall impression and is what is in mind at the next decision, so it should be deliberate and it should not try to recap. Working memory holds about **four chunks** ([Cowan 2001](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/11830840_The_magical_number_4_in_short-term_memory_A_reconsideration_of_mental_storage_capacity)), which is the ceiling for how many separate ideas the ending can carry.

Editorial craft supplies the structure. Leonard Pitts' three endings are a twist, a quote or a return to the beginning ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/educators-students/2016/want-a-strong-ending-to-your-story-here-are-3-tips/)); Susan Orlean's advice is to avoid summary conclusions, not explain what the story means and stop rather than trail off ([Nieman Storyboard](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2026/01/16/how-to-end-a-story/)). That is the case against Haven's rejected slogan: explaining the meaning in one line reduced nine findings to one. It is also the case against repeating the MVP timeline, which restates a visual the reader already decoded.

Two NN/g findings govern the handoff and the owner's list constraint. Pages should not dead end, and large whitespace or visual full stops create a false bottom that makes readers think the page is over ([NN/g related content](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/related-content-pageviews/)); footers are a legitimate destination for contact ([NN/g footers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/footers/)). Weak or misleading signifiers cost **22% more time and 25% more fixations** in a controlled 71 user study ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/flat-ui-less-attention-cause-uncertainty/)), and non-clickable items must not borrow link styling ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/clickable-elements/)). On Haven, where list rows are interactive, any ending item that looks like a row will be read as a link at the exact moment the page wants to feel finished. Static content should be prose or bare type with no container, rule, hover or arrow; the only interactive element in the ending zone should be the next case study.

## Three structurally different endings for Haven

All three sit after the last findings section, stop at the Identity launch per the evidence file and hand off to one named next case study card followed by the footer ([HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md](../reference/haven/HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md)). A and C keep the owner's existing Heading/64 "What happens next", a plain label he has already used across every study; B replaces it because its content is mostly what already happened. None uses a list, card, timeline or slogan. Each starts from its own concept. Copy is draft; bracketed items need Chadwick's wording.

| | A. Bookend | B. Tally | C. Closing note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concept | Return to the teens the study could not reach; the next tests are how Haven reaches them | Metalab's results row, filled with what happened rather than projected impact | One short first-person note, no visuals |
| Studio precedent | Poynter return to the beginning; Koto statement plus paragraph | Metalab Ro and Midjourney activity counts | Instrument Nike statement; Koto |
| Text elements | 4 | 9 | 3 |
| Words | 130 to 160 | 60 to 80 | 70 to 90 |
| Largest type | 64 heading, 40 statement | 40 title, 64 digits | 64 heading, 40 statement |

### A. Bookend: the people the study could not reach (recommended)

Structure: Heading/64 "What happens next"; one bright 40px statement of about 20 words; two 20px paragraphs (about 55 words each) set side by side on the 232px indent, or stacked on mobile. Four elements, roughly 140 words.

> **What happens next** (Heading/64)
>
> Nine findings changed the design. None came from the teens Haven is for, because a minor needs a guardian's consent to join a study. (40px, bright)
>
> Retesting comes first: new participants, the revised Confidante selection, a count of how often people still change their pick. (20px)
>
> Then an at-home test, in the moment, with 18 and 19 year olds who still live with family. They can consent on their own and they live in the conditions Haven is built for. Community follows as Phase 2. (20px)

Why this one wins: it is the only option where the next steps resolve the story's central tension instead of trailing after it. The at-home test is the answer to the consent wall, so the ending is a bookend (Poynter) rather than a roadmap, which the craft sources warn makes work feel unfinished. It references all nine findings as a body of work instead of elevating one, it names a real status and one specific next test in under 200 words (the Plantd pattern) and it stays at four chunks. The statement at 40px keeps the page's existing section scale, which is what narrative endings do across the studio sample. The published parental permission data (75% of sexual and gender minority teens unwilling or unsure to join such a study) supports the constraint if Chadwick wants a citation in the body ([Mustanski et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5768203/)). What would change this recommendation: if the page's opening and research sections do not already name the consent constraint, the bookend introduces new information at the end, which the cognitive load evidence argues against, and C becomes the better choice.

### B. Tally: what actually happened, in Metalab's grammar

Structure: a 12px label "Where it stands" plus a 40px title (for example "Tested with proxies, not yet at home"); three figure pairs with digits at Heading/64 and 20px captions; one 20px paragraph of next steps. Nine elements, about 70 words. Suggested figures: "6 / interviews across two proxy groups", "6 / moderated usability tests", "9 / findings, each traced to a design change", then the paragraph naming the retest, the at-home test with 18 and 19 year olds and Phase 2. Use "6 interviews" and "6 usability tests" as separate figures and never sum them to 12, per the evidence file ([HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md](../reference/haven/HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md)).

This is the Metalab parity option. Metalab keeps the stat row even when business outcomes are private, filling it with scope counts such as Ro's 654+ components and Midjourney's 10k+ messages ([Metalab Ro](https://www.metalab.com/work/ro); [Metalab Midjourney](https://www.metalab.com/work/midjourney)). Counts, never percentages, are the honest unit at n = 6. Digits stay at 64 rather than introducing an 88px role Haven does not have. Two conditions decide it. If the findings section already shows all nine with their design changes, the tally is a summary conclusion and should not ship. If Haven's interactive list rows use hairline dividers, the figures must drop Metalab's hairline tops and separate by space alone, or they will read as three more rows.

### C. Closing note: Chadwick's voice, cut to studio length

Structure: Heading/64 "What happens next"; one bright 40px passage of about 35 words; one 24px gray passage of about 45 words. Three elements, no visuals, about 80 words. This keeps the existing Option C concept and its strongest line on measuring less on purpose, while fixing its size problem: the current draft sets about 80 words at 40px, well past the roughly 30 words any studio in the sample sets at display size.

> Haven stops at the Identity launch, before anyone has used it at home. Next I'd retest the revised Confidante selection, then test at home with 18 and 19 year olds who still live with family. (40px, bright)
>
> In the moment, so nothing depends on memory. And I'd measure less than usual, on purpose. For someone who isn't out, a usage log is a record of who they are. Community waits for Phase 2. (24px, gray)

It is the quietest option and the most robust: it needs no setup elsewhere on the page and cannot be mistaken for anything interactive. Its weakness is that it states status and plan without the research tension that makes A land.

### The handoff is part of the ending

For all three, the next element after the ending is one named card for the next case study with its one-line claim, the single-card pattern Pentagram, Koto and Metalab use for a linear catalogue. The gap between the ending and that card should be no larger than the page's standard section gap, because an oversized gap reads as a false bottom. Contact lives in the footer, not duplicated inside the ending. The card is then the only thing in the ending zone that looks clickable, which is exactly the contrast the affordance research asks for.

## Conclusion

The research reframes what Haven's ending is for. Studios with results end on numbers because the numbers are the peak; studios without them end on a single statement at body scale and move on, and none of them apologizes for the absence. Haven's equivalent of a peak is not a metric but the gap its research could not close, and the next test is the one designed to close it. An ending that says so reads as judgment, while an ending that lists future work reads as unfinished.

The practical constraint that emerges is size, not structure. Every option above holds four or fewer ideas, sets no more than about 35 words at display size and leaves the next case study as the only interactive element. If Chadwick keeps those three limits, the choice between A, B and C comes down to whether the page's opening already names the consent problem, which is a check of the Figma master, not a matter of taste.
