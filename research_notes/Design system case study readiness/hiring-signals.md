# What hiring managers and design leaders look for in a design system case study (senior, lead and principal)

Research run 19 Oct 2026. Every finding below comes from a page that was opened in full. Things seen only in search snippets are listed under Gaps as leads, never as findings.

Source quality, read this first:
- Very little published material is about design system case studies specifically. Most of what turned up is generic portfolio advice. Each source is tagged GENERIC or DS SPECIFIC.
- The most promising DS specific sources all sit on Medium or on Medium hosted domains (Into Design Systems, Design Systems Collective). Medium blocked every fetch (403 from Cloudflare), and the Into Design Systems Substack copy is paywalled after its intro. Their content is therefore unverified and appears only under Gaps.
- No source found holds up a named individual's design system case study as good. The examples in section 6 are ones I found and read myself. They are not reviewer endorsed.
- The tool budget ran past fifteen calls (about 27) because the Medium blocks meant fetching other sources instead.

## 1. What hiring managers, design directors and reviewers say they want from a design system case study

### Takeaway
No opened source by a hiring manager speaks to design system case studies specifically; the one DS specific talk found (Justine Montgomery, Stripe, 2025) is behind a paywall. What hiring managers say about case studies in general lines up: start from a goal and end on a measured outcome, show what was left out and why, name your role and who you partnered with, and say what did not work.

### Cited findings
- Etsy's guide (written by its product design recruiter and hiring managers) asks each case study to show "Your approach in starting and ending a project with a goal and an outcome", "The process you used", "How you solve complex problems", "Results and the impact to the team & organization" and "How you measured the outcome". Section "Case study 101". GENERIC. ([Etsy guide](https://extfiles.etsy.com/CareersPage/ProductDesign.pdf))
- Etsy asks for an honest ending: "Showcase the end result/outcome Did your product meet its goals? What worked? What didn't? What did you learn from it?" Section "Present it well". ([Etsy guide](https://extfiles.etsy.com/CareersPage/ProductDesign.pdf))
- Etsy: "Highlight your contribution(s) & collaboration(s) Outline your role, impact in each case study and who you partnered with to make things happen." Section "Present it well". ([Etsy guide](https://extfiles.etsy.com/CareersPage/ProductDesign.pdf))
- Etsy wants the case study to stand alone: "ensure anyone can step in and absorb the problem and solution you developed without having questions about how you did it." Section "Present it well", "Tell a clear story". ([Etsy guide](https://extfiles.etsy.com/CareersPage/ProductDesign.pdf))
- Etsy: "Highlighting your approach in translation of quantitative & qualitative insights into your design decisions" and "Show us how you think and work, we would love to get a sense of your point of view." Section "Introspection tips". ([Etsy guide](https://extfiles.etsy.com/CareersPage/ProductDesign.pdf))
- NN/g survey respondents (204 UX hiring professionals): "I'm curious to know what isn't in the design and why, just as much as I'd like to know why elements made it in." Section "What hiring managers are looking for". GENERIC, 2019. ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/))
- NN/g respondents: "Tell me the problem you were trying to solve, your role, any constraints, project timeline" plus "changes from iteration to iteration and how the research informed the design." Same section. ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/))
- NN/g respondent: "Show me how you started with an opportunity and produced real value for a user and the organization." Same section. ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/))
- NN/g respondents: "Don't just show me the finished product." and "I want to see the messy process and all the work and research that was put in to land on that shiny polished design." Same section. ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)); partly in tension with Tom Scott (2026), who turns down "double diamond frameworks and process-heavy case studies" and prefers portfolios that lead with the actual work ([Verified Insider](https://verifiedinsider.substack.com/p/learnings-from-reviewing-500-design)). The two can both hold: NN/g asks for real decision process, Scott objects to generic method theatre.
- Erik Kennedy, praising Simon Pan's portfolio, advises showing "what you accomplished, and acknowledge what remains", and says it "would be more compelling if he described the business success up-front as well." GENERIC. ([Learn UI Design](https://www.learnui.design/blog/great-design-portfolio-examples.html))
- Into Design Systems describes Justine Montgomery's 2025 talk as "honest and practical advice on building a design systems portfolio that actually works", subtitled "a design systems portfolio that tells a real story and shows real impact." That is all that is readable; the body is paywalled. DS SPECIFIC. ([Into Design Systems](https://intodesignsystems.substack.com/p/how-to-build-a-strong-design-systems))

### Inferences
- Inference: for a design system, "goal and outcome" means a problem the system was built to fix (inconsistency, slow builds, drift between design and code) and a measured change in it. A component inventory alone answers neither Etsy nor NN/g.
- Inference: NN/g's "what isn't in the design and why" translates to components or token tiers deliberately not built. A small startup system has many of these, and recording them now is cheap.
- Inference: Etsy's "What didn't [work]" suggests a senior case study should name a decision that was reversed.

### Gaps
- No opened source quotes a hiring manager or design director about design system case studies specifically. The leads are all on Medium (blocked) or paywalled: Into Design Systems, "How to build a Design System portfolio that gets you hired" (https://intodesignsystems.medium.com/how-to-build-a-design-system-portfolio-that-gets-you-hired-ab15045a29f6). Its search snippet said a Stripe design systems designer listed tokens, documentation, accessibility, systems thinking, collaboration with engineers and product and measurable impact, and advised going deep on one component rather than a whole system. Unverified; the page could not be opened.
- No recruiter guide from a design specific recruiter turned up in the searches.

## 2. Named failure modes

### Takeaway
The sources name generic failures (screens without context, process theatre, missing outcomes, unclear role, sloppy presentation, AI work that "looks vibed"). No opened source names "component gallery with no story" as a design system anti pattern. The DS specific failures below are inferred from what the sources say is missing.

### Cited findings
- Tom Scott turns down "double diamond frameworks and process-heavy case studies", and also "only showing vibe-coded work that looks vibed". He wants to see designers "refining their day-to-day craft as designers alongside building." Poor typography and typos count against applicants, and he says he can judge within about ten seconds whether someone put in real effort. GENERIC, Jul 2026. ([Verified Insider](https://verifiedinsider.substack.com/p/learnings-from-reviewing-500-design))
- Scott: password protected portfolios should be unlocked or not submitted. Etsy says the reverse is fine as long as you share the password ("If your most relevant work is password protected, this is a friendly reminder to share the password."). Conflict between sources. ([Verified Insider](https://verifiedinsider.substack.com/p/learnings-from-reviewing-500-design); [Etsy guide](https://extfiles.etsy.com/CareersPage/ProductDesign.pdf))
- Erik Kennedy, on the Ueno portfolio: "I'd still love to see more about the rationale behind specific decisions." Missing rationale is a weakness even in work he praises. ([Learn UI Design](https://www.learnui.design/blog/great-design-portfolio-examples.html))
- Smashing (Dan Donald) on a design file that never becomes code: "Having an amazing Figma library is great, but if it doesn't at some point become code" (the article goes on to say it stays a set of images without a release process). Section "People-powered". DS SPECIFIC. ([Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/04/artifacts-design-systems/))
- Lovart lists red flags for AI assisted work: presenting the model as having chosen the strategy, hiding heavy human correction, and "Claims of 'fully automated brand systems' with no veto". Its composite candidate feedback reads "We cannot see what you did." Vendor source. Sections 9 and 4. ([Lovart](https://www.lovart.ai/blog/ai-design-portfolio-interview-2026))

### Inferences
- Inference: the DS failure modes that follow from the cited asks are (a) a component gallery with no problem or outcome (fails Etsy's goal and outcome), (b) no rationale for token or component choices (Kennedy, NN/g), (c) a Figma library with no evidence it reached code (Donald), (d) "we built" with no named personal ownership (Etsy) and (e) AI produced pages presented with no human veto shown (Lovart, Scott).
- Inference: the four real case studies in section 6 show (a), (c) and (e) in practice: three of four have no metrics and none shows a governance model or code parity.

### Gaps
- The search snippet for the Design Systems Collective essay "The $200K design system that nobody uses: why most companies are building libraries, not systems" framed "library, not system" as a named failure and gave 80% developer adoption in six months as a marker of success. The page was blocked (Medium), so this is unverified. https://www.designsystemscollective.com/the-200k-design-system-that-nobody-uses-why-most-companies-are-building-libraries-not-systems-73bd0e604995

## 3. Artefacts strong design system case studies show

### Takeaway
No hiring source lists the artefacts it wants in a DS case study. Donald's Smashing case study is the best opened catalogue of what a real working system produces: proposals, anatomy, documentation, testing lenses, versioned releases, a workflow map, a governance definition, usage tracking and a pre-build benefits and risks list. Read with Etsy's "how you measured the outcome", those are the things to capture now.

### Cited findings
- Component proposals: "A proposal starts by recognizing the component's purpose", with sections for purpose and intent, use cases, anatomy and related components. Section "The source of truth and making proposals". ([Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/04/artifacts-design-systems/))
- Anatomy feeds documentation: "What are the elements, spacing units, and typography that make the component?" and the proposal "actually starts the basics of the documentation for it too". Same section. ([Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/04/artifacts-design-systems/))
- Testing lens: "Visual regression (VRT), automated tests, manual testing?", plus prompts for tracking, accessibility, SEO, performance and states. Same section. ([Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/04/artifacts-design-systems/))
- Releases: "every change would be versioned and released immediately to the site", conventional commits used "to automatically trigger versioning of our code", and "a beta release to manually pull into apps to validate before making that change permanent". Section "Workflow, communication and evolution". ([Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/04/artifacts-design-systems/))
- Governance: "Governance: how you define responsibilities and workflows". Section "Outreach". ([Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/04/artifacts-design-systems/))
- Usage tracking: "looking at where a given component is used" to judge the risk of a change. Measures: "we didn't set out a list of KPIs". Section "What to measure?". ([Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/04/artifacts-design-systems/))
- Pre-build case: "Potential benefits" and "Potential risks" lists and a SWOT "as a way to form something robust". Section "The problem space". ([Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/04/artifacts-design-systems/))
- Mehri Russo's case study opens on an audit finding: "There were dozens of buttons styles, incorrectly applied font weights, and single-use icons everywhere." The audit is described in text only, with no image. ([Mehri Russo](https://www.mehrirusso.com/work/design-system))
- Adham Dannaway explains token naming ("The name of each colour token consists of up to 4 words that describe how and where to use the colour on an interface"), a choice and its reason ("I decided to use transparent colours, rather than solid ones, to help ensure components look consistent on different coloured backgrounds") and a test ("I tested the contrast of colours across light and dark mode to ensure consistency"). Jul 2024. ([Adham Dannaway](https://www.adhamdannaway.com/portfolio/figma-design-system))
- Kevin Quiec shows component states documented for developers: molecules document states and scenarios so developers know how to implement them. ([Kevin Quiec](https://kevin-quiec.fr/project/merchsquare-designsystem))
- Etsy asks for "Results and the impact to the team & organization" and "How you measured the outcome". ([Etsy guide](https://extfiles.etsy.com/CareersPage/ProductDesign.pdf))

### Inferences
- Inference: given the use (capture now, write later), the artefacts that are only cheap to capture at the time are: the before state (audit screenshots, count of one-off styles), dated decision records with the rejected options, the token tier diagram as it stood at each change, the proposal or request that started each component, a usage or adoption count at a fixed point and again later, and the first time a component shipped in code. Nobody can reconstruct a before state afterwards.
- Inference: Donald's team set no KPIs at the start and still told the story to stakeholders. A small startup can do the same, but Etsy's "how you measured" means at least one baseline number should be written down before rollout.
- Inference: code parity (Donald's "if it doesn't at some point become code") is the artefact that separates a system from a Figma library. A screenshot of the same component in Figma and in production, or a Code Connect mapping, would show it.

### Gaps
- No opened source mentions token architecture diagrams, decision records (as a named format) or adoption numbers as things reviewers expect. The Smashing article does not name roadmaps, audits, contribution guidelines, decision logs or changelogs.
- Search snippets cited zeroheight 2025 report figures on token adoption and a W3C Design Tokens Format Module (v2025.10, 28 Oct 2025). Neither page was opened. Do not cite either until checked.

## 4. Showing leadership in a system versus component craft

### Takeaway
Thin evidence. The sources ask for role, collaborators and organisational impact (Etsy) and praise "showing the team he led" (Kennedy). Donald's case study shows leadership as running proposals in open sessions, a public plan board and a defined governance workflow. No opened source compares how much weight leadership should get against component craft for a lead.

### Cited findings
- Etsy: outline "who you partnered with to make things happen" and "Results and the impact to the team & organization". ([Etsy guide](https://extfiles.etsy.com/CareersPage/ProductDesign.pdf))
- Kennedy praises Gabriel Valdivia's case studies for "mentions of "0→1" + showing the team he led." GENERIC. ([Learn UI Design](https://www.learnui.design/blog/great-design-portfolio-examples.html))
- Donald: proposals discussed in open sessions; "our plan and breakdown on a board" kept visible for colleagues (section "Ownership and community"); a six step workflow map from a need emerging to a component reviewed in live use ("Once tested, it can be used"). ([Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/04/artifacts-design-systems/))
- Kevin Quiec, the only product designer at a startup, shows leadership as building with engineers: "The Design System was built with them and by listening to their needs." Team listed: himself, four developers, two business owners. ([Kevin Quiec](https://kevin-quiec.fr/project/merchsquare-designsystem))
- Mehri Russo frames the start as her own initiative: "Working at a startup with no design team, I saw a need for a design system." and "I was given two sprints to create the beginnings of a design system." ([Mehri Russo](https://www.mehrirusso.com/work/design-system))

### Inferences
- Inference: at a small startup the leadership story is less about running a federated contribution model and more about getting engineers and product to rely on the system: who asked for what, what was agreed, what changed in how the team ships. Capture the meetings, the agreements and the first time someone else used or contributed to the system.
- Inference: Russo's "I saw a need" and "I was given two sprints" is a clean pattern for showing initiative and the mandate obtained. A lead would add what the mandate was traded for.

### Gaps
- No source found on running workshops, adoption campaigns or governance as portfolio evidence for a lead or principal, and no source on how principal level expectations differ from lead.

## 5. Presenting work executed by teammates or AI agents you directed

### Takeaway
For teammates the ask is consistent: name your role and your partners (Etsy, NN/g). For AI, the 2026 sources (two generic, one vendor) agree: disclose where AI materially shaped what is being judged; put the note in the process section, not as a top disclaimer; and show your own framing, rejections, vetoes and corrections, with evidence such as decision records and before and after changes. One hiring reviewer (Scott) penalises AI work that "looks vibed".

### Cited findings
- Etsy: "Outline your role, impact in each case study and who you partnered with". ([Etsy guide](https://extfiles.etsy.com/CareersPage/ProductDesign.pdf))
- Learnist's test for disclosure: whether the tool "materially shaped the work the reader is evaluating". Usually disclose generated UI components, page structure, suggested architecture or scope and drafted case study copy. Usually skip spelling, formatting and small wording alternatives. Section "What level of use needs disclosure". ([Learnist](https://www.learnist.org/how-to-disclose-ai-tools-in-portfolio/))
- Learnist on placement: in a case study, put the note in the process section, not at the top as a disclaimer. Wording in three parts: the tool's role, your ownership and evidence the reader can check. Section "Format and placement" and "Recommended wording". ([Learnist](https://www.learnist.org/how-to-disclose-ai-tools-in-portfolio/))
- Learnist: "Never present AI-generated work as fully hand-built if that would mislead the reader." Evidence to strengthen the note: commits, tests, prompt logs, before and after changes, known limitations, decision records and notes on rejected suggestions. ([Learnist](https://www.learnist.org/how-to-disclose-ai-tools-in-portfolio/))
- Lovart: "Hiring managers are not asking whether you use AI. They assume you do." Disclose "process where it changes evaluation" because "Mystery finals create distrust." Case study shape: the brief, what AI proposed first, what was rejected and why, and whether fixes were local edits or regenerations. "Hiring managers hire the diagnostician." On metrics: claim only what you owned. Sections 1, 3, 5, 7 and FAQ. Vendor source with a commercial interest. ([Lovart](https://www.lovart.ai/blog/ai-design-portfolio-interview-2026))
- Lovart recommends a private "portfolio scrap" folder with "defect + fix" captions as source material for case studies. Field notes. ([Lovart](https://www.lovart.ai/blog/ai-design-portfolio-interview-2026))
- Bestfolios: "Show how you design for AI, documenting iterations, and decision-making makes your process visible and credible." The article's eight examples are mostly about designing AI products or AI chatbots on portfolios, not about disclosing AI executed work. Thin. ([Bestfolios](https://bestfolios.substack.com/p/how-designers-are-actually-using))
- Tom Scott rejects "only showing vibe-coded work that looks vibed". ([Verified Insider](https://verifiedinsider.substack.com/p/learnings-from-reviewing-500-design))

### Inferences
- Inference: for a Design Lead whose system pages were partly built by AI agents under his direction, the honest and persuasive framing is the one Lovart and Learnist converge on: he owned the brief, the rules the agents worked to, the checks and the vetoes; the agents executed. The evidence that makes this checkable is a log of what an agent produced, what he rejected and why, and what changed after. That log is cheap to keep now and impossible to rebuild later.
- Inference: "a system that makes agents build to standard" could itself be the leadership story (governance applied to non human contributors), but no source tested that framing with hiring managers.

### Gaps
- No source from a design hiring manager on AI agent executed design system work specifically. No source on presenting work by teammates who are engineers versus designers.
- Lovart's search snippet mentioned EU AI Act Article 50 and a Figma figure that 73% of hiring managers want AI tool proficiency. Neither appeared in the opened pages as verified fact, so neither is used.

## 6. Real, publicly viewable design system case studies by individual designers

### Takeaway
None of the opened sources holds up a named individual design system case study as good. Below are four I found and read, with what each does. All are self published. None shows adoption metrics, a governance model or code parity, which is itself a sign of how rare those are (inference).

### Cited findings
- Kevin Quiec, Merch Square design system (project 2019 to 2021, page updated Jul 2026). Five act story structure ("Setting the scene", "Rising action", "Climax", "Solving the problem", "(Re)solution"); names his role as "lead Product Designer" and "the only Product Designer in the startup"; lists the team (four developers, two business owners); accessibility as a stated pillar; component states documented for developers; outcome "Supporting 2 mobile apps and 1 web app"; candidly records that the company went bankrupt after two years. No audit, tokens, governance or metrics. Closest match to a small startup Design Lead. ([Kevin Quiec](https://kevin-quiec.fr/project/merchsquare-designsystem))
- Adham Dannaway, "My Figma design system" (Jul 2024). A commercial product, not a team system. Strong on decision rationale per foundation (token naming, transparent colours, contrast parity across modes, elevation in dark mode, state layers) and on testing by building example interfaces ("I also tested out my design system by creating lots of example interfaces. This helped me iron out the kinks."). No adoption, governance or team. ([Adham Dannaway](https://www.adhamdannaway.com/portfolio/figma-design-system))
- Mehri Russo, design system for Sunday (no date shown). Sections: introduction, where to start, research, audit, edit, evolve. Audit finding stated in text; role stated as "Created components and design tokens" and "Site audit"; mandate stated ("two sprints"). One image of screens, no metrics, no governance. ([Mehri Russo](https://www.mehrirusso.com/work/design-system))
- Stefan Vojnovic, token governance case study (2021 to 2023; author is a software engineer). Frames the problem as shared meaning ("A shared UI foundation only scales when teams agree on what the tokens mean") and states partial ownership ("I helped define and operationalize the token layer"). No artefacts or numbers shown. A counterexample: the right topic told with nothing to look at. ([Voynovich](https://www.voynovich.com/work/token-governance))

### Inferences
- Inference: combining what each does well gives a model: Quiec's story arc and honest ending, Dannaway's per decision rationale, Russo's audit opening and stated mandate, Vojnovic's governance framing, plus the artefacts and numbers none of them show.

### Gaps
- No reviewer endorsed example found. Leads not opened: a Storyblocks design system rebuild that begins with a UI inventory (seen in a search snippet, URL not given) and Kenneth W. Courtney, "Design system reimagined" (https://www.kennethwcourtney.com/case-studies/scalable-design-system/).
