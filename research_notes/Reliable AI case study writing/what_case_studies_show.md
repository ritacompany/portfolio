# What a product design case study is for: showing the work and thinking versus making an argument

Scope note: builds on `reports/What gets designers interviewed.md` and `reports/Case study results without metrics.md`. Those reports already cite Backes, Latin, Cox, NN/g, First Round (Facebook), Indeed, MentorCruise and Zhuo. This file adds and corrects; it does not repeat their findings except where a source needs re-reading.

Several fetches were blocked (HTTP 403): Intercom's 2017 portfolio post, Design Week's Ikea and Deliveroo piece, Somya Verma's "historical fiction" post, Alex Couch's micro case studies and a UX Planet red flags post. Anything cited from those comes from search snippets only and is marked SNIPPET.

## 1. What hiring managers and design leaders say a case study should show

### Takeaway
Named practitioners agree: a case study should show the designer's thinking through real decisions. That means trade-offs, constraints, what was cut or failed, who owned what and where people disagreed. A tidy finished story ranks below that. Julie Zhuo puts "polished little case studies" in the *okay* designer column.

### Cited Findings
- Julie Zhuo (former VP Design, Facebook) contrasts okay and excellent designers. Okay designers "recount projects that seem like polished little case studies." Excellent designers "tell you what they tried that didn't work," discuss "which tradeoffs shaped the final outcome" and say "what they versus other people owned and where they disagreed." She also writes: "Great work leaves 'residual pain', and the pain is the more interesting story." [Julie Zhuo, The Looking Glass](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer)
- Zhuo: okay designers "think in screens," excellent designers "think in systems, story, and worlds," and ask "Should this exist at all?" [Julie Zhuo](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer)
- NN/g survey of 204 hiring professionals (Rachel Krause, 2019): "Don't just show me the finished product. I want to see the messy process and all the work and research that was put in to land on that shiny polished design." Another respondent: "I'm curious to know what isn't in the design and why, just as much as I'd like to know why elements made it in." [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- NN/g respondents want "the problem you were trying to solve, your role, any constraints, project timeline" and "changes from iteration to iteration and how the research informed the design." [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- Emily Backes (senior product designer, former technical recruiter): "A designer who narrates events without ever taking a position is showing me a diary"; "A case study that reads like a diary of what happened" does not earn a second read. What shows judgment is "what they'd do differently, what they pushed back on." [Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)
- Lucas Rappart (product designer and portfolio reviewer, March 2026) lists senior portfolio mistakes. They include "No single decision is named," "The team section is missing or vague," "The work is too clean" and "There's no 'what I'd do differently'". He prescribes "Name three decisions per case study": the architectural one ("what we chose to design vs not design"), the trade-off one ("what we sacrificed") and the specific tension between teams. A senior portfolio is "three or four well-told stories." [Lucas Rappart](https://www.lucasrappart.com/blog/portfolio-mistakes-senior)
- Leslie Yang (hiring manager, former lead product designer) wants "the user problem, business goals, research, and why those led to the design decisions they made," plus "the role, responsibilities, who else was on the team, and the project duration." [Leslie Yang](https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/what-a-hiring-manager-looks-for-in)
- Jared Spool treats a portfolio as partial evidence. He has to talk to the person to understand it, and he wants the story of how the work happened. [UXfolio interview with Jared Spool](https://blog.uxfol.io/how-to-build-a-ux-portfolio/). His 2026 workshop copy says you "can no longer just describe a project" [Maven, Jared Spool](https://maven.com/p/62f27e/job-search-2026-what-makes-a-great-ux-portfolio). That page is course marketing.
- Tom Scott with Eugene Trofimov (former Apple design leadership), Dec 2025: "Start with the outcome. Lead each case study with a concrete result before you go into the details"; "Show your decision-making process, your niche expertise and, most importantly, the results you delivered." [Verified Insider](https://verifiedinsider.substack.com/p/the-playbook-to-finding-a-design)
- Shai (former Head of Design at an edtech company, 500+ portfolios reviewed, Nov 2024): "The story behind your work is what they're really interested in"; highlight "the thought process behind it." [Designfolio](https://designfolio.substack.com/p/portfolio-tips-ux)
- Intercom (2017, SNIPPET): the post criticises long, overly detailed process stories as much as visual polish without the business problem. It favours short entries covering what the product is, the problem and the designer's role. [Intercom blog](https://www.intercom.com/blog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/13339)

### Inferences
- Across sources, the measure is visible judgment. Process for its own sake fails (Intercom, Latin's "school homework"). A tidy story that hides the friction also fails (Zhuo, NN/g, Backes, Rappart's "too clean").
- "Show the work" in the senior designer's sense matches this consensus closely when it means considerations converging into named decisions. It fails when it means a chronological record. Backes' "diary" is that failure mode.

### Gaps
- No source found from Figma, Google or Meta recruiters in 2020 to 2026 stating case study criteria under their own name. The First Round Facebook piece is in the earlier report.
- Andy Budd, Dan Mall, Pablo Stanley and Ryan Scott Tandy: no primary statements on case study purpose found in this pass.

## 2. Is there support for the "argument / single takeaway" framing, and is there pushback?

### Takeaway
Real support for "argument" exists only at the level of the whole portfolio, where it means the hiring argument: this person can do this job. No named practitioner found here says a single case study should reduce to one thesis or "one thing they repeat." The nearest sources (Zhuo's "core message," Backes' "taking a position") turn out, read in full, to prize exactly the messy, multi-decision content a single takeaway compresses away.

### Cited Findings
- Support, portfolio level: Algonquin College Graphic Design faculty (undated, page footer 2026): the portfolio is "the most important artifact to support your hiring argument"; "Each piece will be evidence supporting that argument"; "The sequence is the case you build for why someone should hire you." The page has no sentence about one takeaway per piece. [Algonquin Design](https://algonquindesign.ca/articles/portfolio-prep-for-jr-designers.html)
- Support, portfolio level: Zhuo says strong portfolios have "a really strong core message or concept" and should "feel like a trailer"; "the first job is not completeness but rather intrigue." [Julie Zhuo](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer). Context matters here. These lines describe the portfolio. In the same article, case study content is judged on failures, trade-offs, ownership, disagreement and "residual pain."
- Support, framing: Spool's workshop copy calls a persuasive portfolio "a sales brochure for why you're an ideal fit rather than a museum of your career" (SNIPPET, course marketing). [Maven, Jared Spool](https://maven.com/p/cd1f0f/ux-job-search-what-hiring-managers-are-looking-for)
- Partial support: Backes asks the designer to take positions ("what they pushed back on"). That means owning stances on specific decisions, which is not the same as one overall thesis. [Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)
- Partial support: a UX Planet article frames a case study as an argument "built around the problem, the paths considered, the constraints, and the outcomes" (SNIPPET, author unverified). Here "argument" means reasoned evidence across many considerations. [UX Planet](https://uxplanet.org/ux-portfolio-using-craft-and-ai-to-rise-beyond-the-crowd-f2b49b280914)
- Partial support: Rappart: "The narrative is the show." The same piece demands three named decisions and visible tension. [Lucas Rappart](https://www.lucasrappart.com/blog/portfolio-mistakes-senior)
- Pushback, polish: Zhuo puts "polished little case studies" in the okay column, against excellent designers who show what didn't work. [Julie Zhuo](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer)
- Pushback, polish: NN/g hiring managers ask for "the messy process" and "what isn't in the design and why." [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- Pushback, format: Alex Couch (senior designer, 10+ years, SNIPPET) says most real work "doesn't look like the 'storybook' case studies" and calls the format "kinda contrived," then publishes micro case studies instead. [Alex Couch, Medium](https://medium.com/alex-couch-s-portfolio/9-micro-case-studies-9a9e8584af3d)
- Pushback, format: Somya Verma's post is titled "Your case study is historical fiction," arguing that tidy linear narratives misrepresent the work (SNIPPET only; content not verified). [Peerlist](https://peerlist.io/somyaverma/articles/your-case-study-is-historical-fiction)
- A counter-current that also cuts against a single takeaway: one 2026 guide says most case studies are written as narratives while hiring managers look for decision quality (options, constraints, trade-offs, outcomes) (SNIPPET, author unverified). [UX Planet](https://uxplanet.org/good-ux-portfolios-never-get-interviews-6ea28fb2f9b3)

### Inferences
- The protocol line "a case study is an argument, not a record" holds half of the evidence. Practitioners do reject the record (Backes' diary, Latin's homework, Intercom's long process stories). But the "argument" they accept is the hiring argument, made with evidence of judgment across several decisions. A thesis about the project is a different thing.
- "Pick the one thing they repeat" has no named practitioner support in this pass. It conflicts with Zhuo, NN/g, Rappart's three decisions and Backes, all of whom want several decisions, the rejected options and the friction to stay visible.
- Correction to the earlier report in this folder: `What gets designers interviewed.md` uses Zhuo's "core message" to support a one-sentence takeaway for a case study ending. Zhuo's line is about the portfolio as a trailer. Her case study criteria point the other way. A consistent standard is a clear hiring argument at portfolio level, with each case study as dense evidence of decisions. A case study can open with a clear frame (Scott: lead with the outcome; Rappart: lead with the problem) without reducing its body to one point.

### Gaps
- No primary source found that explicitly argues for "one repeatable takeaway per case study." If the protocol drew on one, it was not locatable.
- Tobias van Schneider reportedly argues "the formula for case studies has expired" and that you should focus less on process and more on what was actually done. That comes from a secondhand Italian newsletter summary; the original post was not found. [Designabile](https://designabile.substack.com/p/designabile-18-il-portfolio-del-designer?open=false)

## 3. Crediting collaborators, naming colleagues and quoting stakeholders

### Takeaway
Practitioners want ownership made legible: who else was on the team, what the designer owned versus others and where they disagreed. They ask for roles and team composition, not for colleagues' names or stakeholder quotes. No named source here recommends quoting stakeholders.

### Cited Findings
- Zhuo: excellent designers say "what they versus other people owned and where they disagreed"; vagueness is a warning sign: "If it feels like a dance, then they may not have a lot to say about the project and their involvement in it." [Julie Zhuo](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer)
- Backes: "A reviewer can't credit you for a decision they can't find." Her model phrasing is "I did X, as part of a team that also did Y and Z," instead of "the team did X." [Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)
- Yang: note "who else was on the team" alongside role and duration; show collaboration visually (for example, FigJam sessions with cross-functional partners). [Leslie Yang](https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/what-a-hiring-manager-looks-for-in)
- Rappart: "The team section is missing or vague" is a senior-level mistake. [Lucas Rappart](https://www.lucasrappart.com/blog/portfolio-mistakes-senior)
- Shai: the overview should name "the team involved" and the working arrangement. [Designfolio](https://designfolio.substack.com/p/portfolio-tips-ux)
- A van Schneider summary (secondhand) calls the designer's own share of a team project "an undervalued piece of information that is actually very important." [Designabile](https://designabile.substack.com/p/designabile-18-il-portfolio-del-designer?open=false)
- Generic advice (SNIPPET, unnamed design manager) calls "we" a red flag because "the interviewer is not looking to hire you and your previous team." [Prototypr](https://blog.prototypr.io/the-biggest-red-flag-of-design-portfolios-9a625a605674)

### Inferences
- The consensus asks for team by role ("a PM, two engineers") and ownership by decision. Naming colleagues adds little for a stranger reader and raises the ownership ambiguity reviewers screen for. That fits the repo's existing "no colleague names" rule.
- Disagreement is wanted (Zhuo) as evidence of the designer's judgment, framed around positions, not people.

### Gaps
- No source found on quoting stakeholders or users in case study copy, for or against.
- No source distinguishes crediting named colleagues from describing roles.

## 4. What makes a case study read as AI-written or generic (2024 to 2026)

### Takeaway
No survey measures recruiter reactions to AI-written case studies. Practitioner observation converges on symptoms rather than detection: identical structures and headings, fluent but generic sentences, actions without intent and missing decision logic and constraints. Reviewers penalise generic writing whether or not AI wrote it.

### Cited Findings
- A portfolio reviewer reports case study headings and sentence patterns converging across candidates (templates like "X problems, X solutions") and warns against letting AI "guess what happened" or supply the voice (SNIPPET). [Be Your Own Design Team](https://beyourowndesignteam.beehiiv.com/p/how-to-use-ai-when-building-a-design-portfolio)
- A hiring manager quoted in a 2025 to 2026 newsletter: "I just went through a hiring process and the AI-generated portfolios and case studies were everywhere... At first glance everything looked polished" (SNIPPET, name not verified). [Be Your Own Design Team, 2025 vs 2026 portfolio](https://beyourowndesignteam.beehiiv.com/p/2025-portfolio-vs-2026-portfolio-what-changed)
- UXfolio (vendor of an AI case study generator, so biased): generic AI tools "optimize for fluent paragraphs, not scannable logic" and produce case studies that "describe actions, but never explain intent or impact"; AI-generated text collapses into general statements under interview follow-up. [UXfolio](https://blog.uxfol.io/ux-portfolio-ai-case-study-workflow/)
- Muzli 2026 guide: a case study "too generic, too polished, or missing decision logic" can feel AI-generated even when it isn't (SNIPPET). [Muzli](https://muz.li/blog/how-to-build-a-ux-portfolio-that-actually-gets-you-hired-2026/)
- Fountain Institute (2026): generic AI case studies strip out judgment, constraint and trade-offs, "exactly what hiring managers look for" (SNIPPET). [The Fountain Institute](https://www.thefountaininstitute.com/blog/senior-product-designers-hired)
- Pre-AI baseline: Backes: "Sameness is what this reader is actually screening against, not skill," and "the fortieth portfolio built from the same three-act structure." [Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)
- Statistics like "78% of recruiters use AI screening" circulate across vendor posts with no traceable origin. Do not use them. [Muzli](https://muz.li/blog/how-to-build-a-ux-portfolio-that-actually-gets-you-hired-2026/)

### Inferences
- The AI tell and the "single takeaway" protocol may be linked. Compressing a project to one repeatable point and a clean arc produces the polished, templated, friction-free shape reviewers already flag as generic. Specific constraints, rejected options and owned disagreements are the hardest content for AI to invent and the most common tell when it is absent.

### Gaps
- No named Figma, Google or Meta recruiter statement on AI-written case studies found.
- No data on detection rates or rejection rates for AI-written portfolios.

## 5. Showing systems or framework work rather than a single screen

### Takeaway
Evidence is thin and mostly vendor or anonymous. What exists says to show the problem the system solved, how it changed how teams work (adoption, governance) and what was deliberately left out. Showing components alone falls short. Zhuo's "think in systems" is the strongest named signal that systems thinking is valued.

### Cited Findings
- Zhuo: excellent designers "think in systems, story, and worlds," not screens. [Julie Zhuo](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer)
- Anonymous hiring manager on Team Blind: wants to see how you worked with feature teams, drove adoption and created operational efficiencies at scale, proving more than "a library in Figma" (SNIPPET). [Team Blind](https://www.teamblind.com/post/how-to-present-design-systems-work-in-portfolio-qmqcm81k)
- Portfoliobox (vendor): go macro to micro. Open on the real product, show the chaos before, then foundations; reviewers won't care about border-radius logic without knowing what the application does (SNIPPET). [Portfoliobox](https://www.portfoliobox.com/magazine/how-to-present-a-design-system-portfolio-without)
- Rappart's architectural decision ("what we chose to design vs not design") and a related post on systems work covering "tokens, components, governance, and the long list of what to skip in v1." [Lucas Rappart](https://www.lucasrappart.com/blog/portfolio-mistakes-senior)
- NN/g: "what isn't in the design and why" applies directly to framework scope decisions. [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)

### Inferences
- Framework work fits the "many considerations converge into decisions" model better than a single takeaway does. Its value sits in scope boundaries, sequencing and how it changed others' work, which are several decisions by nature.

### Gaps
- No named source found on presenting non-visual framework or strategy work (scheduling models, product AI strategy) in a case study. Guidance is almost entirely about component design systems.
