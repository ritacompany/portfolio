# How individual product designer case studies end

Research date: 2026-09-30. Portfolio endings were pulled from the live pages (raw HTML, text only) on that date. Source for the portfolio list: portfolios linked from Learn UI Design's "10 great UX portfolio examples" roundup (Erik Kennedy) and Job Roadmaps' "Best Product Designer Portfolios 2026". Seniority is noted only where the page states it. Several roundup picks are mid-level or early career, not senior or staff; that is flagged per entry.

## Key question 1: Catalogue of real case study endings

### Takeaway
Across 12 live case studies, no two ended the same way. Only 4 of 12 had a titled reflection section. The more common pattern among experienced designers was a factual one or two line closing (launched, released, next step) followed by an onward module (next project, other projects, contact). Concept and self-initiated projects were the ones most likely to carry an explicit reflection or "what's next" block.

### Cited Findings

**Bettina Bergendahl, Malu (self-initiated meal planning app, concept not yet built)**
- Ending section titled "Lessons Learned / Next Steps", with an h2 sub-point "Focusing on the 'why'". Content: first self-initiated project, being sole decision maker, resisting going beyond MVP ("a million other ideas that weren't pursued"). Roughly 120 to 150 words of reflection. : [bettinabergendahl.com/work/malu](https://www.bettinabergendahl.com/work/malu-recipe-meal-planning-app)
- Then a one line next step: next is finding a developer, "I'll update this project when that happens!" Then an h2 "Have thoughts about this project?" with "Send me a message, I'd love to talk." Then Previous / Next project links with full project titles. : [same](https://www.bettinabergendahl.com/work/malu-recipe-meal-planning-app)
- Layout: reflection, one line next step, conversational contact prompt, prev/next nav. Four beats, short.

**Bettina Bergendahl, Little Otter Health (shipped, 2022, first design hire)**
- No reflection section. The page ends with a one line link out: "Want to see how it ended up? See the mobile app here and more about Little Otter Health here!", then Role and Team metadata, then Next project. The ending hands the reader to the live product instead of describing results. : [bettinabergendahl.com/work/little-otter-health](https://www.bettinabergendahl.com/work/little-otter-health-a-pediatric-mental-health-platform)

**Gabriel Valdivia, Daylight (shipped product work; Valdivia is a long-time design lead, ex Facebook and Dropbox per his About page, not re-verified here)**
- No reflection, outcome or next steps. The last content section is an h3 "Design System" paragraph plus image grid, then the site footer. The case study ends on the work itself, not a wrap-up. : [gabrielvaldivia.com/work/daylight](https://www.gabrielvaldivia.com/work/daylight)

**Karolis Kosas, MUBI (shipped)**
- The page is a short project summary. "Results" appears near the top as three bullets ("Increase in paid subscribers and visitors", "Increase in app downloads and mobile subscribers", new apps for Android, Amazon Fire and PlayStation), then images, then "Back / Up". No closing section at all. Results are front-loaded, not an ending. : [karoliskosas.com/mubi-5](https://karoliskosas.com/mubi-5/)

**Anh Nguyen, Moonai (shipped Jan 2022, small startup)**
- A short outcome paragraph ("Moonai launched on the app store in January 2022", top 12 finalist in She Loves Tech 2021, accepted into FemTech Lab), then h5 "Learnings + takeaways" with bolded sub-heads "Global cross-functional communication" and "Flexibility in product strategy and project management", each a 60 to 100 word paragraph. Personal tone ("please don't quiz me! :)"). : [anguyen.design/moonai](https://www.anguyen.design/moonai)

**Steph Parrott, Plantd (self-initiated side project, beta, early 2020; Parrott's portfolio lists Square, Box, Microsoft)**
- h2 "What's Next?" Content: beta launched early March, paused by COVID, bulleted next steps ("Beta feedback", "Growing the community") including a concrete early observation (beta users adding food with eggs or cheese, showing the definition of plant-based varies). Ends "feel free to sign up for beta access!" Then "More Featured Projects" as large h1 cards with year ranges, then "Get in touch". About 150 words in the ending. : [stephparrott.com/plantd](https://www.stephparrott.com/plantd/)

**Steph Parrott, Box Annotations (shipped, 2015 to 2017)**
- No titled ending. The last paragraph is one factual sentence: annotations were released through the Box View API and the web app team is incorporating them. Then "More Featured Projects". Same author as above: the shipped study ends on a fact, the side project ends on "What's Next?". : [stephparrott.com/box](https://www.stephparrott.com/box/)

**Yingrui Chen, Travmates (hypothetical solo UX/UI project, early career)**
- Section marker "/reflection" with h1 "What I would do differently next time." Three sub-points: "Testing the MVP in a real-world scenario", "Cross-functional Collaboration", "Measuring impact with validated metrics" (states plainly "Although this is a hypothetical project"). Then "see also" with three project cards and a large repeated h1 "Let's make great products / get in contact". : [yingruichen.com/travmates](https://www.yingruichen.com/travmates)

**Rachel Chen, PokerGPT (shipped MVP, early career)**
- Label "Reflection" with h2 "What I learned" and two h3 lessons written as imperatives or claims: "Keep cutting it down to the MLP." and "User research is not enough." Each is one or two sentences. Then footer. Headline-style lessons, very short. : [rachelchen.tech/projects/pokergpt](https://www.rachelchen.tech/projects/pokergpt)

**Rachel Chen, Mobile-first for Figma (6 day design challenge, Oct 2025, concept)**
- Label "Outcome" with h2 "We had the chance to share our work with incredible designers in NYC!" and one line: they were voted winner and got feedback. No reflection. The external validation stands in for metrics on a concept. : [rachelchen.tech/projects/figma](https://www.rachelchen.tech/projects/figma)

**Renee Chen, Optoma Management Suite (early career)**
- Ends on "Style Guide" images then h1 "More to explore..." with back to top / work / about. No outcome or reflection. : [renee-chen.webflow.io/optoma-management-suite](https://renee-chen.webflow.io/optoma-management-suite)

**Ellen Covey, Komoot x Garmin (password protected)**
- Public page ends with "Access the full story" and a public vs full version toggle. The ending could not be read. : [ellencovey.com/komoot-x-garmin-password.html](https://ellencovey.com/komoot-x-garmin-password.html)

### Inferences
- Titles observed: "Lessons Learned / Next Steps", "What's Next?", "Learnings + takeaways", "Reflection / What I learned", "/reflection: What I would do differently next time", "Outcome". Designers with the longest shipped track records (Valdivia, Parrott on Box, Kosas, Bergendahl on Little Otter) mostly had no titled ending at all.
- For unshipped or self-initiated work, the strongest endings in this set (Parrott's Plantd, Bergendahl's Malu) did three things in under 200 words: stated the real status of the project, named a concrete next step and gave one specific observation or lesson rather than a general one. Parrott's egg and cheese observation is the kind of specific detail reviewers ask for.
- Rachel Chen's PokerGPT ending shows lessons written as opinionated headlines work as a compact format: the heading is the lesson, the body is one sentence.
- Nearly every page ends with an onward module (next project, more projects, contact). This matches Erik Kennedy's advice (Key question 3).

### Gaps
- Type size of the ending headings and body could not be recorded. Pages were read as text; most are Framer, Webflow or Squarespace with generated CSS classes. Heading levels are recorded instead (h1 to h6) as a rough proxy.
- Simon Pan's Uber case study (simonpan.com, the most cited "senior" example) returned HTTP 401 on 2026-09-30. It appears to be restricted now. Anh Nguyen's NYT Games and Allbirds studies also returned 401. Emmi Wu's voter guide (Framer) rendered only decorative ASCII art in raw HTML.
- Only a few entries are verifiably senior or staff. The roundups mostly feature mid-level or early-career designers. No staff-level individual portfolio with a readable concept case study ending was found. Bestfolios, Awwwards and Read.cv were not sampled (JS-rendered or not reached within the tool budget).
- Publication dates of individual case studies were mostly not stated on the pages.

## Key question 2: Which ending titles are common and which read as strong or filler

### Takeaway
"Reflection", "Learnings/Lessons learned", "What's next" and "Outcome/Results" are the standard set. Reviewers do not object to the titles; they object to generic content under them. Advice has shifted toward a specific frame: "what I'd do differently now" or "what changed in my thinking".

### Cited Findings
- IxDF's conclusion guide names three required components: final product, results and impact, reflections and lessons. Reflection should cover how you overcame challenges, learnings from mistakes, how your perspective changed and next steps for iteration. Yu Siang Teo, 2019, updated 2026. : [IxDF](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/how-to-write-the-perfect-conclusion-to-your-ux-case-study)
- The Crit advises "End with what changed in your thinking" rather than generic lessons, naming "what you would preserve, revisit, or investigate next." Nikki Kipple, updated September 2026. : [The Crit](https://thecrit.co/resources/complete-portfolio-guide)
- ADPList newsletter recommends: "Conclude each project with a short 'What I'd do differently now' section," which "signals humility, critical thinking, and a mindset of continuous improvement." Author and date not shown in fetched text. : [ADPList Substack](https://adplist.substack.com/p/the-best-portfolio-playbook-thats)
- Florian Bölter (Oct 15, 2025): hiring managers "spot generic phrasing instantly" and "Don't just copy the Research / Define / Ideate / Prototype template. That reads like a checklist." He argues section titles should tell a story rather than follow a template. : [Open Doors Careers](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-recruiters-and-hiring-managers-actually-look-at-your-portfolio)

### Inferences
- The filler risk is the template title plus generic content ("I learned the importance of user research"). The strong version either states the lesson as the heading (Rachel Chen) or ties it to a specific observation (Parrott). Story-telling headings per Bölter would argue for a heading that says the lesson, not the word "Reflection".
- "Outcome" is only strong when there is something to put under it. On a concept, an "Outcome" heading with nothing measurable invites the reader to notice the absence.

### Gaps
- No source found that ranks titles by reviewer preference or counts title frequency across a large sample.

## Key question 3: What hiring managers and reviewers say about the last section, memory and length

### Takeaway
The best-sourced claim is that the ending carries extra weight in memory (serial position / recency). Length advice is short: IxDF says 4 to 5 sentences. Erik Kennedy says the bottom of a case study should carry two calls to action, not a retrospective.

### Cited Findings
- IxDF: people remember beginnings and endings best (serial position effect, Ebbinghaus), so the conclusion matters for recall. Ideal length 4 to 5 sentences, stretching to 3 or 4 paragraphs only if results or reflections are substantial; roughly match the intro. : [IxDF, Teo](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/how-to-write-the-perfect-conclusion-to-your-ux-case-study)
- Erik D. Kennedy (Mar 29, 2024): "At the bottom of a portfolio case study, I believe there are two calls-to-action you should include: 1. Read another case study (if someone's not yet convinced) 2. Hire/contact me (if someone is convinced)." He lists "too much text" as one of four common mistakes. : [Learn UI Design](https://www.learnui.design/blog/great-ux-ui-portfolio-case-study.html)
- Bölter: hiring managers skim and judge clarity and story quickly. : [Open Doors Careers](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-recruiters-and-hiring-managers-actually-look-at-your-portfolio)
- IxDF quotes Anett Illés (UXfolio) on showing learning appetite: recruiters look for problem solvers motivated to learn. (Wording is IxDF's paraphrase.) : [IxDF](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/how-to-write-the-perfect-conclusion-to-your-ux-case-study)

### Inferences
- Taken together, the advice points to an ending with two parts: a short substantive close (status plus one sharp lesson or next step, about 4 to 5 sentences) and an onward link. The live portfolios above match this more often than they match the long "Reflection" template.
- The serial position claim is general memory research applied to portfolios by IxDF, not a study of hiring managers. Treat it as a reasonable argument, not evidence about reviewers.

### Gaps
- No named senior or staff hiring manager quote specifically about the final section (as opposed to results in general) was found and verified. The Medium piece "What I Look for When Reviewing a UX Case Study as a Hiring Manager" (design-bootcamp) returned 403. NN/g's portfolio article URL tried returned 404. Podcast transcripts were not reached.

## Key question 4: How strong concept or unshipped case studies end without metrics

### Takeaway
Reviewers accept no metrics if the ending is honest about status and gives the strongest real evidence available: a named hypothesis and how it would be measured, external validation, beta observations or a concrete next step.

### Cited Findings
- The Crit: separate "measured results, observed behavior, approved direction, shipped work, and what remains untested". Without outcome data, use "the strongest honest evidence available: approval, launch, adoption, fewer support questions, design-system use, stakeholder learning, or a clearly named hypothesis." : [The Crit](https://thecrit.co/resources/complete-portfolio-guide)
- Bölter: "Show results, or if you don't have data, describe what success would look like and how you'd measure it." : [Open Doors Careers](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-recruiters-and-hiring-managers-actually-look-at-your-portfolio)
- Real examples: Parrott's Plantd ends with real status, a paused beta and a specific early observation. Bergendahl's Malu ends with an honest status (next is finding a developer). Chen's Travmates labels itself hypothetical and names validated metrics as the thing it lacks. Rachel Chen's Figma concept uses an event win as outcome. : [Plantd](https://www.stephparrott.com/plantd/); [Malu](https://www.bettinabergendahl.com/work/malu-recipe-meal-planning-app); [Travmates](https://www.yingruichen.com/travmates); [Figma concept](https://www.rachelchen.tech/projects/figma)

### Inferences
- For a senior designer the Travmates pattern (listing what you did not do) is weaker than Plantd's (reporting what you saw and what happens next). Stating a hypothesis and its measure reads as judgement; listing missing steps reads as apology.
- No observed concept ending tried to fake an Outcome section with invented numbers.

### Gaps
- No example found of a verifiably senior or staff designer ending a bootcamp or concept case study. Senior portfolios in this sample mostly show shipped work.
