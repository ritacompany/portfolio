# How concept, bootcamp and self-initiated case studies stand out without launch or metrics

Research date: 2026-09-30. Access note: Medium, UX Collective (bootcamp.uxdesign.cc) and UX Planet returned HTTP 403 to the fetch tool and proxy mirrors were unreachable, so several hiring manager posts on those platforms are cited only from search snippets and are flagged as such. Commercial bias is flagged per source.

## 1. What differentiates a strong concept case study from the typical one, and what gets skimmed

### Takeaway
The typical bootcamp case study is skimmed because it follows the same linear template (interviews, personas, sketches, mockups, test) with a clean "perfect" solution, and often reuses the same prompts. The strong one shows the messy middle: why one option won over another, what was left out, what failed and how the designer adapted.

### Cited Findings
- Matej Latin (self-taught designer, says he has reviewed 1,000+ portfolios, post dated 2024-03-06) estimates about 90% of the portfolios he reviews are rejected for presenting a linear design process; he estimates 70 to 80% of designers simplify case studies into a linear structure. Named failure modes: cookie-cutter sequence "problem, interviews, personas, sketches, mockups, visual design, prototype, testing", "perfect" solutions that lack credibility, and omission of setbacks and adaptation. His fix: tell it with messy details, unexpected challenges and what was learned. These percentages are his own estimates, not a survey: [Matej Latin, "90% of designers are unhirable?"](https://matejlatin.com/blog/90-percent-of-designers-are-unhirable/)
- NN/g survey of 204 UX hiring professionals (Rachel Krause, 2019-08-04): case studies should show the problem, the candidate's role, how research led to the solution, outcomes, design concepts that did not work and why, and lessons learned. A quoted hiring manager wants to know "what isn't in the design and why". For student work, the article advises adding realistic business constraints and openly acknowledging unrealistic elements: [NN/g, 5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- A hiring manager post on Design Bootcamp (Medium, 2023, "Portfolio review checklist. 2023. Part 2") says Google/Coursera bootcamp graduates almost always include personas without saying where they came from or how they were used afterwards; everything in the case study must be there for a reason (search snippet only, page returned 403): [Portfolio review checklist 2023 Part 2](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/portfolio-review-checklist-2023-part-2-4e63c96f8478)
- A UX/UI design director writing on Medium says bootcamp students often use the same projects and copy, and claim to be sole designer on team projects, which is "easily discoverable" and disqualifying; many portfolios share near-identical layouts, templates and work examples (search snippet only, 403): [Some advice and thoughts about UX bootcamps and portfolios from a UX/UI & Design Director](https://medium.com/@allllex/some-advice-and-thoughts-about-ux-bootcamps-and-portfolios-from-a-ux-ui-design-director-206d6fe46446)
- A similar snippet describes bootcamp portfolios as "very polished but also formulaic", with hiring managers seeing the same thing from multiple candidates, which "raises doubts about depth of understanding" (search snippet only): [UX portfolio: What hiring managers look for, Design Bootcamp](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/ux-portfolios-what-hiring-managers-look-for-b7c5285133fc)
- A hiring manager on Design Bootcamp says reviewers scan section titles and images for the problem, the path from problem to solution, design decisions, how the candidate worked with constraints and how they knew they were on the right track (search snippet only): [Design portfolio evaluation process as a hiring manager](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/my-portfolio-evaluation-process-342005398262)
- A Design Bootcamp hiring manager article warns against listing methods never actually used ("Competitive Audit", "User Testing") just to sound impressive (search snippet only): [What I Look for When Reviewing a UX Case Study as a Hiring Manager](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/what-i-look-for-when-reviewing-a-ux-case-study-as-a-hiring-managerhow-i-review-ux-case-studies-as-a-994f1537c2e8)
- Review time is short. Korin Harris (Figma design recruiter, Dribbble, updated 2024-03-11) says design managers and heads of design spend "minutes or seconds" on a portfolio and look for problem to solution to iterations to role to lessons learned: [Dribbble, What design recruiters look for](https://dribbble.com/resources/career/design-recruiter-portfolio-tips). UX Playbook claims an average of 60 seconds per case study, with no method given (commercial source, see below): [UX Playbook](https://uxplaybook.org/articles/how-to-showcase-impact-in-ux-case-studies-without-clear-metrics)
- Indeed Design (April 2020) interviewed six Indeed UX leaders. Corey Chandler (UX Design Manager) ranks shipped work above concepts because shipped products face real challenges concept work avoids; Susan Le (UX Director) looks for depth and breadth with clear problem statements; Matthew Nesbitt asks "what's your superpower?": [Indeed Design, UX design portfolio advice from hiring managers](https://indeed.design/article/ux-design-portfolio-advice-from-hiring-managers/)

### Inferences
- The skim trigger is recognisability: a template sequence, stock artefacts (personas, journey maps) with no downstream use, and a familiar prompt. A concept case study earns attention by breaking the template where the thinking actually happened.
- "What is not in the design and why" is the most portable differentiator for concept work because it needs no launch and no metrics, only honest reasoning.

### Gaps
- No systematic survey isolates concept-only portfolios versus shipped-work portfolios. The 90% figure is one reviewer's estimate.
- Several strongest hiring manager posts sit on Medium and could only be read via snippet.

## 2. How a concept project should frame itself

### Takeaway
Consensus across sources: label the status up front, name the constraints you imposed or inherited and treat them as the story, go deep on a narrow problem, and pick real, unsolved problems over redesigns of famous products.

### Cited Findings
- Honest status up front is treated as a credibility signal, not a penalty. A Design Bootcamp hiring manager: transparency about conceptual status shows integrity and self-awareness, valued even above a polished process (search snippet only): [What I Look for When Reviewing a UX Case Study as a Hiring Manager](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/what-i-look-for-when-reviewing-a-ux-case-study-as-a-hiring-managerhow-i-review-ux-case-studies-as-a-994f1537c2e8)
- NN/g: build realistic business constraints into student projects and acknowledge anything unrealistic so employers can tell it apart from real-world work: [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- Tobias van Schneider argues unsolicited redesigns lack real-world constraints and so do not let reviewers assess product design skill; he suggests choosing struggling, lesser-known products with real problems over big brands (search summary of his DESK Magazine writing; direct page returned 404): [House of van Schneider, portfolio tips](https://vanschneider.com/blog/portfolio-tips/)
- Depth over breadth: pick one friction point in an existing app and tell a before and after story rather than redesigning a whole product (search snippet, low authority training-institute blog): [animationcoursesahmedabad.com](https://www.animationcoursesahmedabad.com/blog/ahmedabad/graphic-web-ui-ux-multimedia-design/ux-design-portfolios-tips-that-got-students-hired-after-training/514/details). UXfolio (commercial portfolio builder, Akos Izsak, 2024-04-22) recommends only 2 to 3 of the best projects for juniors: [UXfolio junior guide](https://blog.uxfol.io/junior-ux-designer/)
- Speculative work "requires careful framing": say it is speculative, explain assumptions and show how decisions would apply in a real context (search snippet from UX Playbook / Chris Nguyen, commercial): [UX Playbook 2025 guide](https://uxchrisnguyen.medium.com/how-to-write-ux-case-studies-that-land-you-job-in-2025-00549454b39e)
- A personal origin can supply a sharp problem insight. Ellen Covey's self-initiated "The Beakery" begins with her hatching quail in 2020 and finding no feed matched their needs, then interviews four bird owners to test whether the need was shared. DesignerUp (a paid course provider) praises it for a clear problem statement tailored to a niche audience: [Ellen Covey, The Beakery](https://ellencovey.com/the-beakery); [DesignerUp, 10 exceptional portfolios](https://designerup.co/blog/10-exceptional-product-design-portfolios-with-case-study-breakdowns/)
- Sarah Doody (career coach, commercial) advises answering the "so what" behind each decision and showing evidence that you can do the job described, with "less personality" (search snippet of her podcast pages): [Career Strategy Podcast](https://www.careerstrategylab.com/podcasts/)

### Inferences
- For a concept project in a sensitive domain, "honest status plus constraints as the story" matters more, not less: reviewers will ask what the designer would not do without clinical, legal or community input, and naming those limits is the evidence of judgment.
- A narrow, specific problem with a personal or observed origin beats a broad "app for X" premise because it produces decisions a reviewer can evaluate.

### Gaps
- No hiring manager source found that specifically addresses framing concept work in high stakes or sensitive domains (mental health, LGBTQIA+, youth, safety). This is inference only.

## 3. Which parts carry the most weight for concept work

### Takeaway
With no outcome data, weight shifts to the decision layer: problem framing, the options considered and rejected, what testing changed, and constraint handling. Visual craft is a baseline filter, not the differentiator at junior level.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g's list explicitly includes "design concepts that didn't work and why" and lessons learned, both available to concept work; when metrics are missing it points to qualitative impact: [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- UXfolio (commercial) says a scrappy mid-fidelity prototype with explained decisions is worth more than pixel-perfect screens alone, because at junior level "the thinking is the differentiator" (search snippet): [UXfolio junior guide](https://blog.uxfol.io/junior-ux-designer/)
- Testing that reverses or changes a design is the substitute outcome. UX Playbook (commercial) names usability test results as the prime non-metric evidence, e.g. task completion in half the time: [UX Playbook, impact without metrics (2025-01-20)](https://uxplaybook.org/articles/how-to-showcase-impact-in-ux-case-studies-without-clear-metrics). The Beakery shows the pattern: first study found users could not find "add group" buttons, she enlarged and repositioned every "add new" button, and a second round of 14 participants all found it: [Ellen Covey, The Beakery](https://ellencovey.com/the-beakery)
- Craft is a gate: Annie Jarvis (Indeed Senior UX Director) says her first question is whether the portfolio looks modern: [Indeed Design](https://indeed.design/article/ux-design-portfolio-advice-from-hiring-managers/). Korin Harris (Figma recruiter) says spelling errors signal poor attention to detail and can derail strong candidates: [Dribbble](https://dribbble.com/resources/career/design-recruiter-portfolio-tips)
- Point of view counts: DesignerUp praises Tom Petty's portfolio for knowing "what it wasn't, as well as what it was": [DesignerUp](https://designerup.co/blog/10-exceptional-product-design-portfolios-with-case-study-breakdowns/)

### Inferences
- Rank for concept work: (1) a sharp problem insight, (2) one pivotal decision with rejected alternatives, ideally changed by testing, (3) constraint and ethics handling, (4) craft as a threshold. Ethics carries weight mainly when the domain makes it the core constraint.

### Gaps
- No source ranks these elements quantitatively for concept work specifically. A snippet cites a figure that 54.5% of respondents rate research process as most important, but that is from a UX research portfolio context (User Interviews) and was not verified: [User Interviews](https://www.userinterviews.com/blog/ux-research-portfolio-examples-templates)

## 4. Examples of concept or bootcamp case studies that helped designers get hired

### Takeaway
Verifiable, hiring-manager-praised concept case studies are scarce. Most "got me hired" examples are self-reported and many showcases come from course sellers praising their own graduates.

### Cited Findings
- Ellen Covey, "The Beakery" (self-initiated, iOS concept). What stood out: personal origin of the problem, a niche audience, progressive disclosure decision, a two-round usability test that changed the design. Praised by DesignerUp (commercial; no claim that this project got her hired): [ellencovey.com/the-beakery](https://ellencovey.com/the-beakery)
- Madeline Wukusick (DesignerUp graduate, career changer from graphic and data design) and Rohit Singh (engineer to product designer, self-initiated "Booksite") are showcased by DesignerUp; Madeline is described as landing roles quickly. Commercial bias: both are graduates of the publisher's own course: [DesignerUp](https://designerup.co/blog/10-exceptional-product-design-portfolios-with-case-study-breakdowns/); [madelinewukusick.com](https://madelinewukusick.com); [khadush.in](https://khadush.in)
- A designer reports landing a first product design job in 2020 with zero referrals and only concept case studies, crediting creativity and structure over realness (self-report, Medium snippet): [How I built a UX portfolio with no experience (and got a job)](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/how-i-built-a-ux-portfolio-with-no-experience-and-got-a-job-5ea7c22b4b2d)
- Sensitive-population concept case studies exist in quantity (e.g. a queer workplace community concept with interviews of queer job seekers and a company "queer-friendliness" review feature: [Leeanne Panda](https://leeannepanda.medium.com/cultivating-a-creative-safe-space-for-the-queer-community-through-technology-a-ux-design-case-fe596261611f); mental health bootcamp projects such as Sage from Springboard: [bootcamp.uxdesign.cc](https://bootcamp.uxdesign.cc/case-study-designing-an-application-to-foster-better-mental-health-assistance-7440b060a3ae)). None found carries a hiring manager's public praise or a documented hire.

### Inferences
- Mental health app concepts are among the most common bootcamp topics, so the domain alone is not a differentiator and may trigger the "seen it" skim. What would differentiate is handling the stakes: crisis pathways, what the product refuses to do, and consent.

### Gaps
- No verifiable hiring manager praise found for a specific sensitive-population concept case study (mental health, LGBTQIA+, youth, safety).
- Bestfolios, ADPList recordings and portfolio review videos were not reached within the call budget.

## 5. How the end should leave the reader

### Takeaway
Hiring guidance converges on ending with reflection that proves judgment: what the designer learned, what did not work, and what they would test or change next if it were real. Qualitative evidence from testing stands in for results.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g lists lessons learned and failed concepts among essential components: [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- Korin Harris ends her recommended project story on "lessons learned": [Dribbble](https://dribbble.com/resources/career/design-recruiter-portfolio-tips)
- Without formal metrics, reflections on what you would do differently demonstrate critical thinking and growth (search snippet, commercial UX Playbook): [UX Playbook 2025](https://uxchrisnguyen.medium.com/how-to-write-ux-case-studies-that-land-you-job-in-2025-00549454b39e)
- The Beakery ends on test evidence: nearly all participants created and ordered a mix, summarised as users "completing tasks with ease" and "converting": [Ellen Covey](https://ellencovey.com/the-beakery)
- Latin's STAR framing puts a Result last but insists the Action section carry adaptations and learning: [Matej Latin](https://matejlatin.com/blog/90-percent-of-designers-are-unhirable/)

### Inferences
- Strongest last impression for concept work: a specific, honest statement of what is proven (test evidence), what is not (what launch would have to validate) and the next decision the designer would make. This converts "no metrics" from an apology into a demonstration of product judgment.

### Gaps
- No source empirically compares ending types. Recommendations are practitioner opinion.

## Source bias summary
- Commercial: UX Playbook (sells courses and critiques), DesignerUp (showcases own graduates), UXfolio (portfolio builder), Sarah Doody / Career Strategy Lab (coaching), Dribbble (platform, though author is a Figma recruiter).
- Independent or research based: NN/g (survey of 204 hiring professionals, 2019), Indeed Design (in-house leaders, 2020), Matej Latin (individual reviewer, 2024; also sells courses but the post is editorial).
