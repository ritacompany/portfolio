# What makes a product design hiring manager or recruiter decide to interview after a case study

Research date: 2026-09-30. Quote verification note: quotes below were extracted through a fetch tool that summarizes pages. Treat every quote as "verify against source before publishing." Quotes marked (UNVERIFIED) came only from search snippets or aggregators and could not be traced to a primary page. Three primary pages were blocked (HTTP 403 or 404): Jared Spool's Medium article, the UX Collective version of Matej Latin's "30 seconds" post, and Tanner Christensen's interview process post.

## 1. How much time reviewers spend per case study and where they look

### Takeaway
No rigorous published survey measures time per case study. Named practitioners converge on a two stage pattern: a scan of about 10 to 30 seconds that decides whether a case study gets read, then 2 to 3 minutes on one or two case studies if it earns it. The first screen of a case study (title, hook, problem, role, outcome) carries the decision.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g surveyed 204 UX professionals in charge of hiring (Rachel Krause, Aug 4 2019). The article gives no time-per-portfolio figure; it says hiring managers "don't have a lot of time to dedicate to each candidate's portfolio" and recommends 3 to 5 detailed case studies. Source: [NN/g, 5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/). The underlying survey is part of NN/g's UX Careers report: [User Experience Careers, 2nd ed. PDF](https://media.nngroup.com/media/reports/free/UserExperienceCareers_2nd_Edition.pdf)
- Julie Zhuo (April 14 2026) describes hiring managers "rummaging through a lot of portfolios in a single block of time," scanning "40 or 50 or maybe 100 URLs" in an hour. Source: [Julie Zhuo, How to Spot a World-Class Designer](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer)
- Mollie Cox, hiring manager (LinkedIn, roughly early 2024): "I have about 2-3 minutes per portfolio. Definitely more if you engage me." Her order: homepage and nav, the site's own design quality, About page, first case study (headline, then "product, problem, outcome"), process (concepts, sketches, iterations), then "skim again and look for numbers," then a second case study for consistency, then the resume. Source: [Mollie Cox on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/molliecox_hiring-manager-pov-my-portfolio-review-activity-7158534013253545984-Pgmm)
- Emily Backes, senior product designer and former technical recruiter (undated): 10 seconds on the homepage, 2 to 3 minutes per case study if it advances. "The fastest way to lose me is still a homepage that makes me guess what you do." Source: [Emily Backes, What Recruiters Actually Look At](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)
- Matej Latin, senior product designer at a Big Tech company receiving "a couple of hundred applications per week" (March 14 2025): most portfolios are rejected in about 30 seconds. Basis is personal experience, not a survey. What gets looked at in that window: image quality, writing and grammar, case study titles and opening hooks, context, the designer's own contribution. Source: [Matej Latin, Only 30 seconds to reject your portfolio?](https://adplist.substack.com/p/only-30-seconds-to-reject-your-portfolio)
- Tom Scott, design recruiter (Feb 19 2026): "Interviewers are time-poor and pattern-matching quickly"; reviewers look at outcomes before details. Source: [Tom Scott, FAQ: Design Hiring in 2026](https://verifiedinsider.substack.com/p/faq-design-hiring-in-2026)
- Figures that circulate but could not be traced to a primary source (UNVERIFIED): "6 to 8 seconds initial scan" ([UX University newsletter](https://newsletter.uxuniversity.io/p/a-hiring-manager-will-spend-6-seconds)); "a survey of 74 UX recruiters found 3 to 5 in-depth case studies performed better" and "portfolios failing to quantify research impact get filtered out 70% of the time, according to NN/g" (both surfaced only via search summary of [figr.design](https://figr.design/blog/ux-research-portfolio) and [pathunbound.com](https://pathunbound.com/ux-portfolio-examples/)). The 70% NN/g figure does not appear in the NN/g article fetched above. The "6 seconds" number appears to be borrowed from the generic resume eye-tracking study (Ladders), not a portfolio study.

### Inferences
- The realistic budget per case study is: seconds to earn a read, then roughly 2 to 3 minutes of scanning, not reading. A case study is judged mostly on its title, the opening block and whatever numbers or decisions a skimmer's eye can land on.
- Reviewers read a first case study and often check a second only for consistency. The first case study in the order does most of the work.

### Gaps
- No NN/g 2020 portfolio survey with time data was found; the 2019 NN/g article with its 204-person survey is the closest primary source. No Figma, ADPList or Dribbble survey with time-per-case-study data was found.
- No eye-tracking or analytics study of portfolio case study reading was found.

## 2. Signals that predict "I want to talk to this person" and the top red flags

### Takeaway
The strongest positive signals are visible judgment (why a decision was made, what was cut, what constraint forced a tradeoff), unambiguous personal ownership, a clear problem framing and a distinct point of view. Craft is a gate, not the differentiator. The top red flags are "we" with no "I", final screens with no path to them, textbook linear process and sameness.

### Cited Findings
Positive signals
- NN/g's hiring managers: "Show me how you started with an opportunity and produced real value"; they want reasoning behind decisions including elements that did not make the cut, plus problem, role, constraints, timeline and how research influenced the work (2019). Source: [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- Julie Zhuo's three criteria at Facebook: the idea ("Is there a solid rationale behind why they worked on what they worked on?"), usability, and craftsmanship ("Did the designer sweat over all the details?"). Originally 2015, updated Feb 2024, so outside the preferred window but still widely cited. Source: [First Round Review, Facebook's method for hiring designers](https://review.firstround.com/an-inside-look-at-facebooks-method-for-hiring-designers/)
- Julie Zhuo (2026): portfolios that stand out have "something visceral in their quality" and "a really strong core message or concept." Strong candidates "remember the mess." Source: [Julie Zhuo](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer)
- Indeed Design hiring managers (April 2020). Susan Le, UX Director: looks for "depth and breadth" and project overviews with "problem statement, process, team makeup." Corey Chandler, UX Design Manager: "I would rather see a smaller product that actually shipped... How designers weather challenges is more interesting." Source: [Indeed Design, UX Design Portfolio Advice from Hiring Managers](https://indeed.design/article/ux-design-portfolio-advice-from-hiring-managers/)
- Leslie Yang, hiring manager and former lead product designer (June 26 2023): wants a "balance of process, craft, and clear understanding of the user and business." Source: [Leslie Yang, What a hiring manager looks for](https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/what-a-hiring-manager-looks-for-in)
- Emily Backes: specific ownership statements, acknowledged tradeoffs and constraints, quantified outcomes, a distinct perspective across projects, what they would do differently. "A reviewer can't credit you for a decision they can't find." "Competence isn't the bar... Sameness is what this reader is screening against." Source: [Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)
- Tom Scott (2026): the underlying question is "Does this person think the way our best designers think?"; signals are curiosity, agency, rigor and cross-functional collaboration. Visual craft is non-negotiable for IC roles. AI fluency is "the sharpest dividing line" in 2026. "Live, in-progress work can outperform a polished portfolio." Source: [Tom Scott](https://verifiedinsider.substack.com/p/faq-design-hiring-in-2026)
- Jared Spool's four missing pieces in case studies (via search summary; primary page blocked): timeline and order of steps, effort and depth, the candidate's role in collaborative work and how it changed, and what they learned. His 2019 thread asks "Which pieces were you solely responsible for?... Who did you need approval from?" Sources: [Jared Spool, Reviewing UX Portfolios: 4 High-Risk Hiring Mistakes](https://jmspool.medium.com/reviewing-ux-portfolios-4-high-risk-hiring-mistakes-ea004cb845ae) (content not directly verified); [Jared Spool on X, 2019](https://x.com/jmspool/status/1122673629077090304)

Red flags
- Final mockups only. Susan Le: "It's a red flag when all I see are final mockups with no understanding of how the candidate arrived at that solution." Source: [Indeed Design](https://indeed.design/article/ux-design-portfolio-advice-from-hiring-managers/)
- Unclear ownership. Leslie Yang: "I will move on to another applicant's portfolio because I don't know if they or someone else worked on the designs." Source: [Leslie Yang](https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/what-a-hiring-manager-looks-for-in). Same flag from Emily Backes ("we redesigned" vs "I redesigned") and Matej Latin.
- Textbook linear process. Matej Latin, from 1,000+ portfolios reviewed (Feb 25 2025), says roughly 90% fail because of cookie-cutter, linear research-to-UI stories; he describes them as reading like "school homework." Personal estimate, not a survey. Source: [Matej Latin via ADPList, 90% of designers are unhirable?](https://adplist.substack.com/p/90-of-designers-are-unhirable)
- Generic or effortful to parse. Zhuo: portfolios that "require effort before they reward attention." Source: [Julie Zhuo](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer)
- Communication and detail failures in the first 30 seconds: pixelated images, typos, generic titles, case studies over a 10 minute read. Source: [Matej Latin](https://adplist.substack.com/p/only-30-seconds-to-reject-your-portfolio)
- The portfolio site itself. Mollie Cox: "Did you UX your own site?" Source: [Mollie Cox](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/molliecox_hiring-manager-pov-my-portfolio-review-activity-7158534013253545984-Pgmm)
- Zero friction. Emily Backes lists "zero visible friction or constraints" and process without outcomes. Source: [Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)
- Stale polish. Tom Scott: polished work 3 to 4 years old that hides current capability; a portfolio that looks like "an afterthought." Source: [Tom Scott](https://verifiedinsider.substack.com/p/faq-design-hiring-in-2026)
- Access friction. Corey Chandler: password-protected portfolios cause delays and lost consideration. Source: [Indeed Design](https://indeed.design/article/ux-design-portfolio-advice-from-hiring-managers/)

### Inferences
- Across sources the deciding question is less "is this good work" and more "can I see how this person thinks and what they personally decided." Decisions a skimmer can locate (labeled, near the top, tied to a constraint) are what convert.
- For a senior candidate, sameness is the main risk. A distinct framing or point of view is named by Zhuo, Backes and Latin independently.

### Gaps
- No quantitative ranking of signals (for example a survey weighting judgment vs craft vs outcomes) was found.

## 3. Do reviewers read to the end, and what should the last impression be?

### Takeaway
Mostly no. Reviewers scan top-down and jump to numbers; if the top does not hook, the ending is not seen. The ending matters mainly for the reader who is already interested: it should show outcome, honest reflection (what did not work, what you would do differently) and learning. That is also what interviewers probe.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g: case studies must be scannable because reviewers have little time; very rarely is a portfolio read word for word (the "word for word" phrasing came from a search summary and is UNVERIFIED as NN/g's exact words). Source: [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- Mollie Cox skips to data on a second pass: "skim again and look for numbers." Source: [Mollie Cox](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/molliecox_hiring-manager-pov-my-portfolio-review-activity-7158534013253545984-Pgmm)
- Recommendation to put the result in the first paragraph rather than at the bottom (practitioner guide, author and date not verified). Source: [UX Playbook](https://uxplaybook.org/articles/ux-case-study-minto-pyramid-structure-guide) (UNVERIFIED)
- Spool lists learning as one of four things case studies rarely include: how past work informed this project and how this project informed later work. Source: [Jared Spool](https://jmspool.medium.com/reviewing-ux-portfolios-4-high-risk-hiring-mistakes-ea004cb845ae) (via search summary)
- Emily Backes counts "what they'd do differently next time" as a signal to interview. Source: [Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)
- Facebook red flag: claiming portfolio work is "perfect" with no improvements. Source: [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/an-inside-look-at-facebooks-method-for-hiring-designers/)
- Zhuo (2026) wants designers who discuss what "didn't work." Source: [Julie Zhuo](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer)

### Inferences
- Put the outcome at the top and the reflection at the bottom. The top converts the skimmer; the ending converts the reader who is deciding whether to spend an hour in an interview, and it pre-answers the "what would you do differently" question.
- The best last impression is a specific, owned lesson or open question that the reader wants to ask about, not a summary or a thank you.

### Gaps
- No source measures scroll depth or completion rates on portfolio case studies.

## 4. What hiring managers ask about in interviews and how a case study can plant those conversations

### Takeaway
Interviewers ask the same few things everywhere: what would you do differently, which decisions were yours, why that decision over the alternatives, how you worked within constraints and with partners, and what the outcome was. A case study plants those conversations by naming the decisions, the alternatives rejected, the constraint that forced each tradeoff and one honest unresolved question.

### Cited Findings
- Facebook interview questions (Zhuo): "If you had two more months, what would you have done differently?"; "What decisions were you directly responsible for on large teams?"; and, in critique, "Why do you think this designer made those decisions, and do you agree?" Format: open conversations with 3 to 4 designers, not formal presentations. Source: [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/an-inside-look-at-facebooks-method-for-hiring-designers/)
- Leslie Yang's portfolio presentation (about 45 minutes plus 15 minutes discussion) probes how the candidate "incorporate[s] empathy and user pains," works "within design constraints" and factors "business goals into your design work," plus outcomes. Source: [Leslie Yang](https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/what-a-hiring-manager-looks-for-in)
- Tom Scott (2026): "What would you have done differently?" probes, technical depth, and real answers vs generic frameworks; a gap between articulation and execution is a red flag. Source: [Tom Scott](https://verifiedinsider.substack.com/p/faq-design-hiring-in-2026)
- Spool's questions: which pieces were solely yours, who you needed approval from, how you presented for approval, whether you worked with devs and PMs. Source: [Jared Spool on X](https://x.com/jmspool/status/1122673629077090304)
- Zhuo (2026) cites Soleio's diagnostic question candidates can ask: "If I joined your team for a month, what would I work on?" and values designers who "ask for the ball" and own outcomes. Source: [Julie Zhuo](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer)
- Tanner Christensen (search summary, primary blocked): write the case study like telling a close friend over half an hour what was frustrating, what was exciting and what you would tell them. Source: [Notably, Sarah Doody and Tanner Christensen](https://www.notably.ai/blog/shaping-up-your-portfolio-to-ace-your-next-ux-interview-with-sarah-doody-tanner-christensen) (UNVERIFIED, page failed to load)

### Inferences
- Interview hooks that map to real interview questions: a labeled "decision I'd defend" with the rejected alternative; a named constraint and the tradeoff it forced; an explicit "my call vs the team's call" line; a "what I'd do with two more months" or "what I'd change" line; one open question the work did not resolve.
- These hooks double as the skimmer's signals from section 2, so the same elements serve the 30 second scan and the 60 minute interview.

### Gaps
- No podcast transcripts (Design Details, Honest UX, Dive Club) or Config talks were successfully reviewed in this pass; they may hold further named quotes.
- No survey data on interview question frequency was found.
