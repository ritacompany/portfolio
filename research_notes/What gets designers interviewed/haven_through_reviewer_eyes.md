# Haven through a hiring manager's eyes

Project facts below come only from `reference/haven/HAVEN-ENDING-BRIEF.md` and `reference/haven/HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md` (both read 30 Sep 2026). External criteria are cited with source dates. Several primary pages (Julie Zhuo on Medium, the Design at Meta internship guide, a Design Bootcamp hiring manager post) returned 403 and are cited only where a mirror or search summary was available; those are flagged.

Haven as it stands, for reference: opens on belonging being "a difficult and isolating experience" for LGBTQIA+ teens without affirming environments; research with two proxy groups (3 SMEs, 3 LGBTQIA+ college students), 6 interviews, 5 interview insights, framed by the guardian consent wall; MVP in two phases (Identity, then Community) with "Case study end point" marked at the Identity launch; 6 usability tests, 4 testing insights; solutions (Confidante program, identity quiz, Confidante selection moved after the intro chat, redesigned Confidante profile, scheduling, text-only chats, privacy setup with disguised icon, app switcher hiding, notifications off, PIN with Face ID); all 9 findings tagged to the design changes they drove. Solo CareerFoundry bootcamp project, unshipped.

## Which published criteria apply, and how Haven scores section by section

### Takeaway
Across NN/g, Facebook's hiring method, Julie Zhuo, Indeed's UX managers, Sarah Doody and working reviewers, the shared test is visible intentionality: a real constraint, a decision it forced, evidence that changed the design and the designer's own judgment about what is still uncertain. Haven passes that test in its research framing, the Confidante finding and the privacy setup, and is most exposed in its opening line, its template section structure and its bootcamp, unshipped status.

### Cited Findings
Published criteria (the rubric applied below):
- NN/g surveyed 204 UX professionals in hiring roles; hiring managers want to see how the designer "started with an opportunity and produced real value", the "messy process" rather than only the finished product and "why elements made it in"; they "very rarely" read a portfolio word for word, so scannability matters; student projects without realistic constraints are an implied red flag (published 4 Aug 2019) [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- Facebook's design hiring method judges portfolio work on The Idea (does it solve a real problem), Usability and Craftsmanship, and prizes intentionality: "Every decision they've made has a purpose" (First Round Review, 28 Jan 2015, updated 29 Feb 2024) [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/an-inside-look-at-facebooks-method-for-hiring-designers/)
- Julie Zhuo: okay designers show work, excellent ones "communicate intent"; the first job of a portfolio is "intrigue", not completeness; the best have "a really strong core message or concept" and work like "a trailer" for who the designer is (Looking Glass, dated 14 Apr 2026 on Substack; original Medium post returned 403) [Julie Zhuo, Looking Glass](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer)
- Zhuo on describing work: say what made the problem interesting or challenging, the space of options, the constraints balanced, and the outcome "whether quantitative or qualitative" (search summary of her X thread; not fetched directly) [Julie Zhuo on X](https://twitter.com/joulee/status/1128312350996480000)
- Indeed UX managers (April 2020): Matthew Nesbitt wants "conflict and resolution" and flags formulaic bootcamp portfolios that make candidates "seem scripted"; Corey Chandler prefers shipped products over concept projects and evidence of weathering real-world challenges; Rhonda Gilligan wants validation testing, iteration and "the 'before' if there was one"; Annie Jarvis wants iterative design based on feedback [Indeed Design](https://indeed.design/article/ux-design-portfolio-advice-from-hiring-managers/)
- Sarah Doody: the hiring manager wants "your thought process" and why, not just what; screenshots without explanation are a red flag; the peer panel looks for what didn't go well and lessons learned (published 27 May 2020, updated 10 Jun 2023) [Career Strategy Lab](https://www.careerstrategylab.com/3-users-of-your-ux-portfolio/)
- Emily Backes (senior product designer, former technical recruiter, undated): recruiter screen about 10 seconds; design review 2 to 3 minutes per case study; what advances a candidate is "A number. A decision. A trade-off you'd make differently."; "The designers who tell me what went sideways... are the ones I remember a week later"; zero visible friction reads as over-edited [Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)
- Matej Latin (ADPList newsletter, 14 Mar 2025): hiring managers "ditch most design portfolios in 30 seconds"; rejection triggers include generic titles, boring opening sentences, checklist templates instead of storytelling, vague fluff and case studies over a 10 minute read [ADPList newsletter](https://adplist.substack.com/p/only-30-seconds-to-reject-your-portfolio)
- Florian Bölter (15 Oct 2025): hiring managers spot generic phrasing instantly; template checklist sections are a negative; missing results are a red flag [Open Doors Careers](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-recruiters-and-hiring-managers-actually-look-at-your-portfolio)
- Tobias van Schneider: 1 to 2 in-depth case studies; do not bury the lede; he wants designers who don't leave loose ends, such as missing states or only one of several use cases (search summary of his DESK pages; not fetched directly, undated) [House of van Schneider](https://vanschneider.com/category/portfolio-tips)
- Portfolio review interviews (Meta style) ask "Why did you choose that design solution over this one?" and "What were the tradeoffs?", and expect candidates to critique their own designs (search summary of the Design at Meta internship guide, which returned 403; treat as secondary) [Design at Meta](https://medium.com/designatmeta/a-guide-to-interviewing-for-product-design-internships-d719dd4c146c)
- Bootcamp sameness: portfolios follow the identical sequence of research, persona, site map, user flow, lo-fi, hi-fi, and personas appear "without explaining where they came from" (search summaries of Design Bootcamp posts; the pages returned 403 or were not fetched; low authority) [Design Bootcamp](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/a-hiring-managers-perspective-on-your-portfolio-58eb81c2abd9)
- MentorCruise: speculative projects are acceptable when they document "the decision trail as rigorously as a shipped project would"; cold review runs 3 to 5 minutes (commercial coaching source, quoted in the earlier Haven report) [MentorCruise](https://mentorcruise.com/blog/product-design-portfolio-how-to-build-one-that-shows-business-impact/)

## Which moments make a reviewer want to talk, and which read as bootcamp material

### Takeaway
Three moments would start an interview conversation: testing overturning the Confidante pick, the privacy setup as threat modelling and the consent constraint that made the target user unreachable. The MVP phasing and the measurement restraint are secondary talking points. The opening line, the feature list (quiz, scheduling) and research counts on their own read as bootcamp scaffolding.

### Cited Findings
- Reviewers remember "what went sideways" and want "A number. A decision. A trade-off" [Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)
- Hiring managers want "the 'before' if there was one" and narrative "conflict and resolution" [Indeed Design](https://indeed.design/article/ux-design-portfolio-advice-from-hiring-managers/)
- Interviews probe "What were the tradeoffs?" and ask candidates to critique their own work (secondary, via search summary) [Design at Meta](https://medium.com/designatmeta/a-guide-to-interviewing-for-product-design-internships-d719dd4c146c)
- Process narration such as "I ran 5 moderated tests" is not evidence; reviewers want the consequence (earlier Haven report) [MentorCruise](https://mentorcruise.com/blog/usability-testing-what-your-portfolio-entry-says-about-you-as-a-researcher/)
- In published research, 75% of sexual and gender minority teens would be unwilling or unsure to join a study requiring parental permission, which makes the consent wall a documented problem rather than an excuse (earlier Haven report) [Mustanski et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5768203/)
- Honest qualitative evidence beats fabricated quantitative in senior reviewers' eyes (earlier Haven report) [UX Companion](https://uxcompanion.co.uk/ux-portfolio-mistakes)

Ranked by interview pull (Haven facts from local files; ranking is my judgment against the criteria above):

1. **Confidante selection moved after the intro chat.** Testers did not feel confident choosing without getting to know the Confidante, hesitated, went back and forth and some undid their pick; selection moved after the introductory chat (HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md). It is the only place with a before, a conflict and a resolution, and it shows the designer's own flow being proven wrong. The obvious interview question ("what did you consider instead of an intro chat?") is one Chadwick can answer. Constraint: no count exists, so it must stay descriptive.
2. **Privacy setup.** Disguised icon, app switcher hiding, notifications off, PIN with Face ID (brief). This is threat modelling for a teen whose phone may be seen by family. It invites the tradeoff conversation reviewers look for (discretion versus findability and re-entry friction) and passes van Schneider's no loose ends test because it covers several exposure moments, not one.
3. **Consent constraint and proxy groups.** A minor needs guardian consent; asking would out the teen; college students and SMEs were the closest reachable proxies (HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md). It meets NN/g's realistic constraints bar and is rare in bootcamp work. Its interview value is the judgment behind the proxy choice and its bias, not the logistics.
4. **What testing cannot support.** Quiz completion, passcode and disguise adoption and intro to booking conversion were ruled out as measures from a controlled session (HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md). Signals measurement judgment; a reviewer would ask about it if it is visible, but it is a supporting beat.
5. **MVP phasing (Identity, then Community).** Shows scope discipline. Reads as generic unless the page says why identity must come before community for this user.

Likely to read as bootcamp material:
- The opening line on belonging being difficult and isolating (Latin's boring opening trigger).
- Identity quiz and scheduling as listed features without a stated reason tied to the teen's risk.
- "6 interviews, 5 insights, 6 tests, 4 insights" as bare counts (process narration).
- Template section names.

### Inferences
- The top three moments share one idea: Haven is designed around the teen's exposure risk, and the designer changed his own design when evidence said to. That is the portfolio's core message in Zhuo's sense.
- The page's strongest moment (item 1) sits in the middle of a long page. If NN/g's scan pattern holds, many reviewers never see it unless it is signalled near the top or in a heading. That is a bigger lever on interview odds than the ending.

### Gaps
- No count of testers who hesitated or undid their pick (open fact only Chadwick holds).
- Whether testers overlapped with interviewees is unresolved; safe wording is "6 interviews" and "6 usability tests", never 12.
- No external data on how often reviewers ask about specific case study moments; the ranking is inference from stated criteria.

## What the ending's job should be, given skimming and the rest of the page

### Takeaway
The ending serves only the reviewer who is already interested, so its job is to leave that reader with the designer's judgment about Haven and its teen, in one short beat, then hand off to the next case study. It should not recap the findings, restate the consent logistics, list future work, invent outcomes or deliver a slogan.

### Cited Findings
- Most portfolios are rejected in about 30 seconds; case studies over a 10 minute read are a trigger [ADPList newsletter](https://adplist.substack.com/p/only-30-seconds-to-reject-your-portfolio)
- Design review of a passing case study takes 2 to 3 minutes [Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)
- Hiring managers "very rarely" read a portfolio word for word [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- 81% of viewing time falls in the first three screenfuls (NN/g 2018, cited in the earlier ending report) [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- Reviewers remember the designer who names what went sideways and the trade-off they'd make differently; flawless reads as inauthentic [Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)
- Candidates are expected to critique their own designs and discuss tradeoffs (secondary) [Design at Meta](https://medium.com/designatmeta/a-guide-to-interviewing-for-product-design-internships-d719dd4c146c)
- Generic phrasing and template sections (a stock "What I learned") are spotted instantly [Open Doors Careers](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-recruiters-and-hiring-managers-actually-look-at-your-portfolio)
- CareerFoundry tells students without impact data to lay out how they would measure impact and show next steps (earlier report) [CareerFoundry](https://careerfoundry.com/en/blog/ux-design/ux-case-study/)
- Editors cut summary conclusions; strong endings return to the beginning, twist or quote (earlier ending report) [Nieman Storyboard](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2026/01/16/how-to-end-a-story/); [Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/educators-students/2016/want-a-strong-ending-to-your-story-here-are-3-tips/)
- Doody's peer panel values reflection on what didn't go well and lessons learned [Career Strategy Lab](https://www.careerstrategylab.com/3-users-of-your-ux-portfolio/)
- A clear next action (another case study or contact) belongs at the bottom (earlier ending report) [Learn UI Design](https://www.learnui.design/blog/great-ux-ui-portfolio-case-study.html)

Candidate jobs, judged against the criteria:

| Job | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Restate the strongest decision | No | Recap for a reader who just saw it; editors cut it; the skimmer who missed it never arrives |
| Show the designer's judgment | Yes, primary | The criterion every source shares (intent, trade-off, what went sideways) |
| Reflect | Only if real | A genuine change in how he works would land; a stock "what I learned" is a generic phrasing trigger. The brief lists this as an open fact only Chadwick holds |
| Point forward | Yes, as one clause | Status plus the next real test reads as judgment; a roadmap or list reads as unfinished |
| Invite conversation | Via handoff, not copy | The next case study card and footer do this; a "let's talk" line inside the ending duplicates it |

What the ending should not do (each maps to a failed round in the brief):
- Recap or tally the 9 findings or the research counts (rounds 1 and 3).
- Center proxy groups or research logistics (round 3; the brief calls it a misdirection).
- Reduce the case study to a slogan (round 2's "a profile gives information, a conversation gives trust").
- Propose an unrealistic study of minors without parental permission (round 2).
- Repeat the MVP timeline (round 2).
- Use rows or list styling that reads as interactive (round 1 and the brief's list rule).
- Invent a metric or claim the intro chat change "reduced" hesitation; it has not been retested.

### Inferences
- Because the consent wall is already stated in the research section, an ending that leads with it is a partial recap. The ending should be about Haven and the teen at the Identity launch, with research appearing at most as the reason for the next test.
- The judgment worth ending on is an honest open question about the teen: whether the intro chat first flow and the privacy setup hold up at home, where Haven is actually used, which the controlled sessions could not show. That states status, names the uncertainty Backes rewards and points to the one next test (18 and 19 year olds who still live with family, per HAVEN-EVIDENCE.md) without becoming a list.
- The earlier ending report's constraints still hold and fit these criteria: one beat, four or fewer ideas, about 30 words at display size, the next case study card as the only interactive element.
- A reflection line is the highest upside element but cannot be drafted; it depends on Chadwick's own answer.

### Gaps
- No published hiring manager source addresses the final section of a case study specifically; the ending guidance is inferred from general criteria plus the editorial and reading research in the earlier reports.
- What designing Haven changed in how Chadwick works is unknown and must come from him.

## The single takeaway a hiring manager should leave with

### Takeaway
"He designs for the risk his user carries and changes his own design when evidence says to." Every strong moment on the page (the privacy setup, the consent constraint, the Confidante move) supports that sentence, and the ending should leave it standing rather than add a new idea.

### Cited Findings
- A portfolio should carry "a really strong core message or concept" and work as a trailer for who the designer is [Julie Zhuo, Looking Glass](https://lg.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-a-world-class-designer)
- Reviewers look for a consistent point of view and visible judgment calls [Emily Backes](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)
- Facebook's hiring bar: every decision has a purpose [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/an-inside-look-at-facebooks-method-for-hiring-designers/)
- Iterative design based on feedback is a named hiring manager want [Indeed Design](https://indeed.design/article/ux-design-portfolio-advice-from-hiring-managers/)

### Inferences
- The two halves map to the two things concept work must prove without launch data: product judgment for a specific person (privacy, consent) and willingness to be wrong (testing overturned the pick).
- The takeaway must not borrow Mondai or any employer framing; it is about how Chadwick works, drawn only from Haven.
- A competing candidate takeaway, "he does careful research under hard constraints", is weaker: it foregrounds logistics, which the brief already found to be a misdirection, and it is about method rather than judgment.

### Gaps
- Whether this takeaway fits Chadwick's positioning across the other two case studies is outside these files; it should be checked against the homepage claim before it shapes the ending copy.
