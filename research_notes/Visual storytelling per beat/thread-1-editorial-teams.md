# How editorial graphics teams choose a visual form per story beat

Source dates are given in brackets after each citation. Where a quote reached me only through a search snippet or a secondary write up, that is flagged.

## Q1. From story outline to a list of graphics: the process

### Takeaway
No team publishes a formal "outline to graphics list" template. What they describe is consistent: read the draft or outline looking for points that are about a trend, a place or a process; decide the one idea the reader must get; sketch rough and cheap; kill anything that does not survive the sketch; then one editor owns the final form and placement.

### Cited findings
- NYT graphics desk (Ebola coverage, 2014): graphics editors read reporters' drafts "looking for opportunities", especially points about "statistical trends or locations", then tried to find and chart the data and make "the maps and diagrams that could support, clarify and illustrate those points". Selection weighed "the importance of the story, on its potential for visual storytelling" and whether the desk could absorb it. Ideas were discussed in meetings, roughly sketched, then attempted; some survived and some were dropped. Once assigned, the graphics editor decided the final form; director Steve Duenes gave the final edit and decided art direction and placement. Interviewee Sergio Peçanha. [Storybench, Aleszu Bajak, 9 Jan 2015](https://www.storybench.org/?p=972)
- Steve Duenes (NYT graphics director) on structure: with process graphics "narrative is sort of built in", but with data driven pieces the desk often has "to develop the structure of the graphic" so readers can walk through the data. Flag: seen only in a search snippet; the Nieman Lab page returned 403. [Nieman Lab, March 2016](https://www.niemanlab.org/2016/03/from-service-desk-to-standalone-news-desk-how-the-new-york-times-graphics-department-has-transitioned-to-the-mobile-age/)
- The Pudding (Ilia Blinderman): first "think through the most important points that you're trying to communicate", then consider "which type of visualization would be most effective at expressing it"; build static drafts in a simple tool (Keynote, Figma) before writing code, to test whether the chart communicates the intended insight. Team projects start with "the initial whiteboard session". [The Pudding, "Making Internet Things, Part 2: Design", Ilia Blinderman, undated on page, c. 2018](https://pudding.cool/process/how-to-make-dope-shit-part-2/)
- Jonathan Corum (NYT science graphics editor): before drawing, look for a single visual idea in the research; "Sketches are not commitments"; "I'd rather have an ugly sketch that expresses a core, clear thought, than a beautiful sketch that is not quite there." [Corum, See Think Design Produce 3 talk, 11 Feb 2015, transcript posted 28 Mar 2016](https://style.org/stdp3/); [Corum, "Design for an Audience", 16 Apr 2018](https://style.org/ku/)
- Corum's editing question: "what can you remove", testing whether a cut changes the story or only simplifies it. Sketch for yourself first, then design for others. [style.org/ku, 16 Apr 2018](https://style.org/ku/)
- Simon Scarr (then SCMP graphics director, later Reuters Graphics): research "the general subject I'm covering and not just the data itself"; "I also like to sketch ideas and illustrations out in the early stage". [Visualising Data, Andy Kirk interview, 9 Apr 2013](https://visualisingdata.com/?p=7114)
- Reuters Graphics (Marco Hernandez): "Some of our best ideas ended up being average ones and good ideas have been refined into great ideas." He posts "beta graphics versions of stories that never go public" as #infofails. [Online Journalism Blog, Hanna Duggal, 16 Dec 2020](https://onlinejournalismblog.com/2020/12/16/striking-the-balance-between-graphic-design-and-data-journalism-design-is-a-conversation/)
- Newsroom review practice: several outlets at SRCCON (Quartz, WSJ, NPR, ProPublica, Bloomberg) described a "fresh eyes" check: "somebody who hasn't looked at the data just have a quick look and say, I get this or I don't get this." Becky Bowers (WSJ): if the chart leaves you unable to explain the point, "I would say that's a chart failure." [SRCCON 2015 session transcript, "Let's Stop Worrying and Let Our Reporters Make Their Own Graphics"](https://2015.srccon.org/docs/transcripts/reportergraphics/)

### Inferences
- The repeatable step the case study designer wants maps cleanly onto the NYT practice: read the section draft, mark each point as trend, place, process, comparison or argument, then and only then pick a form. "Process" beats (a framework, a decision flow) are the ones Duenes says carry their own narrative, which suits a qualitative case study.
- The order every source agrees on is idea first, form second, layout last. Nobody starts from a layout.

### Gaps
- No published NYT, FT or Reuters storyboard template or checklist was found. Bloomberg, Washington Post and Guardian process write ups were not found in the time available; a PolicyViz episode with WaPo's Seth Blanchard exists but its content was not accessible.
- Pudding process pages by Jan Diehm were not found; a Design Observer podcast (S9E8, 2021) exists but only its summary was available: https://designobserver.com/s9e8-jan-diehm

## Q2. Decision rules for picking a form per beat, and when a beat stays text

### Takeaway
The governing test across Reuters, NYT and the Pudding is "would the reader understand this just as well without a visual?" If yes, it stays text. For non numeric beats the teams reach for diagrams of mechanism or sequence, annotated photos and real world reference objects, not charts.

### Cited findings
- Reuters Graphics (Scarr): the team works on stories where the visual aspects are essential to understanding, and asks whether visuals add value or whether the reader can understand the story just as well without them. Flag: search snippet only; page returned 403. [Design Week, Jan 2020](https://www.designweek.co.uk/issues/27-january-2-february-2020/in-house-teams-how-reuters-graphics-visualises-catastrophic-world-events/)
- Scarr and Hernandez (Reuters): "If a story does not benefit from a graphic then we don't make one." Hernandez: "Ultimately the strength of the graphic lies in its simplicity and the message it delivers." [Online Journalism Blog, 16 Dec 2020](https://onlinejournalismblog.com/2020/12/16/striking-the-balance-between-graphic-design-and-data-journalism-design-is-a-conversation/)
- Reuters graphics reporter Ally Levine: visuals are not always the best way to tell a story, but when they are, they should be created; her Chile protest story was built around a sequence of Reuters photos. Flag: from search summary of Storybench interview. [Storybench Behind the Scenes](https://www.storybench.org/?p=12263)
- Archie Tse (NYT), outcome of cutting interactives: "We are writing and editing a lot more text." [The Mario Blog, Mario Garcia, 11 Oct 2016, reporting Tse's talk](https://garciamedia.com/?p=2509)
- Corum on non numeric explanation: for mechanisms, cell motion and development over time he breaks loops or dense flowcharts into sequenced steps; adds real world reference points (the rat in the grid cell box, photos of the subject) to anchor abstract results; for data prefers direct comparisons (sorted bars, dot plots) over tables. [style.org/ku, 16 Apr 2018](https://style.org/ku/)
- Corum: "I don't want to have a preconceived notion of what this is going to look like." He replaced a chart with an explanation of what light curves are and what happens when a planet passes in front of its star; "I'm not trying to make simplistic graphics, but I'm trying to make simple graphics." [style.org/stdp3, talk 11 Feb 2015](https://style.org/stdp3/)
- Scarr: "Not everything is data driven or involves technical vector drawing"; for dry subjects he uses "design, use of headline and representations of familiar objects" to draw readers in. [Visualising Data, 9 Apr 2013](https://visualisingdata.com/?p=7114)
- NYT "The Path of the Outbreak": a non numeric motion graphic whose goal was to "explain how the outbreak unfolded over those first few months", showing porous borders and transport routes. [Storybench, 9 Jan 2015](https://www.storybench.org/?p=972)
- Pudding (Daniels via interview summary): focus on stories that would struggle as a 2000 word essay. Flag: secondary. [Off Menu substack, "The proof is in The Pudding"](https://offmenu.substack.com/p/the-proof-is-in-the-pudding)
- FT Visual Vocabulary: a poster and web page organising chart types by the relationship the chart shows (for example change over time, ranking, part to whole), made by the FT graphics desk as a shared language for choosing chart types. Flag: description from a secondary source. [data.europa.eu data visualisation guide](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/visual-vocabulary)
- Burn-Murdoch (FT): "we are not afraid to do a story which just has five line charts in it", i.e. plain forms are acceptable when they carry the point. [Eye 101, Summer 2021](https://eyemagazine.com/feature/article/conversation-with-john-burn-murdoch)
- Blinderman (Pudding) frames the form choice by the type of message: change over time, differences between quantities, variation, geographic patterns. [pudding.cool process](https://pudding.cool/process/how-to-make-dope-shit-part-2/)

### Inferences
- A usable per beat rule set, synthesised from the above: (1) Could the reader get it as well from a sentence? Then text. (2) Is it a sequence, mechanism or loop? Then a stepped diagram. (3) Is it about a real object or place? Then an annotated image or map. (4) Is it a comparison of states (before and after)? Then side by side or a strip, per Corum. (5) Only if it is a quantity does the Visual Vocabulary chart menu apply.
- For a case study with mostly qualitative beats, the relevant precedents are Corum's mechanism diagrams and Reuters' photo sequence stories, not chart selection guides.

### Gaps
- No explicit written decision tree from any of these desks was found. The FT Visual Vocabulary covers numeric data only; I found nothing equivalent from these teams for conceptual or framework diagrams.

## Q3. Annotation, chart headlines that state the finding, and reading order

### Takeaway
Every desk treats annotation as the core of the work, not a finishing layer. Titles should make a point, labels sit directly on the data, and nothing important lives in a tooltip. Reading order is designed: either staged step by step or written so either entry point works.

### Cited findings
- Amanda Cox (NYT): "The annotation layer is critical, even in a newspaper (where the data is not usually super complicated)"; the words in a graphic "should highlight the relevant pattern"; "Most of our graphics can stand on their own". [Simply Statistics interview, 1 Jun 2012](https://simplystatistics.org/posts/2012-06-01-interview-with-amanda-cox-graphics-editor-at-the-new/)
- Cox's talk "Data Visualization at the New York Times" (2014 to 2015) was organised around Scale, Context, Pattern and Annotation Layer, per an attendee's notes; she said graphs are stronger when they say something. Flag: secondary attendee notes. [Ricardo Miranda Zúñiga blog, Feb 2015](https://www.ambriente.com/blog/2015/02/); event listing [Acadia University](https://aida.acadiau.ca/event-reader/Amanda-Cox.html)
- Corum: annotation is "the most important thing that we do on the graphics desk", more important than design. Label directly on the data, remove keys and redundant axis labels; fade background lines and boxes; use one consistent arrow style so readers do not wonder whether differences carry meaning. [style.org/ku, 16 Apr 2018](https://style.org/ku/)
- Corum: "Visualization alone is not explanation." "We need to be able to point, we need to able to annotate." On reading order: "I tried to write in such a way that it didn't matter if you started on the left or on the right." [style.org/stdp3, 2015](https://style.org/stdp3/)
- Burn-Murdoch (FT): the FT goes back and forth on titles and subheadings; a descriptive title means people understand the chart but are not prompted to do anything, while "if you give it a title that makes a point, you're going to get a stronger reaction". Also: "There's a lot of research that shows that when you're using charts to communicate, the text is critical." [Eye 101, Summer 2021](https://eyemagazine.com/feature/article/conversation-with-john-burn-murdoch)
- Tse (NYT): "If you make a tooltip or rollover, assume no one will ever see it." "If content is important for readers to see, don't hide it." [The Mario Blog, 11 Oct 2016](https://garciamedia.com/?p=2509). A secondary forum post attributes an 85 percent figure for readers ignoring tooltips to Tse's slides; I could not verify it in the slides. [futureofcoding linen thread](https://linen.futureofcoding.org/t/22673047/i-recall-coming-across-some-opinions-against-interactivity-i)
- SRCCON review criteria: can a reader extract the main point without spending a minute on the chart; does the comparison match the story. [SRCCON 2015](https://2015.srccon.org/docs/transcripts/reportergraphics/)

### Inferences
- "Title states the finding" is directly supported by Burn-Murdoch and Cox ("graphs are stronger when they say something"). For a case study beat, the section's diagram should carry a one line claim, not a label like "Framework overview".
- Cox's "can stand on their own" plus Corum's either side entry implies each visual should be readable without the paragraph around it, which matters for skimming recruiters.

### Gaps
- I did not reach an FT Chart Doctor column specifically on titles; the book How Charts Work (Alan Smith, Pearson, 2022) likely covers it but its text was not accessible.

## Q4. Making a graphic work as a still (print, social, mobile)

### Takeaway
The NYT and Pudding both moved toward scroll only, stacked, static forms because readers do not interact, and the Pudding recommends designing mobile first to force the essential version. The FT builds one chart that outputs to several sizes, and notes animation helps on social feeds.

### Cited findings
- Tse (NYT, Malofiej 24, Pamplona, March 2016, talk "Why We Are Doing Fewer Interactives"): steppers, tabs, fixed panels and sliders were used less because "readers weren't getting to all of the content"; "Readers just want to scroll." "If you make the reader click or do anything other than scroll, something spectacular has to happen." "When deciding whether to make something interactive, remember that getting it to work on all platforms is expensive." "Fewer small graphics embedded in articles. More stand-alone visual stories." "We still do interactives, but the bar is now VERY high." [The Mario Blog, 11 Oct 2016](https://garciamedia.com/?p=2509); event coverage [Nieman Lab, March 2016](https://www.niemanlab.org/2016/03/at-the-malofiej-infographics-world-summit-the-best-form-of-storytelling-is-often-static/); slides repo [github.com/archietse/malofiej-2016](https://github.com/archietse/malofiej-2016) (PDF not text readable via fetch)
- Russell Samora (The Pudding): mobile "First. Or second. It doesn't matter as long as you plan it out, ideally from the get-go." "The main advantage of starting with mobile first is that it forces you to pare down your experience to the nuts and bolts." "Sometimes doing a series of stacked charts is the way to go"; "a static chart or image can be downright quicker to code and debug on deadline." Keep scroll driven transitions only "if the transitions are truly meaningful, and not just something to make it pop". "The whole trend towards scrollytelling is to get away from forcing the user to click to reveal content." [The Pudding, "Responsive scrollytelling best practices", April 2017](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/)
- Burn-Murdoch (FT): "we only code one chart but the output of that is a mobile phone-sized version" (plus other sizes); "animated graphics resonate more with people" on social, because "something that's moving can catch your eye"; "We do them where they need to be done first and then work out where else we can use them." [Eye 101, Summer 2021](https://eyemagazine.com/feature/article/conversation-with-john-burn-murdoch)
- Reuters Graphics serves print and many language clients, so files must be editable and translatable, and the team avoids sending a large interactive to a client who needs print. Flag: search snippet. [Design Week, Jan 2020](https://www.designweek.co.uk/issues/27-january-2-february-2020/in-house-teams-how-reuters-graphics-visualises-catastrophic-world-events/)
- Reuters' Beirut blast explainer was "one long Illustrator ai2html artboard with scaled squares": a static artboard pipeline, not a bespoke interactive. [Online Journalism Blog, 16 Dec 2020](https://onlinejournalismblog.com/2020/12/16/striking-the-balance-between-graphic-design-and-data-journalism-design-is-a-conversation/)

### Inferences
- For a portfolio case study the safe default is the Tse and Samora position: each beat's visual is a static, stacked frame that works with no interaction; animation or scroll steps are optional enhancements that must not hold anything the reader needs.
- Samora's "mobile first forces you to pare down" doubles as a test for the per beat step: if the beat's visual cannot survive at phone width, the idea is not yet singular.

### Gaps
- No primary NYT or WaPo write up found on how they produce the print or social still from a scrollytelling piece specifically.
