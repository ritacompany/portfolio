# Layout and scrollytelling: how a long page carries a story, and which patterns survive as stills

Scope note: these notes serve a design portfolio case study laid out in Figma on a 12 column grid with 40px margins and gutters, built from existing section templates (title blocks, text plus one image, text plus two or three images, carousels). Motion comes later, so every pattern is judged on whether it works as a still first. Items under "Cited findings" are source claims. Items under "Inferences" are mine and are not claims any source makes.

## What taxonomies of scrollytelling and narrative layout exist?

### Takeaway
There is no single canonical taxonomy. The usable ones stack: Segel and Heer (2010) give genres and the author driven versus reader driven axis (martini glass, interactive slideshow, drill down), McKenna et al. (2017) give seven "flow factors" including story layout and stepper versus scroller, Oesch, Roth and Renner (NZZ, 2022) give five concrete scrollytelling techniques, and Bostock and The Pudding give the practitioner mechanics (sticky graphic with stepping text, no scrolljacking). Every one of the five NZZ techniques can be rendered as a sequence of key frames, which is the main thing a still layout needs.

### Cited findings

Academic taxonomies
- Segel and Heer, "Narrative visualization: telling stories with data" (IEEE TVCG 2010), systematically reviewed news and research case studies, identified distinct genres of narrative visualization and characterised them by interactivity and messaging. The central tension is between the narrative flow the author imposes and the story discovery the reader does through interaction. [IDL abstract](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/narrative)
- The same paper names three common structures: martini glass, interactive slideshow and drill down story. [Search summary of Segel and Heer, primary PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/narrative.pdf)
- Martini glass: a tightly author driven introduction (the narrow stem) followed by a stage where readers get controls to filter or aggregate (the wide bowl). A "reverse martini glass" opens with an interactive chart and then gives the author's findings. [data.europa.eu, martini glass story structure](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/martini-glass-story-structure)
- Interactive slideshow: interaction happens inside individual slides rather than across the whole piece, and a single slide can itself follow a martini glass shape. [data.europa.eu, martini glass story structure](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/martini-glass-story-structure)
- I could not extract the full list of Segel and Heer's genres from a readable source in this session (the PDF did not parse). Commonly cited as seven genres (magazine style, annotated chart, partitioned poster, flow chart, comic strip, slide show, film/video/animation), but treat this list as unverified here. [Primary PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/narrative.pdf)
- McKenna, Henry Riche, Lee, Boy and Meyer, "Visual narrative flow: exploring factors shaping data visualization story reading experiences" (EuroVis / Computer Graphics Forum 36(3), 2017) analysed 80 stories from popular websites and named seven flow factors: navigation input, level of control, navigation progress, story layout, role of visualization, story progression and navigation feedback. [Microsoft Research abstract](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/visual-narrative-flow-exploring-factors-shaping-data-visualization-story-reading-experiences/)
- McKenna et al. frame the "stepper versus scroller" debate: clicking or tapping through a story is like a slideshow, scrolling is like panning up and down a long document. [Utah VDL publication page via search summary](https://vdl.sci.utah.edu/publications/2017_eurovis_narrative-flow/)
- Oesch, Roth and Renner, "Scrolling into the newsroom: a vocabulary for scrollytelling techniques in visual online articles" (Information Design Journal, listed as forthcoming 2022), from the Neue Zürcher Zeitung graphics team, analysed 50 examples and grouped scroll elements ("scrollers") into five techniques: graphic sequence, animated transition, pan and zoom, moviescroller and show and play. [Benjamins catalogue](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/idj.22005.oes); [data.europa.eu scrollytelling introduction](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/scrollytelling-introduction)
- Their definition: scrollytelling is "a storytelling format in which visual and textual elements appear or change as the reader scrolls", usually with a fixed or updating visual that changes as text blocks scroll by. [data.europa.eu scrollytelling introduction](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/scrollytelling-introduction)
- Graphic sequence: text blocks move over a fixed visual that is replaced with a new graphic at each text block. Animated transition: a fixed graphic animates into a new state when a new text block arrives. [Search summary of the EU guide drawing on Oesch et al.](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/scrollytelling-introduction) (the per technique pages were not fetched, so these two definitions come from a search summary)
- Seyser and Zeiller, "Scrollytelling: an analysis of visual storytelling in online journalism" (22nd International Conference Information Visualisation, IEEE, 2018, pp. 401 to 406, DOI 10.1109/iV.2018.00075) asks which infographic types are used for complex data in online long form journalism and how they are embedded in the article. Full text is closed access and I could not read its categories. [IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/8564193); [FH Burgenland record](https://people.hochschule-burgenland.at/entities/publication/360687e9-e683-431c-b576-7f372b279621/details)
- Bach et al., "Narrative design patterns for data driven storytelling" is a 2018 book chapter (in Riche, Hurter, Diakopoulos and Carpendale, eds., Data-Driven Storytelling), not a CHI paper. It describes 18 patterns in five groups: argumentation, flow, framing the narrative, empathy and emotion, and engagement. Each pattern serves a purpose such as engaging the audience, evoking empathy or creating flow and rhythm. [University of Twente record](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/narrative-design-patterns-for-data-driven-storytelling/); [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/narrative-design-patterns-for-data-driven-storytelling/)
- Bach et al. also published "Design patterns for data comics" (CHI 2018) with 29 patterns. [Academia.edu listing](https://www.academia.edu/53971109/Design_Patterns_for_Data_Comics)
- Hullman, Drucker, Henry Riche, Lee, Fisher and Adar, "A deeper understanding of sequence in narrative visualization" (InfoVis 2013) analysed 42 professional narrative visualizations, ranked transition types between views and found memory and subjective rating benefits for sequences that use parallelism (repeating structure across views) as a structural device. [IEEE VIS 2013 listing](https://ieeevis.org/year/2013/paper/infovis/deeper-understanding-sequence-narrative-visualization); [Hullman lab](https://www.hullmanlab.northwestern.edu/paper/2013/02/01/story-sequence.html)
- Hullman, Kosara and Lam, "Finding a clear path: structuring strategies for visualization sequences" (EuroVis 2017): people structure sequences hierarchically, grouping by shared properties (time period, measure, aggregation level, region) and then ordering within groups. A common region or aggregation level was judged a better grouping basis than a common time period or measure. [Eurographics digital library](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf13194)

Practitioner taxonomies and mechanics
- The Pudding (Russell Goldenberg), "How to implement scrollytelling": the pattern is scroll to trigger, where a text "step" tells a fixed or sticky graphic to move to a new state; the graphic stays fixed while text moves and snaps back into the flow as the section enters and exits, because the scrolly section "is not the entire story". It separates scrollytelling (monitors scroll) from scrolljacking (alters scroll), calling the latter "generally considered bad practice". [The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/process/how-to-implement-scrollytelling/)
- Bostock, "How to scroll": two schools. Snap to section (scroll advances in discrete steps), which he opposes as "indirect and detached" because it loses the scroll bar and keyboard controls. Position fixed screens with normal scrolling, which he favours; transitions can be position based or time based, and the reader can interrupt. "Making content visible by scrolling is almost always better than hiding it behind a click." Scrolling suits narrative because it is linear. Clicking a stepper "is a comparatively complex action that requires greater deliberation." Show a cue that more content exists below a full screen cover. [Bostock](https://bost.ocks.org/mike/scroll/)
- Archie Tse (NYT graphics) gave "Why we are doing fewer interactives" at Malofiej 2016. [Nieman Lab via search summary](https://niemanlab.org/?p=122600) A forum post attributes to it the lines "If you make a tooltip or rollover, assume no one will ever see it" and that 85% of visitors ignore such interactions, and the soundbite "readers just want to scroll". These are secondhand; I could not open the slides to confirm. [Future of Coding forum](https://linen.futureofcoding.org/t/22673047/i-recall-coming-across-some-opinions-against-interactivity-i); [slides on GitHub, not parsed](https://github.com/archietse/malofiej-2016/blob/master/tse-malofiej-2016-slides.pdf)
- The phrase "scroll is the new click" predates Tse: a 2012 Hacker News thread links a UX Movement article "Why scrolling is the new click". I found no source that attributes the exact phrase to Tse. [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=3448518)

### Inferences
Catalogue of scrollytelling patterns and how each survives as a still. "Still form" is my adaptation for a 12 column Figma page; the pattern names and definitions are cited above.

| Pattern (source) | What it does in motion | Survives as a still? | Still form on the 12 column grid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sticky graphic with stepping text (Pudding, Bostock "position fixed") | Graphic pinned, text steps change its state | Yes, well | Storyboard strip: 3 to 4 key states as small multiples (e.g. 3 x 4 columns) with the step text as captions under each. Or text plus one image template where the image is the final, fully annotated state |
| Graphic sequence (NZZ) | Graphic swapped per text block | Yes, best of all | Already a sequence of discrete frames. Stack them, or lay them side by side with parallel structure (same crop, same scale) per Hullman 2013 |
| Animated transition (NZZ) | One graphic morphs between states | Partially | Before and after pair, or first, middle and final state. The tween itself is lost; keep only states that carry meaning. Pudding's test applies: if the transition is the point (change over time, movement), the still must show both ends side by side |
| Pan and zoom (NZZ) | Camera moves across one large image | Yes | Overview plus detail: one full width image with numbered callouts, and 2 or 3 cropped detail panels below keyed to the numbers |
| Moviescroller (NZZ) | Scroll scrubs a video | Weakly | Filmstrip of 4 to 6 frames. Only worth it if each frame differs meaningfully |
| Show and play (NZZ) | Animation autoplays when in view | Weakly | Poster frame (the most telling frame) with a caption that states what happens |
| Snap to section / stepper / slideshow (Bostock, McKenna) | Discrete click or swipe steps | Yes | This is the carousel template. In a still, unroll the carousel into a visible row so no frame is hidden behind a click |
| Martini glass (Segel and Heer) | Author led stem, then free exploration | The stem yes, the bowl no | Run the stem as the narrative sections; the bowl becomes one dense overview image (the full system) at the end, which readers can explore with their eyes |
| Reverse martini glass | Overview first, then findings | Yes | Open with the full system image, then sections that each zoom into one part |
| Drill down story (Segel and Heer) | Reader picks which branch to open | Poorly | Choose the one branch that matters for the argument and lay it out; mention the others in a small index |

## What do practitioners say about designing the static or mobile fallback first?

### Takeaway
The clearest practitioner guidance is The Pudding's: decide per piece whether the scroll transition carries meaning; if it does not, stack the steps as a series of static charts, and starting from mobile forces the essentials. Tse's reported NYT position and Bostock's essay both push toward linear scroll with nothing hidden behind interaction. Nobody I found argues "final state image only" is enough when change itself is the story.

### Cited findings
- Starting with mobile forces you to strip the experience to essentials, with desktop as the enhanced version. Desktop first can surface bolder ideas but needs a plan for scaling down. [The Pudding, responsive scrollytelling](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/)
- Keep the scroll on mobile only when transitions are "truly meaningful, and not just something to make it pop", such as change over time or spatial movement. Otherwise stack into a series of static charts, specifically when each step reads well as a standalone chart, when the mobile version needs a different chart type, when animation would hurt performance or when deadlines favour static. [The Pudding, responsive scrollytelling](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/)
- Avoid steppers and swipe or tap approaches because they require clicks or override native scrolling. Replace hover with fixed text or annotations. Keep it short: content that feels fine on desktop can feel tiring on a phone. [The Pudding, responsive scrollytelling](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/)
- The original Pudding implementation post explicitly does not cover responsive or static fallback. [The Pudding, how to implement scrollytelling](https://pudding.cool/process/how-to-implement-scrollytelling/)
- Bostock does not address static fallback directly; his closest guidance is to keep standard keyboard controls and normal scrolling working. [Bostock](https://bost.ocks.org/mike/scroll/)
- Reported from Tse's 2016 talk (secondhand): assume nobody sees tooltips or rollovers, and readers just want to scroll. [Future of Coding forum](https://linen.futureofcoding.org/t/22673047/i-recall-coming-across-some-opinions-against-interactivity-i)
- Scrollytelling pieces are expensive to produce and tend to be used for complex analysis or investigations rather than breaking news (citing Seyser and Zeiller). [Scrolly2Reel, arXiv 2403.18111](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2403.18111)
- Slideshow and scrollytelling forms can be converted into one another, and scrollytelling articles are usually text centred with images and video supporting the narrative. [Survey of narrative visualization tools, arXiv 2206.12118](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.12118)

### Inferences
- "Each step reads well as a standalone chart" is the right test for this portfolio. Every key frame should carry its own caption and make sense alone, so the still layout is the mobile layout and motion becomes the enhancement later.
- Replace every would-be hover with a printed annotation on the image. Tse's reported finding and Pudding's mobile advice point the same way.
- Carousels in the template set hide frames behind a click, which is the stepper problem Bostock and Pudding describe. Where a sequence carries the argument, unroll it into a visible row of 2 or 3 images instead. Keep carousels for supplementary galleries.
- Because the scrolly section "is not the entire story" (Pudding), a sticky sequence in the final motion build should be a bounded section, which maps cleanly to one template block in the still.

## What layout principles from editorial design and multimedia learning support narrative, and what pacing rules follow?

### Takeaway
The best evidenced principles are Mayer's: put words next to the picture they describe (spatial contiguity), signal what matters, cut what does not and segment long material. NN/g eyetracking supports meaningful subheadings (the layer cake scan is the most effective) and warns that attention drops sharply after the first two screens. Editorial pacing advice (alternate dense and open spreads, use pull quotes as entry points) is consistent across sources but those sources are design guides, not studies.

### Cited findings

Multimedia learning (Mayer)
- Twelve principles: multimedia, coherence, signalling, redundancy, spatial contiguity, temporal contiguity, segmenting, pre-training, modality, voice, personalization and image. Spatial contiguity: place related images and text near each other. Signalling: use colour, contrast or cues to highlight the most important information. Coherence: cut extraneous material. Segmenting: break long lessons into shorter, learner paced parts. Pre-training: introduce key concepts before the main lesson. [Dartmouth summary](https://services.dartmouth.edu/TDClient/1806/Portal/KB/PrintArticle?ID=171655)
- Reported median effect sizes from a later Mayer review: coherence d = 0.70, signalling d = 0.46, redundancy d = 0.87, spatial contiguity d = 0.79, temporal contiguity d = 1.30, segmenting d = 0.70. The 2014 Cambridge Handbook chapter reports signalling supported in 24 of 28 tests, median d = 0.41, and coherence median 0.86. Figures differ by review. [Mayer 2020 PDF via search summary](https://ugc.futurelearn.com/uploads/files/7d/d6/7dd6188d-c343-4311-b064-ac98d2c95abc/Multimedia_Principles._R._E._Mayer__2020.pdf); [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://resolve-he.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/CD5B7AE1279A9AB81F8EEBB53DBEC86E)

Scanning and attention (NN/g)
- Four scanning patterns: F pattern (start of lines, top of page, when there are no subheadings), spotted (fixating words that stand out), layer cake (fixating headings and subheadings, then reading the section needed) and commitment (reading closely). Layer cake is "by far the most effective way in which users can scan pages" apart from reading. Most users "will read very little from a wall of text." Advice: meaningful subheadings that state the point, short sections, front load key information, style key terms. [NN/g text scanning patterns](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/)
- 2018 eyetracking (120 participants, over 130,000 fixations at 1920 x 1080): 57% of page viewing time above the fold, 74% in the first two screenfuls (to 2160px). Over 42% of time in the top 20% of the page and over 65% in the top 40%. In 2010 the above the fold share was 80%. NN/g cautions this may partly reflect short pages or users giving up. [NN/g scrolling and attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)

Sequence structure
- Parallel structure across a sequence of views improves memory and subjective ratings. [Hullman et al. 2013](https://ieeevis.org/year/2013/paper/infovis/deeper-understanding-sequence-narrative-visualization)
- Readers and authors group views hierarchically by shared properties, then order within groups, and viewers' sense of a sequence's quality largely matches the creators'. [Hullman, Kosara and Lam 2017](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf13194)

Editorial pacing (practice guides, not studies)
- Pull quotes break up long body copy and act as entry points; ideally the pull quote sits on the same page as its source text. [WOU magazine layout, pull quotes](https://people.wou.edu/~visuanod/magazine_layout/pullquote.html); [Publitas magazine layout](https://www.publitas.com/blog/how-to-design-a-magazine-layout/)
- If every spread is equally dense the reader fatigues; if every spread is airy it feels thin. Alternate information heavy pages with visually open spreads. Keep body type quiet so accents stand out. Squint test: the order elements emerge from blur is the order readers see them. [Made Good Designs, magazine design principles](https://madegooddesigns.com/magazine-design-principles/); [Made Good Designs, editorial design guide](https://madegooddesigns.com/editorial-design-guide/)
- Spread level decisions include pull quote placement, image placement, title width and varying image number and size. [Capilano editorial lecture](https://eportfolios.capilanou.ca/judysnaydon/wp-content/uploads/sites/7204/2018/10/360_18-Editorial-Lecture-1.pdf)

### Inferences
Catalogue of layout patterns that carry a story, the reader job each serves and its grounding. Template mapping is my adaptation to the existing Figma sections.

| Layout pattern | Reader job | Grounding | Template and grid use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Title block with a one line claim | Decide whether to read on; get the point before the detail | NN/g layer cake and front loading; Mayer pre-training | Title block. Make the subhead state the finding, not the topic |
| Text plus one image, side by side | Read an explanation while looking at what it explains | Mayer spatial contiguity (d about 0.79) | Text plus one image. Text 4 to 5 columns, image 7 to 8. Caption beside, not below the fold |
| Sticky graphic with stepping text, as storyboard | Follow how one thing changes across steps | Pudding scroll to trigger; Hullman parallelism | Text plus three images: same crop and scale per frame, step text as caption under each |
| Small multiples as a sequence | Compare states at a glance, see the pattern of change | Hullman 2013 parallelism; Pudding "stack into static charts" | Text plus two or three images in equal 4 or 6 column cells. Group by one shared property (Hullman 2017) |
| Before and after | See what the work changed | Animated transition reduced to its end states (NZZ, Pudding) | Text plus two images, identical framing, labels "before" and "after" in the same position |
| Annotated screenshot | Know where to look in a dense interface | Mayer signalling and coherence; Tse's reported "nobody sees tooltips" | Full width or 8 column image with numbered callouts printed on it, notes keyed alongside |
| Step sequence (process) | Understand order and dependency | Segel and Heer slideshow; Mayer segmenting | Numbered row of frames, or a vertical stack of text plus one image blocks with a step index |
| Overview then detail (pan and zoom as still) | Grasp the whole system, then its parts | NZZ pan and zoom; reverse martini glass | One full bleed overview, then 2 or 3 detail crops keyed by number |
| Martini glass | Get the authored argument, then explore | Segel and Heer | Narrative sections first, a single dense "whole system" image near the end |
| Full bleed moment | Mark a turn in the story; rest the eye; show scale | Editorial alternation of dense and open spreads | Span all 12 columns (or edge to edge). Ration to the beats where the image is itself the evidence |
| Pull quote or big number | Re-enter a long section; anchor one fact in memory | Editorial entry points; NN/g spotted scanning; Mayer signalling | Inside or after a long text block, 6 to 8 columns, on the same screen as its source text |
| Stepper or slideshow (carousel) | Browse optional extras | Bostock and Pudding: clicks are costly and get skipped | Carousel template, only for material the argument does not depend on |

Pacing rules derived from the above
- Front load. With about three quarters of attention in the first two screens (NN/g), the title block plus the first visual should carry the whole claim of the case study.
- Alternate dense and light. After a text heavy section or a dense annotated screenshot, follow with an open section: a full bleed image, a big number or a pull quote (editorial alternation guidance).
- A full width visual earns its space when it is the evidence for the beat, when scale or wholeness is the point (a system map) or when it marks a turn between acts. It does not earn it as decoration between two text blocks. This is inference from Mayer's coherence principle plus editorial pacing guidance.
- Use a pull quote or big number where a section runs past roughly one screen of uninterrupted text, placed within the same screen as the text it pulls from. The "one screen" threshold is my inference, not a sourced number.
- Keep framing identical across any sequence of frames so the eye reads the change, not the layout (Hullman parallelism).

### Gaps
- Z pattern and Gestalt grouping guidance for editorial layouts was not sourced in this session. NN/g has relevant material on Gestalt proximity and on the F pattern for web pages, but I did not fetch it, so no claims are made here.
- No source in this session gives an evidence based text to image ratio for long narrative pages. Any ratio would be invented.
- Editorial pacing sources are practice guides and course materials, not studies.

## What evidence exists on reader comprehension or engagement for these patterns?

### Takeaway
Evidence is thin and mostly about engagement, not comprehension. McKenna et al. (240 participants) found visuals and navigation feedback such as animated versus static transitions affect engagement, while discrete versus continuous control may not, and a secondhand report says steppers and scrollers both beat text with static visuals with no difference between them. A 2026 study of 25 designers found interactive onboarding (scrollytelling or chatbot) beat static on engagement and guideline adherence but not on comprehension. Mayer's principles carry the strongest comprehension evidence and they apply fully to stills.

### Cited findings
- McKenna et al. 2017: two exploratory studies of stepper and scroller flows, then a crowdsourced study with 240 participants. Visuals and navigation feedback (static versus animated transitions) affect engagement; level of control (discrete versus continuous) may not. [Microsoft Research abstract](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/visual-narrative-flow-exploring-factors-shaping-data-visualization-story-reading-experiences/)
- Kosara's conference report on the same paper: engagement "was higher for steppers and scrollers" than text plus static visuals, with no difference between stepper and scroller. Secondhand summary. [eagereyes, EuroVis 2017 report](https://eagereyes.org/blog/2017/eurovis-2017-thursday)
- Chen, Shete, Anavekar, Peiris and Kong (arXiv 2607.03023, July 2026), 25 UX designers and students: interactive techniques (scrollytelling and chatbot pooled) scored significantly higher than static on guideline adherence and instruction clarity and were preferred, with higher engagement; comprehension did not differ significantly; scrollytelling and chatbot did not differ. Preprint, small sample, numbers not in the abstract. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.03023)
- Parallel structure in a sequence of static views improves memory and subjective ratings. [Hullman et al. 2013](https://ieeevis.org/year/2013/paper/infovis/deeper-understanding-sequence-narrative-visualization)
- Mayer's contiguity, signalling, coherence and segmenting principles have replicated learning effects in the d = 0.4 to 0.8 range. [Mayer 2020 PDF](https://ugc.futurelearn.com/uploads/files/7d/d6/7dd6188d-c343-4311-b064-ac98d2c95abc/Multimedia_Principles._R._E._Mayer__2020.pdf); [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://resolve-he.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/CD5B7AE1279A9AB81F8EEBB53DBEC86E)
- General reading attention, not specific to scrollytelling: Chartbeat found 35% of desktop users leave before scrolling, and a Marfeel analysis reported 75% of readers never pass 10% scroll depth (both as reported in a search summary). [Chartbeat blog](https://blog.chartbeat.com/?p=3927)
- Tse's reported claim that about 85% of visitors ignore interactive elements is secondhand and unverified. [Future of Coding forum](https://linen.futureofcoding.org/t/22673047/i-recall-coming-across-some-opinions-against-interactivity-i)

### Inferences
- Engagement gains from scrolly formats appear to come from the visuals and transitions, not from the mechanism of control. For a still first portfolio, that means the stills themselves (clear frames, parallel framing, printed annotations) carry most of the value, and motion later adds engagement rather than comprehension.
- Comprehension evidence (Mayer) is about adjacency, signalling and cutting, all of which work identically on a static page. So a still that obeys contiguity and signalling is not a degraded version of the motion piece for understanding.
- A portfolio reviewer is a time poor skimmer, closer to NN/g's layer cake scanner than a committed reader. Headings that state findings and captions that stand alone serve that reader directly.

- Seyser and Zeiller 2018 and Oesch et al. 2022 full texts were not readable in this session (closed access or blocked), so their detailed categories and any reader findings are not captured.
- The "Zhang et al. scrollytelling patterns" paper named in the brief could not be located. The closest items are Lu et al. 2021 (automatic unit visualization scrollytelling, with Xiaolong Luke Zhang as a co-author, a system paper rather than a pattern study) [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/automatic-generation-of-unit-visualization-based-scrollytelling-f/) and Oesch et al. 2022.
- McKenna et al.'s exact numbers and the values of each flow factor (for example the story layout options) were not extracted because the PDF did not parse. [Paper PDF](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~miriah/publications/narrative-flow.pdf)
- Tse's 2016 slides were not readable; quotes attributed to him are secondhand.
