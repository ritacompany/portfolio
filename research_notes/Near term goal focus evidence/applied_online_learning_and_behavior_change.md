# Applied evidence: pacing, weekly structure and persistence toward long-term goals (adult online learning and digital behavior change)

Product context these notes serve: a career-change product for adults whose weeks vary. It gives a weekly set of tasks due at the end of the week, a pace signal for the current stage, and a separate overview page for the multi-month journey.

Verification legend: [V-abstract] = checked against the abstract or publisher/repository record. [V-secondary] = checked only against a secondary summary. [UNVERIFIED] = from background knowledge, not confirmed in this session; the writer should check it before citing.

## Q1. MOOC and online course completion: base rates and what scaled interventions show

### Takeaway
Completion of open, self-paced online courses is low and did not improve over six years (about 3% of all edX registrants at MIT/Harvard; Jordan's median was 12.6% across 129 courses). Light-touch planning and scheduling nudges that looked promising in small studies shrink by an order of magnitude at scale: they lift early engagement but not final completion, and one scheduling nudge slightly harmed persistence.

### Cited Findings
- **Jordan (2015)**, "Massive open online course completion rates revisited: Assessment, length and attrition," *IRRODL* 16(3), 341–358, doi 10.19173/irrodl.v16i3.2112. Data on 221 MOOCs, with completion data available for 129. Completion ranged from 0.7% to 52.1% (median 12.6%). Longer courses had significantly lower completion. Auto-graded-only courses completed more. In the courses with activity data, the first two weeks were critical: after that, active-learner curves levelled off. [V-abstract] — [IRRODL](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/2112); [ORO record](https://oro.open.ac.uk/43566)
- **Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente (2019)**, "The MOOC pivot," *Science* 363(6423), 130–131, doi 10.1126/science.aav7958. 5.63M learners and 12.67M registrations in 565 MITx/HarvardX courses, 2012 to May 2018. Low completion "has not improved over 6 years." Completion fell from 2013–14 to 2017–18 for all learners, for those who intended to complete, and for verified (paid) learners. About 3.13% of all participants completed (secondary report). [V-abstract + V-secondary] — [MIT TSL](https://tsl.mit.edu/research/the-mooc-pivot/); [Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/node/7744); [Replication repo](https://github.com/jruiperezv/MOOC_Pivot)
  - [UNVERIFIED] Commonly cited detail from the same paper: completion among verified-track learners was about 46% in 2017–18, and fewer than 1 in 5 of those who intended to complete did so. Check against the paper.
- **Kizilcec, Reich, Yeomans, et al. (2020)**, "Scaling up behavioral science interventions in online education," *PNAS* 117(26), 14900–14905, doi 10.1073/pnas.1921417117. About 250,000 students in 247 HarvardX/MITx/Stanford courses over 2.5 years, with preregistered iterative waves. Self-regulation interventions (including plan-making) raised engagement in the first few weeks but **not final completion**. Value-relevance interventions raised completion for learners in developing countries, but only in courses that had a global achievement gap. Effects were "an order of magnitude smaller" than prior small-scale studies predicted. ML could not reliably target who would benefit. [V-abstract] — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7334459); [Cornell news](https://news.cornell.edu/node/319503); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/06/200615152116.htm)
- **Baker, Evans & Dee (2016)**, "A randomized experiment testing the efficacy of a scheduling nudge in a MOOC," *AERA Open* 2(4). N = 18,043. An emailed prompt with a link to a scheduling tool, asking students to schedule when they would watch lectures, had **no effect on near-term engagement** and **weakly significant negative effects** on longer-term engagement, persistence and performance. The harm was concentrated among students who registered close to the course start and among students with .edu addresses. The authors suggest that theory-grounded nudges can interact with varied motivations in unintended ways. [V-abstract] — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/003c9cb555344521b6aefb5c20fffa47); [Data/analysis files](https://www.openicpsr.org/openicpsr/project/107221)

### Inferences
- Attrition is front-loaded (Jordan's first two weeks). The first one or two weekly sets in a career-change product are where structure matters most.
- A one-off, self-directed "plan your schedule" prompt is not reliably helpful and can backfire. It is weak evidence for designs whose whole structure is a single upfront plan.
- Early engagement gains that fade (Kizilcec 2020) suggest that a single intervention at onboarding is not enough. Structure has to recur, as a weekly cadence does.
- Base rates come from free, open MOOCs with very mixed intent. Paid, goal-committed adults (bootcamps, career changers) complete more, so do not transpose the 3–13% figures directly.

### Gaps
- No large RCT was found that directly compared "weekly task set due end of week" against "self-paced long-horizon plan" in adult career-change or bootcamp products.
- Bootcamp completion data is mostly vendor-reported (for example, CIRR reports). No peer-reviewed source was retrieved this session.

## Q2. Self-paced versus instructor-paced/cohort courses; weekly deadlines

### Takeaway
Instructor-paced runs with deadlines tend to show higher completion than self-paced runs of the same course, but the gap is modest, varies by learner, and is sometimes absent. The evidence is mostly observational or single-course. The classic lab evidence that external, evenly spaced deadlines beat self-set ones (Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002) failed a 2026 replication.

### Cited Findings
- **MIT supply chain MOOC (thesis, Bilal)**: switching from instructor-paced to self-paced lowered completion by about 6 percentage points and pass rate by about 10%, with satisfaction unchanged. Effects were uneven: some learners showed no change and others a steep fall. [V-secondary; thesis] — [DSpace@MIT](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/145224); related: [Learning analytics case study on course pacing](https://dspace.mit.edu/entities/publication/1746a463-1bfe-4fb1-bdf7-2b6c0dd3ea0c)
- **MIT Digital Learning Lab analysis**: about 88,000 learners across 16 edX course runs. Self-paced mode did not raise engagement for all learners. The best mode depends on course goals and learner population. [V-secondary] — [MIT Open Learning xTalk summary](https://openlearning.mit.edu/news/3-things-you-missed-if-you-missed-dll-xtalk-curricular-innovations-moocs)
- **Landscape ecology MOOC (USGS)**: instructor-paced runs reached 14% (2018) and 11% (2019) completion against 8–9% for the 2020 self-paced run. Single course, different years, not controlled. [V-secondary] — [USGS pubs](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70229066)
- **Ihantola et al.** (as cited in an Italian paper): retention was 45% instructor-paced against 13% self-paced. [V-secondary; original not checked] — [Publicatt](https://publicatt.unicatt.it/handle/10807/258473)
- **Open Praxis (2019), two open online courses (Griffith)**: certificates, quiz completion and marks were **not significantly different** between tutor-led and self-paced versions. [V-abstract] — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/f64c16aa94b049869ba8b2e0a7d764b2); [Griffith repository](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/items/7b87aa2c-ec48-430d-8313-682f3dcf87f3)
- **Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002)**, "Procrastination, deadlines, and performance: Self-control by precommitment," *Psychological Science* 13(3), 219–224. Students self-imposed costly deadlines, and these helped, but less than externally imposed, evenly spaced deadlines. [V-abstract] — [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/810365)
  - **Contested:** Hyndman & Bisin's replication of Study 2 (proofreading) found that deadline conditions had a negligible effect on performance. Evenly spaced, externally imposed deadlines did not stand out. This is reported as published in *Psychological Science* in 2026. The working paper was verified; the journal page returned 403, so the final publication details are [V-secondary]. — [Replication working paper (NYU)](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/wp.nyu.edu/dist/c/16384/files/2025/09/Ariely_Replication-1.pdf); [Psychological Science DOI page](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09567976261460772)

### Inferences
- Moderate support for a regular external cadence (deadlines or weekly checkpoints) over fully open-ended pacing, especially for less self-regulated learners. It is not a universal win, and some learners do no worse when self-paced.
- The evidence supports giving the product's weekly "due end of week" a firm rhythm, but the failed replication argues against claiming that rigid evenly spaced deadlines are proven optimal. A weekly window (rather than daily deadlines) is a reasonable middle ground for adults with variable weeks. This is an inference; no study was found that tested week-length windows against daily ones.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found isolating "weekly deadline windows" from other cohort features (peers, instructor presence, synchronous starts). Cohort benefits may come from social accountability rather than pacing itself.
- [UNVERIFIED] Some edX/Coursera internal reports suggest session-based courses with deadlines complete more than on-demand ones. No primary source was retrieved.

## Q3. Self-regulated learning: proximal subgoals, planning, monitoring

### Takeaway
Correlationally, time management, effort regulation, metacognition, goal setting and strategic planning are the SRL strategies most associated with achievement in online settings. The associations are small and weaker than in face-to-face settings. Zimmerman's model treats proximal, specific subgoals plus self-monitoring as the core of forethought and performance control.

### Cited Findings
- **Broadbent & Poon (2015)**, "Self-regulated learning strategies & academic achievement in online higher education learning environments: A systematic review," *Internet and Higher Education* 27, 1–13, doi 10.1016/j.iheduc.2015.04.007. 12 studies from 2004–2014. **Time management, metacognition, effort regulation and critical thinking** were positively correlated with online achievement. Rehearsal, elaboration and organisation had the least support. Peer learning showed a moderate effect, but its CI crossed zero. Effects online appear **weaker** than face to face. Note: the request calls this a meta-analysis; it is a systematic review that pooled correlations. [V-abstract] — [Deakin repository](https://dro.deakin.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Self-regulated_learning_strategies_academic_achievement_in_online_higher_education_learning_environments_a_systematic_review/20907691); [Galway summary](https://openpress.universityofgalway.ie/onlinelearningtoolbox/?p=33)
  - [UNVERIFIED] Commonly cited pooled r values: time management about .14, effort regulation about .11, metacognition about .06, critical thinking about .15. Check the full text, Table 3.
- **Kizilcec, Pérez-Sanagustín & Maldonado (2017)**, "Self-regulated learning strategies predict learner behavior and goal attainment in MOOCs," *Computers & Education* 104, 18–33, doi 10.1016/j.compedu.2016.10.001. 4,831 learners in 6 Coursera MOOCs, with self-report linked to logs. **Goal setting and strategic planning** predicted attainment of personal course goals. Working professionals reported more goal setting and planning than students. All groups reported low self-evaluation. Correlational. [V-abstract + V-secondary] — [UC Chile repository](https://repositorio.uc.cl/dspace/items/8b9d2488-921e-4be3-b4ee-4e85b816445f/full); [JIME review](https://jime.open.ac.uk/articles/527)
- **Zimmerman (2002)**, "Becoming a self-regulated learner: An overview," *Theory Into Practice* 41(2), 64–70. Cyclical forethought, performance and self-reflection phases. Goal setting with specific, proximal goals, strategic planning and self-monitoring sit in the forethought and performance phases. [UNVERIFIED in session; standard citation]
- **Bandura & Schunk (1981)**, "Cultivating competence, self-efficacy, and intrinsic interest through proximal self-motivation," *JPSP* 41(3), 586–598. Children (N = 40) with proximal (per-session) subgoals progressed faster and gained more self-efficacy and interest than those with only a distal goal. This is the classic origin of the "proximal subgoals" claim. Small, child, lab-classroom sample. [UNVERIFIED in session]
- Related meta-evidence: a 2025 meta-analysis of SRL in online and blended learning reports an overall SRL–performance effect of about .14 (K-12 and higher education). [V-secondary; title not retrieved] — [via search summary, Frontiers/DOAJ 2025](https://doaj.org/article/ee348b3f858e4ea89bc0d83d6874d4b7)

### Inferences
- A weekly task set is effectively an externally supplied proximal subgoal plus a planning scaffold. This substitutes for the planning and time-management skills that predict success but that many adults lack. It is the logic Zimmerman and Bandura & Schunk support.
- Low self-evaluation across groups (Kizilcec 2017) argues for a built-in review step: a weekly look-back or the pace signal.

### Gaps
- Most SRL–achievement evidence is correlational self-report. The causal evidence that SRL scaffolds raise completion at scale is weak (see Kizilcec 2020).

## Q4. Adult learners' time constraints and dropout

### Takeaway
For adult online learners, dropout is driven less by individual characteristics than by external factors (family and organizational support, time and work conflicts) and by how relevant and satisfying the course feels. A structure that tolerates variable weeks and keeps relevance visible addresses the main levers.

### Cited Findings
- **Park & Choi (2009)**, "Factors influencing adult learners' decision to drop out or persist in online learning," *Educational Technology & Society* 12(4), 207–217. Revised dropout model for adult, non-degree online learners. [V-secondary for citation] — [Encyclopedia.pub (Park & Choi entry)](https://www.encyclopedia.pub/entry/59363); [OLJ article describing the model](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/download/935/313)
  - [UNVERIFIED in session, widely cited] Sample of 147 adult learners. Dropouts and persisters did **not** differ on individual characteristics (age, gender, education). They differed on **family and organizational support** and on **satisfaction and relevance**: lack of support and low relevance predicted dropout.
- **Lee & Choi (2011)**, "A review of online course dropout research: Implications for practice and future research," *Educational Technology Research and Development* 59(5), 593–618. Review of 35 empirical studies, identifying 69 dropout factors in three groups: student factors (academic background, relevant experience, skills, psychological attributes), course/program factors, and environmental factors (work commitments, family and life circumstances, supportive environment). [V-secondary for counts] — [ResearchGate PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Youngju_Lee/publication/227246914_A_review_of_online_course_dropout_research_Implications_for_practice_and_future_research/links/0f317530de159c4d98000000/A-review-of-online-course-dropout-research-Implications-for-practice-and-future-research.pdf)
- **Choi & Park (2018)**, path-analytic model of adult dropout in online degree programs, *Computers & Education*. Examined scholastic aptitude, **physical constraints** (time and work), interaction with content, satisfaction and GPA. [V-secondary] — [LearnTechLib](https://learntechlib.org/p/200738)
- A test of Park's model in asynchronous online courses found age, employment status and satisfaction were significant predictors. [V-secondary; student-authored page, low reliability] — [JSUMS](https://www.jsums.edu/ephd/?p=1144)

### Inferences
- Variable weeks (work, family) are a documented dropout driver. A weekly window, instead of daily deadlines, gives within-week flexibility while keeping a near-term checkpoint. The design should also anticipate weeks with near-zero capacity, for example a "light week" mode, so that one bad week does not read as failure (see the streak findings in Q5).
- Relevance and satisfaction predict persistence. Tying each week's tasks visibly to the career goal matters, which supports keeping the overview reachable but not dominant.

### Gaps
- The Park & Choi sample and effect details were not confirmed from the full text in this session.

## Q5. Digital behavior change: goal setting, action planning, progress monitoring, weekly targets, streaks

### Takeaway
Progress monitoring (d+ = 0.40) and goal setting for physical activity (d = 0.55) have solid meta-analytic support. Monitoring works better when it is recorded and reported. Streak mechanics raise repetition while a streak is intact, but a broken streak lowers re-engagement, especially when users blame themselves. Weekly targets can be set without a streak framing.

### Cited Findings
- **Harkin, Webb, Chang, Prestwich, Conner, Kellar, Benn & Sheeran (2016)**, "Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence," *Psychological Bulletin* 142(2), 198–229. 138 RCTs, N = 19,951. Interventions increased monitoring frequency (d+ = 1.98) and goal attainment (**d+ = 0.40**, 95% CI 0.32–0.48). Increased monitoring mediated the effect. Effects were larger when progress was **reported or made public** and when it was **physically recorded**. [V-abstract] — [White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/)
  - Secondary report: a bias-adjusted estimate of about d = 0.19, and subgroup values (0.47 reported private, 0.19 unreported private, 0.55 public with k = 3). [V-secondary only; verify] — [Silicon Canals summary](https://siliconcanals.com/t-progress-monitoring-recorded-public-goal-achievement/)
- **McEwan, Harden, Zumbo, et al. (2016)**, "The effectiveness of multi-component goal setting interventions for changing physical activity behaviour: a systematic review and meta-analysis," *Health Psychology Review* 10(1), 67–88, doi 10.1080/17437199.2015.1104258. 45 articles, 52 interventions, n = 5,912. Medium effect, **d = 0.552** (95% CI 0.43–0.67). 20 moderators were tested, including goal content and added BCTs. [V-abstract] — [Bath research portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-multi-component-goal-setting-interventions-f/); [Full text PDF](https://eclass.uth.gr/modules/document/file.php/PE_P_160/2022%20goal%20setting%2C%20imagery%2C%20attention%20Yannis%20Theodorakis/2020%20The%20effectiveness%20of%20multi%20component%20goal%20setting%20interventions%20for%20changing%20physical%20activity%20behaviour%20a%20systematic%20review%20and%20meta%20analysis.pdf)
- **Michie, Richardson, Johnston, et al. (2013)**, "The Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy (v1) of 93 hierarchically clustered techniques," *Annals of Behavioral Medicine* 46(1), 81–95, doi 10.1007/s12160-013-9486-6. Relevant BCTs: 1.1 Goal setting (behavior), 1.3 Goal setting (outcome), 1.4 Action planning, 1.5 Review behavior goal(s), 1.6 Discrepancy between current behavior and goal, 2.2 Feedback on behavior, 2.3 Self-monitoring of behavior. The product's weekly task set maps to 1.1 and 1.4. The pace signal maps to 1.6 and 2.2. The weekly reset maps to 1.5. [UNVERIFIED in session; standard citation, numbering from memory]
- **Silverman & Barasch (2023)**, "On or off track: How (broken) streaks affect consumer decisions," *Journal of Consumer Research* 49(6), 1095–1117, doi 10.1093/jcr/ucac029. Seven studies. People are more likely to repeat a behavior that extends an intact streak, even with past behavior held constant, because keeping the streak becomes a goal in itself. **After a break, engagement drops.** The effect is stronger when people blame themselves, and it weakens when a "repair" option exists. Barasch notes that "get back on track" reminders typically do not work, and suggests offering a new goal. [V-abstract + interview] — [UDel repository](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb); [CU Boulder Q&A](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions)

### Inferences
- Progress monitoring is among the best-supported ingredients. A per-stage pace signal fits it, and Harkin's moderators suggest making progress visible and recorded (logged completions), not just implied.
- A weekly reset naturally offers a "new goal" after a missed week, which is the remedy Barasch suggests. That favors weekly sets over day-count streaks for adults with variable weeks. Avoid copy that frames a missed week as a broken chain or assigns self-blame.
- Pace signals should frame discrepancy (BCT 1.6) without shaming. This is inference from the streak and self-blame moderator, not tested directly.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head trial of weekly versus daily goal granularity was retrieved this session. [UNVERIFIED] Some physical-activity trials using weekly minute targets (for example, 150 min/week framings) and the Milkman et al. (2021, *Nature*) gym megastudy exist but were not checked here.
- The bias-adjusted Harkin estimate needs checking against the paper.

## Q6. Distant identity goals need near-term pathways (possible selves) and the positive-fantasy caveat (Oettingen)

### Takeaway
A distant identity goal ("future me in a new career") motivates action only when it is linked to concrete near-term strategies. Positive fantasizing about the end state, without expectations or obstacles, predicts **less** effort and worse outcomes, including fewer job offers among graduates. Mental contrasting with implementation intentions (WOOP) has a small-to-medium meta-analytic effect. This supports keeping the multi-month vision on a separate overview, connected to this week's actions, rather than leading with an aspirational end-state.

### Cited Findings
- **Oyserman, Bybee & Terry (2006)**, "Possible selves and academic outcomes: How and when possible selves impel action," *JPSP* 91(1), 188–204. 141 experimental and 123 control low-income 8th graders. A brief intervention **linking academic possible selves to concrete strategies** improved grades, test scores and initiative, and reduced absences and misbehavior over 2 years. Effects were mediated by change in possible selves. The population is adolescents, not adults. [V-abstract] — [UMich Deep Blue PDF](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/2027.42/89942/1/oyserman__bybee___terry_2006.pdf)
- **Oyserman, Terry & Bybee (2002)**, *Journal of Adolescence*: earlier evaluation of the 9-week after-school program, 62 experimental and 146 control. Gains in school bonding, plausible strategies and attendance. [V-abstract] — [UMich Deep Blue PDF](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/2027.42/89945/1/oyserman__terry__bybee__2002.pdf); see also [Possible selves as roadmaps (Oyserman et al. 2004)](https://dornsife.usc.edu/daphna-oyserman/wp-content/uploads/sites/232/2023/11/possible_selves_as_roadmaps.pdf)
- **Oettingen & Mayer (2002)**, "The motivating function of thinking about the future: Expectations versus fantasies," *JPSP* 83(5), 1198–1212. Four studies: job-seeking graduates, students with a crush, exam takers, hip-replacement patients. Follow-ups ran up to 2 years. **Positive expectations predicted effort and success. Positive fantasies predicted the reverse.** In Study 1, graduates who fantasized more positively sent fewer applications, received fewer offers and earned lower salaries (n of about 83 is from a secondary summary). [V-abstract; job-outcome details V-secondary] — [Oettingen & Mayer PDF (Uni Hamburg)](https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/paedagogische-psychologie-und-motivation/personen/oettingen-gabriele/dokumente/oettingen-mayer-2002.pdf); [Prospective Psychology summary](https://prospectivepsych.org/node/60)
- **Wang, Wang & Gai (2021)**, "A meta-analysis of the effects of mental contrasting with implementation intentions on goal attainment," *Frontiers in Psychology* 12:565202. 21 studies, 24 effect sizes, N = 15,907. MCII **g = 0.336**. Effects were larger with direct experimenter interaction (g = 0.465) than with document-only delivery (g = 0.277), which is relevant to app delivery. Possible publication bias means true effects may be smaller. [V-abstract] — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8149892/)
- Cross & Sheffield (2019), as cited in Wang et al., report mental contrasting effects on adult physical-health outcomes: g = 0.28 at up to 4 weeks and g = 0.38 at up to 3 months. [V-secondary] — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/full)
- Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38: implementation intentions meta-analysis, 94 studies, d = 0.65. [V-secondary; verify; likely inflated by publication bias in later re-analyses]

### Inferences
- For career changers, a vivid "dream job" overview could act as positive fantasy if it is not tied to weekly actions and realistic expectations. Keeping the journey on a separate page, with the weekly view as the primary surface, is consistent with this evidence. The overview should show the path, stages and obstacles, not just an aspirational end state.
- Possible-selves evidence argues the two views must be **linked**: each week's tasks should visibly roll up to the identity goal. Separation of pages is fine; disconnection is not.
- MCII effects shrink in document- or app-only formats, so expect small effects from in-app WOOP-style prompts.

### Gaps
- Possible-selves RCTs are in adolescents. No adult career-transition RCT was retrieved.
- No study was found that tests UI placement (overview on the main screen against a separate page) on persistence.

## Cross-cutting: robustness and contested findings

### Takeaway
The robust findings are low baseline completion in open courses, the benefit of progress monitoring, goal setting in physical activity, and small but positive MCII effects. The contested or fragile findings are scaled nudge effects (order-of-magnitude shrinkage), the superiority of external, evenly spaced deadlines (failed replication), self-paced against instructor-paced effects (mixed and context-dependent), and SRL–achievement links (small and correlational).

### Cited Findings
- Scaled interventions are an order of magnitude smaller than lab or small-trial effects. — [Kizilcec et al. 2020, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7334459)
- A scheduling nudge can backfire. — [Baker, Evans & Dee 2016](https://doaj.org/article/003c9cb555344521b6aefb5c20fffa47)
- Deadline result not replicated. — [Hyndman & Bisin replication](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/wp.nyu.edu/dist/c/16384/files/2025/09/Ariely_Replication-1.pdf)
- Monitoring d+ = 0.40 across 138 RCTs, with a possible bias-adjusted figure near 0.19 (verify). — [Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/)
- Pacing effects are heterogeneous by learner. — [MIT DSpace thesis](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/145224); [Open Praxis 2019](https://doaj.org/article/f64c16aa94b049869ba8b2e0a7d764b2)

### Inferences
- The strongest defensible claim for the product: recurring near-term goals plus visible, recorded progress monitoring, linked to a distant identity goal, is better supported than a self-paced long-horizon plan alone. Expect modest effect sizes, and expect heterogeneity across users.
- Weak or contested: any claim that a specific deadline cadence (weekly against other cadences) is optimal.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence was found for career-change products specifically. Everything here is transferred from MOOCs, higher education, health behavior, or adolescent possible-selves work.
