# Goal proximity and goal-setting: weekly targets versus a distant multi-month goal

Scope: evidence on proximal (near-term) versus distal (long-term) goals, for a career-change product whose working surface is a weekly task set due by end of week, while the multi-month journey sits on a separate overview page.

Verification legend used below:
- **[Full text read]**: I read the paper's own text (PDF text layer).
- **[Abstract verified]**: I read the publisher abstract, via OpenAlex, PubMed or the author's repository.
- **[Secondary]**: only seen described in another peer-reviewed paper.
- **[Unverified]**: from memory or a non-primary snippet. Do not cite without checking.

## 1. Bandura & Schunk (1981) and follow-ups: proximal vs distal goals, self-efficacy, interest, performance

### Takeaway
In the founding study, children who set session-by-session subgoals gained more mastery, self-efficacy and free-choice interest in arithmetic than children with only a distal goal. The distal goal did no better than having no goal. Follow-ups (Stock & Cervone 1990; Latham & Seijts 1999) back the self-efficacy pathway. The interest result is contested (Manderlink & Harackiewicz 1984), and every study in this set is small: about 70 or fewer per condition.

### Cited Findings
**Bandura, A., & Schunk, D. H. (1981). Cultivating competence, self-efficacy, and intrinsic interest through proximal self-motivation. *JPSP, 41*(3), 586–598.** [Full text read] — [PDF](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/59faaf5b01b9500001e95457/5bc552d85141987915dab842_Bandura%20&%20Schunk,%201981.pdf)
- **Sample:** 40 children aged 7.3–10.1 (mean 8.4) from six elementary schools. Teachers screened them for "gross deficits" in arithmetic and low interest in it. There were 21 boys and 19 girls, spread evenly across conditions (about 10 per group).
- **Design:** seven 30-minute sessions of self-directed subtraction learning, using 42 pages and 258 problems.
  - Proximal condition: the experimenter suggested a goal of at least 6 pages per session.
  - Distal condition: the experimenter suggested finishing all 42 pages by the end of session 7.
  - There was also a no-goal condition and an untreated control.
  - Goals were "mentioned suggestively rather than prescriptively," so the children chose whether to adopt them.
- **Results (abstract and text):**
  - Under proximal subgoals, children "progressed rapidly," "achieved substantial mastery," and developed self-efficacy and intrinsic interest.
  - "Distal goals had no demonstrable effects."
  - Goal proximity also produced more accurate self-knowledge: self-efficacy judgments closely matched later performance.
- **Intrinsic interest** was measured as the number of subtraction problems chosen in a free-choice period, with a digit-symbol task as the competing activity.
  - Treatment effect: F(3,36) = 3.57, p < .05.
  - The proximal group exceeded all three other groups, which did not differ from each other.
  - About 90% of proximal children chose to do subtraction problems, against about 40% in the other groups.
  - The groups did not differ on the competing activity (F = 0.77).
- **Persistence:** all three treatment conditions persisted significantly more than controls but did not differ from each other. Persistence was therefore not specifically a proximal-goal effect.
- **Self-efficacy and outcomes:** self-efficacy predicted perfect arithmetic accuracy (r(38) = .49, p < .001). Standardized achievement test scores did not predict rate of skill acquisition or gains in self-efficacy.

**Stock, J., & Cervone, D. (1990). Proximal goal-setting and self-regulatory processes. *Cognitive Therapy and Research, 14*(5), 483–498.** Abstract seen only in a search snippet tied to [DOI 10.1007/BF01172969](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01172969); volume and pages confirmed via OpenAlex. Not independently read. [Secondary/abstract snippet]
- **Design:** a complex problem-solving task under three conditions: no subgoal, an attainable subgoal, or an unattainable subgoal.
- **Results:**
  - Setting a subgoal raised initial self-efficacy.
  - Attaining the subgoal raised self-efficacy, satisfaction with performance, and later persistence.
  - Changes in self-efficacy accounted for the behavioral effect of subgoal attainment.
- **Gap:** the sample size was not found.

**Bandura, A., & Simon, K. M. (1977). The role of proximal intentions in self-regulation of refractory behavior. *Cognitive Therapy and Research, 1*(3), 177–193.** A weight-loss field study, listed among the early small-sample proximal-goal field studies in [Rai et al., 2022](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/2022_subgoals-granularity-and-flexibility_JAP.pdf). [Secondary; results not read]

**Manderlink, G., & Harackiewicz, J. M. (1984). Proximal versus distal goal setting and intrinsic motivation. *JPSP, 47*(4), 918–928. DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.47.4.918.** Citation verified via OpenAlex and [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Proximal-versus-distal-goal-setting-and-intrinsic-Manderlink-Harackiewicz/637d4d3b814c0854dc358dd844384cf675813e72). **Abstract and results not accessible** (the publisher elides the abstract). [Unverified findings]
- The usual description in the literature: the study, run with adults on a puzzle-type task, found that distal goals produced **equal or greater** intrinsic interest than proximal goals. That would conflict with Bandura & Schunk's interest result.
  - The usual explanation is that frequent proximal standards can feel controlling or evaluative.
  - A distal goal leaves the in-between activity free, and reaching it gives a larger competence signal at the end.
  - **I could not confirm the direction, task or sample from a primary source. Treat this as unverified.**
- Rawsthorne & Elliot's 1999 meta-analysis of achievement goals and intrinsic motivation ([PSPR PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/1999_RawsthorneElliot_PSPR.pdf)) reportedly **excluded** this study because the descriptive statistics needed for an effect size were unavailable. This comes from a search summary, and I did not locate the passage myself.

**Latham, G. P., & Seijts, G. H. (1999). The effects of proximal and distal goals on performance on a moderately complex task. *Journal of Organizational Behavior, 20*(4), 421–429.** [Abstract verified via OpenAlex]
- **Sample:** 39 young adults, paid piece-rate to make toys.
- **Results:**
  - "Do your best" earned significantly more money than a distal goal alone.
  - Proximal goals added to the distal goal earned significantly more than "do your best."
  - Self-efficacy correlated with earnings at r = .45 (p < .01).
  - Self-efficacy rose significantly **only** in the proximal-plus-distal condition.
- **The authors' interpretation:** an *informational* mechanism (proximal goals plus feedback focus attention on task-appropriate strategies), not a commitment mechanism.
- Locke & Latham (2002) describe the same study as using "a business game" ([American Psychologist PDF](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/locke%20pract%20goal%20setting%202002%20am%20psy.pdf)). That conflicts with the abstract's toy-making task; Rai et al. (2022) describe it as a multi-round simulation in which goal-relevant information changed between rounds. The task was likely a toy-production simulation with shifting market prices. The abstract wording is the authoritative one.

### Inferences
- The robust part of Bandura & Schunk is the **self-efficacy and mastery pathway**. Stock & Cervone and Latham & Seijts reproduce it in adults on different tasks. The **intrinsic-interest** benefit rests mainly on one study of about 10 children per cell, and it is contested.
- The distal-only condition was no better than no goal in both Bandura & Schunk and Latham & Seijts (where it was worse than "do your best"). This is the most directly relevant point for the product: a multi-month goal on its own, with no near-term structure, appears to give little motivational traction.
- Proximal goals work largely by producing **frequent, interpretable feedback**: a visible "I did this week's work" signal that builds efficacy. A weekly task set is a close analogue, provided completion is visible to the learner.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis specifically of proximal versus distal goals was found. The evidence is a set of small lab and field studies.
- Manderlink & Harackiewicz (1984) could not be read. Its direction of effect is unverified.
- No replication of Bandura & Schunk with adult career-changers or in online, self-paced settings was found.

## 2. Locke & Latham goal-setting theory: specific, challenging, proximal goals; learning vs performance goals; effect sizes

### Takeaway
Specific, difficult goals reliably beat "do your best," with meta-analytic d of roughly .42–.80, though the effect is smaller on complex tasks (d ≈ .41–.48 for the most complex). On novel, complex tasks where people must still learn strategies, a specific *learning* goal beats a performance or outcome goal. Proximal goals help mainly when the environment is uncertain or dynamic, and in at least one study they added nothing to performance.

### Cited Findings
**Locke, E. A., & Latham, G. P. (2002). Building a practically useful theory of goal setting and task motivation: A 35-year odyssey. *American Psychologist, 57*(9), 705–717.** [Full text read] — [PDF](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/locke%20pract%20goal%20setting%202002%20am%20psy.pdf)
- **Specific, difficult goals versus "do your best":** effect sizes in meta-analyses "ranged from .42 to .80," citing Locke & Latham (1990).
- **Task complexity moderates the effect.** Wood, Mento & Locke (1987):
  - Goal difficulty: d = .48 for the most complex tasks versus .67 for the least complex.
  - Specific-difficult versus do-your-best: d = .41 versus .77.
- On tasks that are complex for the person, a specific difficult *performance* goal can make people anxious to succeed, so they scramble for strategies instead of learning. "Do your best" sometimes produces better strategies (Earley, Connolly & Ekegren, 1989).
- **Proximal goals:**
  - Latham & Seijts (1999) is cited as showing proximal-plus-distal goals raise self-efficacy and profits.
  - "Performance errors on a dynamic task are often due to deficient decomposition of a distal goal into proximal goals" (paraphrasing Dörner, 1991).
  - Proximal goals aid error management (Frese & Zapf, 1994).
- **Listed causes of failed goal-setting replications:**
  - not setting proximal goals "when the environment is characterized by uncertainty"
  - setting a performance goal when a learning goal is required
  - no feedback
  - no commitment

**Seijts, G. H., & Latham, G. P. (2001). The effect of distal learning, outcome, and proximal goals on a moderately complex task. *Journal of Organizational Behavior, 22*(3), 291–307.** [Abstract verified via OpenAlex] (Locke & Latham 2002 list the pages as 291–302.)
- **Sample and task:** 96 participants on a class-scheduling task that required acquiring knowledge.
- **Results:**
  - "Do your best" beat a specific, difficult *outcome* goal.
  - A specific, difficult *learning* goal beat "do your best."
  - Goal commitment was higher with learning goals.
  - The number of task-relevant strategies implemented was highest with a distal learning goal plus proximal goals.
- **Counter-evidence:** adding proximal outcome goals to a distal outcome or learning goal "did not lead to higher performance" than the distal goal alone.
- **Mechanism:** the self-efficacy effect on performance was mediated by strategy development.

**Seijts, G. H., Latham, G. P., Tasa, K., & Latham, B. W. (2004). Goal setting and goal orientation: An integration of two different yet related literatures. *Academy of Management Journal, 47*(2), 227–239.** [Full text partly read] — [PDF](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/dahe7472/Sejits%202004.pdf)
- **Task:** a cellular-telephone industry simulation with 13 decision periods and a deregulation shock after period 8 that made the earlier strategies stop working.
- **Results:**
  - A specific learning goal beat both a specific performance goal and a vague goal.
  - Self-efficacy and information search mediated the effect of the learning goal.
  - Self-efficacy and performance were reciprocal over time.
- **Gap:** the sample size was not extracted.

**Epton, T., Currie, S., & Armitage, C. J. (2017). Unique effects of setting goals on behavior change: Systematic review and meta-analysis. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 85*(12), 1182–1198.** [Abstract verified via OpenAlex] — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000260)
- 141 papers, 384 effect sizes, N = 16,523, randomized controlled trials only.
- Unique effect of goal setting on behavior change: d = .34, 95% CI [.28, .41].
- Larger when goals were difficult, set publicly, or group goals.

**Harkin, B., et al. (2016). Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence. *Psychological Bulletin, 142*(2), 198–229.** DOI 10.1037/bul0000025. [Abstract verified via repository] — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)
- 138 studies, N = 19,951.
- Interventions raised the frequency of progress monitoring (d+ = 1.98) and goal attainment (d+ = 0.40, 95% CI [0.32, 0.48]).
- Frequency of monitoring mediated the effect.
- Effects were larger when progress was physically recorded or made public.

### Inferences
- For adults learning a new field (a career change), many weekly tasks are *learning* tasks such as researching a role, building a portfolio piece or networking for the first time. The Seijts & Latham line suggests weekly targets framed as **learning or strategy goals** ("find out X," "try two ways to Y") may beat pure output quotas ("send 20 applications") while skills are still forming.
- Goal-setting theory backs proximal goals most clearly when the path is uncertain or dynamic and feedback is needed to adjust strategy, which fits a career change. The Seijts & Latham (2001) null result means "add weekly subgoals" is not by itself a guaranteed performance gain. The benefit runs through the feedback and strategy discovery that proximal goals make possible.
- A weekly task list with visible completion is effectively a progress-monitoring intervention. Harkin et al. (d ≈ 0.40) is arguably the strongest meta-analytic support for the surface, and recording progress physically strengthens the effect.

### Gaps
- I did not read the original Wood, Mento & Locke (1987) or Locke & Latham (1990) meta-analyses. Their numbers are as reported by Locke & Latham (2002).
- No meta-analysis isolating proximal versus distal goal *timeframe* as a moderator was found. Epton et al.'s moderator list, as shown in the abstract, does not include it.

## 3. Subgoals and goal hierarchies: when they help and when they backfire

### Takeaway
Subgoals help most at the start of a pursuit and when the distance to the goal is uncertain: they make the goal feel attainable and create imminent deadlines. They can backfire through complacency or licensing ("I did my part") when people treat a finished subgoal as *progress made* rather than as evidence of *commitment*, and when progress is already certain. The best large field test (N = 9,108) found that subgoal framing raised volunteering by about 7–8% over 12 weeks. A biweekly framing gave more durable benefits than a weekly one, which points to a granularity-versus-flexibility tradeoff.

### Cited Findings
**Fishbach, A., Dhar, R., & Zhang, Y. (2006). Subgoals as substitutes or complements: The role of goal accessibility. *JPSP, 91*(2), 232–242.** [Abstract verified via OpenAlex]
- When people consider *success on a single subgoal*, further actions toward the superordinate goal are seen as **substitutes** and are less likely to be pursued.
- When people treat initial subgoal success as evidence of *commitment* to the superordinate goal, further actions seem **complementary** and are more likely to be pursued.
- Four studies.
- The title's "goal accessibility" framing (whether the overall goal is mentally active) comes from the title. The abstract emphasizes the progress-versus-commitment framing.

**Amir, O., & Ariely, D. (2008). Resting on laurels: The effects of discrete progress markers as subgoals on task performance and preferences. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 34*(5), 1158–1171.** DOI 10.1037/a0012857. [Abstract verified via OpenAlex]
- When the distance to the goal is **uncertain**, discrete progress markers reduce uncertainty and improve performance and preference.
- When the distance is **certain**, they "may generate complacency, sway motivation away from the end goal, and decrease performance," as well as the task's appeal.
- Four experiments.
- **Integrity caveat:** Ariely co-authored this paper. His 2002 procrastination paper with Wertenbroch (below) was retracted in September 2026 over data anomalies, per [Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/09/03/procrastination-study-duke-dan-ariely-psychological-science-data-colada-tampering-retraction/). I found no retraction of Amir & Ariely (2008), but its results should be weighted with caution until independently replicated.

**Huang, S.-C., Jin, L., & Zhang, Y. (2017). Step by step: Sub-goals as a source of motivation. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 141*, 1–15.** DOI 10.1016/j.obhdp.2017.05.001. Note that this is OBHDP, not the *Journal of Consumer Research*. [Abstract-level summary via search and the GSB research page; full text not read] — [Stanford GSB summary](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/focus-small-steps-first-then-shift-larger-goal); [ResearchGate PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/Szu-Chi-Huang/publication/316611531_Step_by_Step_Sub-Goals_as_a_Source_of_Motivation/links/5afe205d0f7e9b98e0197519/Step-by-Step-Sub-Goals-as-a-Source-of-Motivation.pdf)
- **Early in a pursuit,** motivation rests on whether the end goal feels **attainable**, and a subgoal structure raises that sense of attainability.
- **Late in a pursuit,** motivation rests on whether actions feel **valuable**, and focusing on the overall goal raises perceived value.
- Four lab and field experiments (exercise, business reviews, paid crowd-work).
- In the crowd-sourced field study, uploading book information, the hybrid plan (subgoals first, then the overall goal) was the most motivating.
- Rai et al. (2022) list this among field studies with 70 or fewer per condition.

**Rai, A., Sharif, M. A., Chang, E. H., Milkman, K. L., & Duckworth, A. L. (2022). A field experiment on subgoal framing to boost volunteering: The tradeoff between goal granularity and flexibility. *Journal of Applied Psychology*.** DOI 10.1037/apl0001040. [Full text read, author manuscript] — [PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/2022_subgoals-granularity-and-flexibility_JAP.pdf)
- **Sample and design:** preregistered, N = 9,108 crisis-counseling volunteers (more than 3,000 per condition), run over 12 weeks.
- **Conditions:** an annual goal of 200 hours (control), reframed as either 4 hours every week or 8 hours every two weeks.
- **Main result:** both subgoal framings raised volunteering by about 7–8% over 12 weeks.
- **Durability:** the more flexible biweekly framing produced **more durable** benefits.
- **Weekly versus biweekly:** productivity differences between the two framings were "largely" non-significant.
- **Who benefited:** effects did not vary by gender, tenure, or prior goal progress.
- **Procrastination:** both subgoal framings reduced the number of days before a person first volunteered each week.
- **Pilot vignette (N = 900):** granular subgoals were associated with higher self-efficacy and goal commitment and with lower procrastination.
- **Their reading of the earlier literature:** it is mostly one-shot lab studies (Amir & Ariely 2008; Fishbach et al. 2006; Latham & Seijts 1999; Seijts & Latham 2001; Stock & Cervone 1990) or small field studies with 70 or fewer per condition (Bandura & Schunk 1981; Bandura & Simon 1977; Huang et al. 2017; Latham & Brown 2006).
- **Their reply to the complacency worry:** "our field data suggests that subgoals create value even under these circumstances," where progress is observable. Following Gal & McShane (2012), they suggest that over months, attention shifts back to the overarching goal.
- **Flexibility matters:** reduced flexibility raises the chance of a missed subgoal, and goal violations raise the risk of abandonment (Cochran & Tesser 1996; Soman & Cheema 2004).

**Gal, D., & McShane, B. B. (2012). Can small victories help win the war? Evidence from consumer debt management. *Journal of Marketing Research, 49*(4), 487–501.** [Abstract verified via OpenAlex]
- Field data from a debt-settlement firm.
- **Closing individual debt accounts** (completing discrete subtasks) predicted eliminating all debt, regardless of the dollar balance closed.
- The dollar balance closed did not predict elimination once the fraction of accounts closed was controlled for.
- The finding is correlational.

**Ariely, D., & Wertenbroch, K. (2002). Procrastination, deadlines, and performance: Self-control by precommitment. *Psychological Science, 13*(3), 219–224. RETRACTED (September 2026).** — [Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/09/03/procrastination-study-duke-dan-ariely-psychological-science-data-colada-tampering-retraction/); OpenAlex lists it as "RETRACTED."
- It was widely cited, including by Rai et al. (2022), for the claim that evenly spaced external deadlines beat self-imposed ones.
- The retraction followed a failed replication published in *Psychological Science* (July 2026) and Data Colada's forensic posts. Coauthor Wertenbroch requested it.
- **Do not cite it as support for interim deadlines.**

### Inferences
- The product's design (weekly tasks on the working surface, the journey on a separate page) matches the conditions under which subgoals help most:
  - a long, uncertain pursuit, where the distance to the goal in a career change is genuinely uncertain (cf. Amir & Ariely's uncertainty condition)
  - an early-stage need to feel the goal is attainable (Huang et al.)
- The main backfire risk is licensing: "done for this week" read as "made progress, can relax" (Fishbach et al.). Framing completed weeks as evidence of commitment and identity ("you're someone who shows up for this") rather than as banked progress is the evidence-based counter. This is an inference from the abstract's progress-versus-commitment framing and has not been tested in this context.
- Huang et al. suggest the separation should not be static. As learners near the end of the journey, bringing the overall goal back into view may raise motivation. The overview page earns its place, and late-stage weeks could connect tasks to the end goal more explicitly.
- Rai et al. suggest a weekly cadence may be slightly too rigid: a biweekly framing was more durable. For adults with jobs and families, a weekly set with some slack or carry-over may protect against "I missed this week, so I've failed" abandonment.

### Gaps
- No study was found that compares, head-to-head, *hiding* the distal goal from the working surface versus showing both. Most designs give proximal goals *in addition to* a distal goal (Bandura & Schunk; Latham & Seijts).
- Huang et al. (2017) full-text effect sizes were not read.
- Amir & Ariely (2008) has not been independently replicated in anything I found.

## 4. Construal level theory: temporal distance, abstraction and action initiation

### Takeaway
Construal level theory (CLT) holds that distant events are represented abstractly, in terms of *why* and desirability, and near events concretely, in terms of *how* and feasibility. That would make near-term weekly tasks the natural place for actionable planning. But a 2026 multi-lab registered replication found the temporal-distance effect to be **tiny** (d = 0.08 versus the original 0.92). The CLT rationale is therefore weak support, and the design case should lean on goal-setting and subgoal evidence instead.

### Cited Findings
- **Trope, Y., & Liberman, N. (2010). Construal-level theory of psychological distance. *Psychological Review, 117*(2), 440–463.** DOI 10.1037/a0018963. [Abstract verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20438233/); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3152826/)
  - The more remote something is from direct experience (in time, space, social distance or hypotheticality), the more abstractly it is represented.
  - These distances similarly affect prediction, preference and action.
- **Liberman, N., & Trope, Y. (1998). The role of feasibility and desirability considerations in near and distant future decisions: A test of temporal construal theory. *JPSP, 75*(1), 5–18.** [Abstract verified via OpenAlex]
  - Distant-future activities are construed at a higher level.
  - Decisions about distant activities weight desirability more and feasibility less.
  - When choosing assignments, students weighted interest for a distant-future assignment and difficulty for a near-future one.
  - Distant plans related to desirability, not to time constraints.
- **Fujita, K., Trope, Y., Liberman, N., & Levin-Sagi, M. (2006). Construal levels and self-control. *JPSP, 90*(3), 351–367.** [Abstract verified via OpenAlex]
  - This is a counterweight: **high-level (abstract) construals** led to:
    - lower preference for immediate over delayed outcomes
    - greater physical endurance
    - stronger self-control intentions
    - less positive evaluations of temptations
  - Six experiments.
- **Calderon, S., Mac Giolla, E., Ask, K., et al. (2026). Effects of psychological distance on mental abstraction: A registered report of four tests of construal level theory. *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 9*(2).** DOI 10.1177/25152459251401177. [Abstract and summary verified] — [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/36132/)
  - N = 11,775 across 27 countries and regions.
  - The temporal protocol (N = 2,941) directly replicated Liberman & Trope (1998, Study 1): **d = 0.08, 95% CI [0.003, 0.16]**, against an original d = 0.92.
  - The spatial protocol replicated Fujita et al. (2006, Study 1).
  - Overall, the authors report "limited evidence for the predictions of the theory."

### Inferences
- The CLT story is intuitive: near goals are concrete and actionable, while distant goals are abstract and inspiring but hard to act on. But its core temporal-abstraction effect replicated at about a tenth of its original size. Use it as framing at most, not as evidence.
- Even taken at face value, CLT cuts both ways. Fujita et al. find that abstract, distal "why" construals *help* self-control against temptations. This supports keeping the multi-month "why" reachable (the overview page) rather than eliminating it, for example as a one-line reminder at decision points.

### Gaps
- The CLIMR spatial, social and likelihood protocol effect sizes were not extracted.
- No CLT study was found that tests action *initiation* on weekly versus multi-month framed tasks directly.

## 5. Temporal discounting and present bias

### Takeaway
Present-biased (hyperbolic) preferences mean costs today loom larger than distant rewards. People who are naive about this bias keep postponing costly tasks. This is a standard economic rationale for why a career change months away motivates weakly and why near deadlines help. The one prominent direct test of interim deadlines (Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002) is now retracted.

### Cited Findings
- **O'Donoghue, T., & Rabin, M. (1999). Doing it now or later. *American Economic Review, 89*(1), 103–124.** [Abstract via RePEc and summaries] — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cdl/econwp/qt7t44m5b0.html)
  - Present-biased, time-inconsistent preferences lead people who are naive about their bias to **procrastinate on tasks with immediate costs and delayed rewards**.
  - Even small present bias can cause severe harm for naive agents on such tasks.
  - Sophistication, meaning awareness of one's own bias, reduces procrastination.
- **Rai et al. (2022)** argue that subgoals reduce procrastination by creating "more frequent and imminent deadlines." They also argue subgoals raise commitment by asking for "small, near-term sacrifices of time, which are less daunting than large, distant sacrifices" (citing Gourville, 1998). In their field data, both subgoal framings shortened the delay before volunteering each week. — [PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/2022_subgoals-granularity-and-flexibility_JAP.pdf)
- **Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002)**, on externally imposed, evenly spaced deadlines beating self-imposed ones, was **retracted** in September 2026. — [Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/09/03/procrastination-study-duke-dan-ariely-psychological-science-data-colada-tampering-retraction/)

### Inferences
- Career-change tasks are the classic "immediate cost, delayed reward" case: effort now, a new job months later. The O'Donoghue & Rabin model predicts chronic postponement when the only deadline is distant. A weekly due date turns the payoff into something near: completing the week.
- With the Ariely & Wertenbroch evidence gone, the strongest *empirical* support for near deadlines in this set is Rai et al.'s field result on reduced weekly procrastination.

### Gaps
- Laibson (1997) and the meta-analyses of present-bias estimates were not retrieved.
- No study was found that measures discount rates for career outcomes specifically.

## 6. What is robust, contested, or failed to replicate

### Takeaway
Robust: specific, challenging goals beat vague ones; monitoring progress helps (d ≈ .40); subgoal framing raises sustained effort in a large preregistered field test (about 7–8%). Supported but small-sample: proximal goals raise self-efficacy. Contested: proximal goals raising intrinsic interest; proximal goals raising performance on complex learning tasks. Weak or failed: the CLT temporal-abstraction effect. Retracted: Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002) on deadlines.

### Cited Findings
- **Robust, meta-analytic or large preregistered:**
  - Specific, difficult goals versus "do your best": d .42–.80, smaller on complex tasks — [Locke & Latham 2002](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/locke%20pract%20goal%20setting%202002%20am%20psy.pdf)
  - Goal setting in behavior-change RCTs: d = .34 — [Epton et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000260)
  - Progress monitoring: d = .40 on attainment — [Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)
  - Subgoal framing: +7–8% over 12 weeks, N = 9,108 — [Rai et al. 2022](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/2022_subgoals-granularity-and-flexibility_JAP.pdf)
- **Supported, but small samples:**
  - Proximal goals raise self-efficacy: Bandura & Schunk (1981), N = 40; Latham & Seijts (1999), N = 39; Stock & Cervone (1990) — [Rai et al. on power concerns](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/2022_subgoals-granularity-and-flexibility_JAP.pdf)
- **Contested:**
  - Intrinsic interest: Bandura & Schunk positive; Manderlink & Harackiewicz (1984) reportedly not (unverified).
  - Performance on complex learning tasks: proximal outcome goals added nothing to performance in Seijts & Latham (2001) — [OpenAlex abstract](https://doi.org/10.1002/job.70)
  - Complacency from progress markers (Amir & Ariely 2008) is not borne out at the months-long field scale (Rai et al. 2022).
- **Failed or weak replication:** CLT temporal distance gave d = 0.08 versus the original 0.92 — [CLIMR 2026](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/36132/)
- **Retracted:** Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002) — [Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/09/03/procrastination-study-duke-dan-ariely-psychological-science-data-colada-tampering-retraction/)

### Inferences
- The product decision (weekly tasks as the working surface, the journey on a separate page) is well supported on **sustained effort, self-efficacy, procrastination and progress monitoring**. It is weakly or not supported on **intrinsic interest**, and the CLT rationale should not be leaned on.
- Three design implications have direct evidence:
  - frame completed weeks as commitment, not banked progress (Fishbach et al.)
  - allow flexibility or slack within the cadence (Rai et al.)
  - bring the distal goal back into view later in the journey, and at temptation or decision points (Huang et al.; Fujita et al.)

### Gaps
- None of the studies tested adults in career transition, online and self-paced, over many months with *weekly* task sets specifically. Rai et al. (volunteers, 12 weeks) is the closest analogue.
- No study was found that compares showing the distal goal on the working surface versus moving it to a separate page.
