# Cognitive load, overwhelm from large task sets, and the week as a unit of commitment

Scope: evidence for showing a career changer only this week's small, load-sized set of tasks (due end of week, calendar times as suggestions, full curriculum on a separate locked page), plus caveats and counter-evidence.

Verification key: items marked **[verified this session]** were checked against an abstract, publisher page or repository record during this research. Items marked **[from prior knowledge, not re-verified]** are well-known citations given with DOI links but were not re-checked here; confirm figures before quoting them in anything public.

## 1. Working memory and cognitive load: how many active items can people hold?

### Takeaway
Working memory holds roughly 3 to 5 chunks (Cowan's ~4), not Miller's 7, and cognitive load theory says learning degrades when intrinsic plus extraneous load exceeds that limit. This supports keeping the *active* set small and sizing it by load rather than count, but the research is about items held in mind during a task, not about how many to-dos a person can have listed in a week. The link to weekly task lists is an analogy, not a direct finding.

### Cited Findings
- Miller (1956), "The magical number seven, plus or minus two: Some limits on our capacity for processing information," *Psychological Review*, 63(2), 81–97. Cowan's later reading is that Miller's "seven" was a rough estimate and partly a rhetorical device rather than a precise limit. **[citation from prior knowledge; Cowan's characterization verified this session]** — [Cowan 2001, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/)
- Cowan (2001), "The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity," *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 24(1), 87–114. Target article plus commentaries. It brings together a wide range of data for a single central capacity limit averaging about **four chunks**. The limit is observable only when items cannot be recoded into larger chunks through rehearsal or long-term knowledge. **[verified this session]** — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/magical-number-4-in-shortterm-memory-a-reconsideration-of-mental-storage-capacity/44023F1147D4A1D44BDC0AD226838496); [author PDF](https://memory.psych.missouri.edu/assets/doc/articles/2001/cowan-bbs-2001.pdf)
- The four-chunk estimate is not universally accepted. Later work has argued for values closer to seven, and Reynolds et al. (2022) proposed about five in adults. **[verified via secondary review this session]** — [Journal of Cognition review](https://journalofcognition.org/articles/387)
- Sweller (1988), "Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning," *Cognitive Science*, 12(2), 257–285. This is the founding cognitive load theory (CLT) paper: means-ends problem solving uses working-memory capacity that schema acquisition would otherwise use. **[from prior knowledge, not re-verified]** — [DOI 10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4)
- Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas (1998), "Cognitive architecture and instructional design," *Educational Psychology Review*, 10(3), 251–296. This paper separates intrinsic, extraneous and germane load and argues that instruction should cut extraneous load because working memory is limited. **[from prior knowledge, not re-verified]** — [DOI 10.1023/A:1022193728205](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022193728205)
- Critique: de Jong (2010), "Cognitive load theory, educational research, and instructional design: some food for thought," *Instructional Science*, 38, 105–134. It argues that the load types are hard to measure separately, that CLT is partly unfalsifiable after the fact, and that self-report load measures are weak. **[from prior knowledge, not re-verified]** — [DOI 10.1007/s11251-009-9110-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-009-9110-0)

### Inferences
- Sizing the weekly set by cognitive load rather than a fixed count fits CLT better than a "7 tasks" rule. In Cowan's account, chunk size depends on expertise, so a novice career changer should get smaller and fewer heavy items than an expert would.
- Tasks are not held in working memory all week. The working-memory argument applies most directly to the moment a learner looks at the screen and decides what to do. Showing only a few items at that moment reduces the comparison and selection load. Any "4" limit should be treated as a design heuristic, not a law.
- Hiding the full curriculum removes a source of *extraneous* load (scanning, comparing and worrying about future modules) without removing intrinsic task content. This is the standard CLT argument, and it applies here by analogy.

### Gaps
- I found no study that directly tests how many concurrent weekly to-dos adults can manage, or that links Cowan's capacity limit to to-do list length. This is an inferential bridge.
- I did not find validated methods for estimating the "cognitive load" of a real-world career task (for example, writing a cover letter versus watching a video) in advance.

## 2. Choice overload: does showing fewer options help? (Contested)

### Takeaway
The famous jam study found that fewer options led to more action. However, a 2010 meta-analysis found a mean choice-overload effect of about zero with large heterogeneity. A 2015 meta-analysis found reliable effects only when specific moderators were present: high decision difficulty, complex choice sets, high preference uncertainty and an effort-minimizing goal. A novice career changer facing an unfamiliar curriculum plausibly matches several of those moderators, but no meta-analysis supports "fewer is always better."

### Cited Findings
- Iyengar & Lepper (2000), "When choice is demotivating: Can one desire too much of a good thing?" *JPSP*, 79(6), 995–1006. In a grocery-store display, 24 jams attracted more shoppers than 6, but only about 3% of those who stopped at the large display bought, against about 30% at the small one. Students offered 6 essay topics, rather than 30, were more likely to write the extra-credit essay and wrote better ones. **[from prior knowledge, not re-verified]** — [DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995)
- Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010), "Can there ever be too many options? A meta-analytic review of choice overload," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 37(3), 409–425. The review pooled 63 conditions from 50 experiments (N = 5,036). The **mean effect was virtually zero**, with considerable variance between studies. The authors identified several possible preconditions but **no sufficient conditions**. A "more is better" effect appeared where people had clear prior preferences. **[verified this session]** — [Indiana PDF](https://abcwest.cogs.indiana.edu/pmwiki/pdf/scheibehenne.options.2010.pdf); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v37y2010i3p409-425.html)
- Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman published a comment, and Scheibehenne et al. replied, in the same journal. The comment argued that a pooled mean effect "compares apples with oranges" when moderators exist, and that a null mean does not refute choice overload. **[verified this session]** — [Scheibehenne et al. reply PDF](https://abcwest.cogs.indiana.edu/pmwiki/pdf/scheibehenne.reply.2011.pdf)
- Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman (2015), "When product assortment leads to choice overload: A conceptual review and meta-analysis," *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 25(2), 333–358. Four moderators each had a **reliable, significant** effect: **decision task difficulty, choice set complexity, preference uncertainty and an effort-minimizing goal**. Once these are accounted for, the assortment-size effect is significant. Satisfaction/confidence, regret, deferral and switching worked as interchangeable outcome measures. A secondary interview reports 99 observations from 53 studies (N = 7,202), which I **could not confirm** against the paper. **[verified this session, abstract level]** — [Kellogg summary](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual)
- McShane & Böckenholt (2018), *JCR*, is a further meta-analytic treatment. A 2024 *Frontiers* review describes the literature as split: some meta-analyses find strong, moderated effects, while others report replication failures and a general lack of support. **[verified at secondary level this session]** — [Misuraca et al. 2024, Frontiers in Psychology (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11111947/)

### Inferences
- The robust claim is conditional: overload appears when choices are hard, complex and unfamiliar, and when the chooser lacks clear preferences. Career changers new to a field typically have high preference uncertainty about what to learn first. That is the condition under which curation should help most.
- Choice deferral is one of the outcome measures. "I'll decide what to work on later" is the behavior the weekly view is trying to prevent.
- The weekly task view is mostly not an "assortment" of alternatives; it is a set of assigned tasks. Choice overload applies most directly to the curriculum page (which module to pick), not to a fixed weekly list. Prerequisite locking removes the choice entirely, which goes further than reducing options.
- Choice overload should be cited as "contested, moderator-dependent," never as a settled effect, and the jam study should not be used alone.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve exact moderator effect sizes from Chernev et al. (2015).
- I found no choice-overload study in adult self-directed learning or curriculum selection specifically.

## 3. Overwhelm, task aversion and procrastination; unfinished goals and plan-making

### Takeaway
Task aversiveness and the delay of rewards are among the strongest, most consistent predictors of procrastination (Steel 2007). Large, distant and vague goals combine both. Proximal subgoals improve progress and self-efficacy (Bandura & Schunk 1981). The classic Zeigarnik memory effect does **not** hold up meta-analytically. However, Masicampo & Baumeister (2011) found that unfulfilled goals do intrude on cognition and that making a specific plan removes that intrusion. This supports "show a plan for this week" over "show everything outstanding."

### Cited Findings
- Steel (2007), "The nature of procrastination: A meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure," *Psychological Bulletin*, 133(1), 65–94. The review covers 691 correlations. **Task aversiveness, task delay (timing of rewards), self-efficacy, impulsiveness and conscientiousness** (self-control, distractibility, organization, achievement motivation) are "strong and consistent predictors." Neuroticism, rebelliousness and sensation seeking are weak predictors. The results are consistent with temporal motivation theory (expectancy theory plus hyperbolic discounting). Per-predictor r values are in the full text, which I did not retrieve. **[verified this session, abstract level]** — [U Calgary repository](https://prism.ucalgary.ca/handle/1880/47914); [ScienceDaily summary](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/01/070110090851.htm)
- Bandura & Schunk (1981), "Cultivating competence, self-efficacy, and intrinsic interest through proximal self-motivation," *JPSP*, 41(3), 586–598. Children with large arithmetic deficits and low interest did self-directed learning under **proximal subgoals, distal goals or no goals**. The proximal-subgoal group progressed fastest, reached substantial mastery, and developed higher self-efficacy and intrinsic interest. The authors argue that subgoals work best when attainable. The sample was children, not adults. **[verified this session]** — [full-text PDF](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/59faaf5b01b9500001e95457/5bc552d85141987915dab842_Bandura%20&%20Schunk,%201981.pdf)
- Zeigarnik effect (better recall of interrupted tasks) has not held up. Ghibellini & Meier (2025), "Interruption, recall and resumption: a meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects," *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*, DOI 10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w, found **no memory advantage for unfinished tasks**. They did find a general **tendency to resume** interrupted tasks (the Ovsiankina effect). Secondary sources report a recall ratio of about 0.99 and resumption of about 67% against a 50% baseline, which I did not verify in the primary text. Study counts in secondary sources conflict (37 vs 59). **[verified this session, abstract/secondary level]** — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html); [Univ. Bern PDF](https://www.kog.psy.unibe.ch/e48276/e1785865/s41599-025-05000-w1_ger.pdf)
- Masicampo & Baumeister (2011), "Consider it done! Plan making can eliminate the cognitive effects of unfulfilled goals," *JPSP*, 101(4), 667–683. Unfulfilled goals caused **intrusive thoughts** during an unrelated reading task (Studies 1, 5B), **higher accessibility** of goal words (Studies 2, 3) and **worse anagram performance** (Study 4). Writing **specific plans** eliminated these effects. Participants who later executed their plans were the ones who showed no extra intrusions. Proposed mechanism: a plan suspends the goal drive until the specified time. Samples were student lab studies; per-study N was not retrieved. **[verified this session, abstract level]** — [APA supplemental record](https://supp.apa.org:443/psycarticles/supplemental/a0024192/a0024192_supp.html); [abstract reproduction](https://scrapbox.io/nishio/consider_it_done)
- Caveat from the same group: Masicampo & Baumeister (2012), "Committed but closed-minded: When making a specific plan for a goal hinders success," *Social Cognition*, 30, 37–55. I did not retrieve its content; per its title, specific plans can reduce openness to alternative routes. **[title verified only]** — [Baumeister blog listing](https://roybaumeister.com/2012/06/16/committed-but-closed-minded-when-making-a-specific-plan-for-a-goal-hinders-success/)

### Inferences
- Under temporal motivation theory, a whole career-change curriculum has low expectancy (it feels unachievable), a very delayed reward (a job months away) and high aversiveness (ambiguity). A weekly set with completion feedback shortens the delay to a "reward" (finishing the week) and raises expectancy. This is inferred from Steel's model, not tested directly.
- The design should not justify itself with the "Zeigarnik effect." The defensible version is Masicampo & Baumeister's: outstanding goals without a plan intrude on cognition, and a concrete plan quiets them. Locking future modules may help in the same way, because the system "holds the plan" for them. This is untested.
- Ovsiankina (resumption) supports making unfinished weekly tasks easy to resume, for example by showing where the learner left off.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain Steel's per-predictor correlations (task aversiveness r and k) from the full text.
- I found no direct study of "showing the full curriculum vs only the current week" on procrastination or dropout in adult learning platforms.
- I did not retrieve the replication status of Masicampo & Baumeister (2011) and found no large-scale replication.

## 4. Implementation intentions: scheduling tasks into the week

### Takeaway
If-then plans ("when X, I will do Y") have a medium-to-large average effect on goal attainment (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, d ≈ 0.65). That supports suggesting specific calendar slots. But planning many goals at once can backfire by making the whole set feel hard (Dalton & Spiller 2012). That argues for a small weekly set and for framing it as manageable.

### Cited Findings
- Gollwitzer (1999), "Implementation intentions: Strong effects of simple plans," *American Psychologist*, 54(7), 493–503. This is the foundational statement of if-then planning. **[from prior knowledge, not re-verified; title confirmed via repository listing]** — [DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493); [Academia listing](https://www.academia.edu/55735607/Implementation_intentions_Strong_effects_of_simple_plans)
- Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), "Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes," *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 38, 69–119. The meta-analysis covers **94 independent tests** (a secondary source says from 63 reports, unverified) and found **d = 0.65** on goal attainment, which is medium to large. Benefits appeared in initiating goal striving, shielding goals from distraction, disengaging from failing courses of action and conserving capacity. Publication bias and lab-heavy samples are typical concerns with meta-analyses of this era. **[verified this session at citation/secondary level]** — [Konstanz record](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes)
- A newer, larger meta-analysis by Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer covers **642 independent tests** and builds a taxonomy of plan cues and responses. I did not retrieve its pooled effect size, but my recollection is that it is smaller than 0.65; treat that as unverified. **[existence verified via secondary source this session]** — [Konstanz record (context)](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/node/170)
- Dalton & Spiller (2012), "Too much of a good thing: The benefits of implementation intentions depend on the number of goals," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 39(3), 600–614. In a week-long field study, participants were assigned **one or six** everyday goals a day, either with a simple commitment or with implementation plans. Planning helped for **one** goal but not for **multiple** goals. Implemental planning made people notice how hard it would be to execute several goals, which **lowered commitment**. A second study (computer task) replicated the pattern. **Framing multiple goals as manageable** restored the benefit. **[verified this session]** — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-664500.html); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/05/120516152448.htm)
- A converging result appears in "Less is more: The effect of multiple implementation intentions targeting unhealthy snacking habits," *European Journal of Social Psychology* (Verhoeven et al., 2013), where multiple plans were less effective than single ones. **[title verified; authors and details from prior knowledge]** — [Wiley record (proxy link)](https://onlinelibrary-wiley-com-443.webvpn.zafu.edu.cn/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.1963)

### Inferences
- Suggested calendar times work as the "when/where" cue of an implementation intention, and that is where the d ≈ 0.65 evidence applies. The cue matters more than the deadline.
- Dalton & Spiller is the strongest direct argument for keeping the *planned* set small. Scheduling slots for a large set could lower commitment by making the difficulty visible. It also supports copy and UI that present the week's set as manageable (for example, an estimated total time that fits the learner's stated availability).
- Treating calendar times as "suggestions, not deadlines" is compatible with the implementation-intention literature. The plan's value comes from specifying the cue, not from penalizing misses. Whether soft cues work as well as firm self-set ones is untested.

### Gaps
- I found no study isolating system-*suggested* slots from self-*chosen* slots. Most implementation-intention studies have participants write their own plans. Self-generation may matter, so the product may want the learner to confirm or edit the slot.
- I did not retrieve the pooled effect from Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer.

## 5. Fresh start effect and temporal landmarks: is a weekly reset useful?

### Takeaway
Goal-directed behavior (gym visits, "diet" searches, commitment-device sign-ups) spikes at the start of weeks, months, semesters and after birthdays (Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014). Framing a date as a new beginning increases goal initiation (2015), and a field experiment showed that fresh-start framing raised savings (2021). The week start is one of the landmarks that showed effects, which directly supports a weekly reset. Caveats: the effect helps most after failure, it may demotivate people who were succeeding, and an *anticipated* landmark can lead people to postpone until it arrives.

### Cited Findings
- Dai, Milkman & Riis (2014), "The fresh start effect: Temporal landmarks motivate aspirational behavior," *Management Science*, 60(10), 2563–2582. Three archival datasets: U.S. Google searches for "diet" (2004–2012) rose at the start of weeks, months and after federal holidays, not only at New Year's. University gym card-swipe data showed more visits at the start of a **new week**, month and semester, after breaks, and after birthdays. Commitment-contract sign-ups (stickK) showed similar landmark spikes. Sample figures in secondary sources conflict (one says 11,912 gym members over 442 days); check the paper. **[verified this session at secondary level]** — [CHIBE record](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/the-fresh-start-effect-breaking-points-in-life-motivate-virtuous-behavior/); [APS "Why Monday"](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/why-monday-is-the-best-day-for-setting-new-goals.html)
- Dai, Milkman & Riis (2015), "Put your imperfections behind you: Temporal landmarks spur goal initiation when they signal new beginnings," *Psychological Science*, 26(12), 1927–1936. In experiments, describing the same date as a landmark that signals a new beginning (for example, "first day of spring" vs "third Thursday of March") increased intentions to start goals. The proposed mechanism is psychological distance from the past, imperfect self. **[from prior knowledge, not re-verified; title confirmed this session]** — [DOI 10.1177/0956797615605818](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615605818)
- Beshears, Dai, Milkman & Benartzi (2021), "Using fresh starts to nudge increased retirement savings," *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 163, 6–16. Field experiment with university employees (**N = 6,082**). Framing a future date as a fresh start (birthday, first day of spring) increased take-up of future contribution increases without reducing immediate increases. Cumulative contributions over 8 months were about **25% higher** than with other mailings. Placebo landmarks without "new beginning" meaning had no effect. **[verified this session]** — [CHIBE record](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/using-fresh-starts-to-nudge-increased-retirement-savings/)
- Peetz & Wilson (2013), "The post-birthday world: Consequences of temporal landmarks for temporal self-appraisal and motivation," *JPSP*, 104(2), 249–266 (page range varies by source). Landmarks enlarge the perceived gap between the current self and the ideal post-landmark self, which can motivate action toward the ideal. Peetz & Wilson (2014), "Marking time," shows people spontaneously use landmarks to distance themselves from undesirable past selves. **[verified this session at abstract/reference level]** — [SAGE record (2014)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0146167213501559); [review citing both](https://journal.psych.ac.cn/xlkxjz/EN/Y2019/V27/I7/1275)
- Davydenko & Peetz (2019), "Does it matter if a week starts on Monday or Sunday? How calendar format can motivate goal motivation in the moment," *Self and Identity*. How the calendar presents the week's start changes perceived landmarks and motivation. **[existence verified via secondary source; findings not retrieved]** — [Carleton Lifetools](https://carleton.ca/lifetools/time)
- Boundary condition: fresh starts help most after **failure** and can **demotivate** people whose prior performance was good (a reset discards a streak). Reported from Dai's later work, including a word-game score-reset experiment. **[secondary source only, not verified against primary]** — [Psychology Today 2018](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ritual-and-the-brain/201811/the-unexpected-science-of-fresh-starts-and-failures)
- Anticipated landmarks: Koo, Dai, Mai & Song (2020), "Anticipated temporal landmarks undermine motivation for continued goal pursuit" (*OBHDP*). When people anticipate an upcoming landmark, motivation for continued pursuit drops beforehand, as in "I'll restart next week." **[working paper verified to exist; findings from prior knowledge, not re-verified]** — [SSRN PDF via UCLA Anderson](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Koo-Dai-Mai0Song_TemporalLandmark2020_SSRN-id3621221.pdf)
- I found **no replication failure or meta-analysis** of the fresh start effect in this search. Most coverage is secondary or popular. This is absence of evidence, not confirmation. — [search summary sources: Coglode](https://www.coglode.com/nuggets/fresh-start-effect)

### Inferences
- The week start is an empirically observed landmark, so a weekly reset can borrow the effect. The effect is strongest if the product *frames* Monday (or the learner's chosen week start) as a new beginning, since the 2015 and 2021 studies show framing does the work.
- Design risks: (a) a reset after a strong week may feel like losing progress, so carry visible cumulative progress across weeks; (b) late in a week with unfinished tasks, an approaching reset may invite "I'll start fresh next week," which is the Koo et al. pattern. A "due end of week" boundary should probably roll unfinished tasks forward explicitly rather than silently wiping them, and should avoid shame framing.
- Letting the learner choose their week's start day fits Davydenko & Peetz's finding that calendar format shapes perceived landmarks. This is an inference only.

### Gaps
- No preregistered replication of the fresh start effect was found.
- I found no study testing weekly resets inside learning or career platforms.
- Koo et al.'s published version and effect sizes were not verified.

## 6. Deadlines: interim vs final, self-imposed vs external, Parkinson-type effects

### Takeaway
Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002) is the usual citation for evenly spaced deadlines beating one final deadline. However, a 2026 replication of its key study in *Psychological Science* **failed to replicate**, and other field work on interim deadlines is mixed. Treat "weekly deadlines improve performance" as **not robustly supported**. The safer claim is that people know they procrastinate and will choose binding deadlines, and that deadlines mainly affect *timing*.

### Cited Findings
- Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002), "Procrastination, deadlines, and performance: Self-control by precommitment," *Psychological Science*, 13(3), 219–224. People **voluntarily adopt costly self-imposed deadlines**, which shows awareness of their own procrastination. Those deadlines improved performance, but **less than evenly spaced external deadlines**, so people did not set *optimal* deadlines. Study 2 was a proofreading task with paid participants. Samples were small (MIT executive-education students in Study 1, about 50 students in Study 2; sample details from prior knowledge). **[core claims verified this session]** — [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/810365); [Stafforini summary](https://stafforini.com/works/ariely-2002-procrastination-deadlines-performance/)
- **Replication failure:** Hyndman & Bisin (2026), "Replication of 'Procrastination, Deadlines, and Performance: Self-Control by Precommitment,'" *Psychological Science*, 37(8), 557–571, DOI 10.1177/09567976261460772. This replicated Study 2 with adults at a large U.S. public university. **Changes in deadlines had a negligible effect** on all three performance metrics and several survey metrics. Evenly spaced external deadlines **were not especially effective** at reducing procrastination. The authors conclude the original result is not robust. Sample size was not visible in the open abstract. **[verified this session]** — [APS page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/09567976261460772/); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09567976261460772)
- Bisin & Hyndman (2020), "Present-bias, procrastination and deadlines in a field experiment," *Games and Economic Behavior*, 119, 339–357. Students did demand self-imposed deadlines, but those deadlines **did not improve** task completion. **[from prior knowledge, not re-verified]** — [DOI 10.1016/j.geb.2019.11.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geb.2019.11.010)
- Burger, Charness & Lynham (2011), "Field and online experiments on self-control," *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*, 77(3), 393–404. Interim deadlines on a study task did **not** improve completion. **[from prior knowledge, not re-verified]** — [DOI 10.1016/j.jebo.2010.11.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2010.11.010)
- Parkinson-type effects: Bryan & Locke (1967), "Parkinson's Law as a goal-setting phenomenon," *Organizational Behavior and Human Performance*, 2(3), 258–275. Participants given more time than needed worked more slowly; work pace tracked the implicit goal set by the time allowed. This was a small lab study. **[from prior knowledge, not re-verified]** — [DOI 10.1016/0030-5073(67)90021-9](https://doi.org/10.1016/0030-5073(67)90021-9)

### Inferences
- The product's "due end of week" works best framed as a **planning horizon**, not as an evidence-backed performance booster. The strongest supported mechanisms are proximal goals (section 3), implementation-intention cues (section 4) and the weekly landmark (section 5), not deadline pressure itself.
- Parkinson-type effects suggest that a week-long window for a 30-minute task invites drift. Pairing each task with a suggested slot (a cue) plus an estimated duration counters this better than the deadline alone.
- Because Ariely & Wertenbroch failed to replicate, any design rationale that cites "evenly spaced deadlines beat one final deadline" should be revised or carry an explicit "original finding did not replicate" note.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of interim deadlines in education was found in this session.
- The sample size of the 2026 replication was not retrieved.

## 7. Planning fallacy: do near-term, small chunks give better estimates?

### Takeaway
People reliably underestimate completion times for their own tasks (Buehler, Griffin & Ross 1994). Unpacking or segmenting tasks into subtasks reduces the underestimation in several studies. The evidence is small and conditional: it mostly involves short tasks, the effect is weaker for distant-future tasks, and it removes bias more than it improves precision.

### Cited Findings
- Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994), "Exploring the 'planning fallacy': Why people underestimate their task completion times," *JPSP*, 67(3), 366–381. Honors students predicted about 34 days to finish a thesis and took about 55. Fewer than half finished by their own "best guess" time. People focus on plan-based scenarios and neglect past experience. **[from prior knowledge, not re-verified]** — [DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366)
- Kruger & Evans (2004), "If you don't want to be late, enumerate: Unpacking reduces the planning fallacy," *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 40(5), 586–598. Listing subcomponents raised time estimates and reduced underestimation. Unpacking is less effective when a task has few components. **[verified this session at secondary level]** — [Princeton-hosted review PDF](https://ourapps.deptcpanel.princeton.edu/uploads/190805145602-A-7320.pdf); [backward planning paper discussing it](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/16/16101/jdm16101.html)
- Forsyth & Burt (2008), "Allocating time to future tasks: The effect of task segmentation on planning fallacy bias," *Memory & Cognition*, 36(4), 791–798. Segmented tasks received more allocated time than the same task presented whole, because summed subtask estimates exceed holistic ones. All tasks averaged under 2 hours. **[verified this session at secondary level]** — [DOI](https://dx.doi.org/10.3758/MC.36.4.791)
- Boundary conditions: unpacking is less effective when components are easy (Hadjichristidis, Summers & Thomas 2014) or when tasks are in the distant future (Moher 2012). Some interventions remove *bias* without improving *accuracy*, so people become as likely to overestimate. Other studies found no benefit (Byram 1997; Connolly & Dean 1997). **[verified this session at secondary level]** — [Colby CogBlog review](https://web.colby.edu/cogblog/?p=3204); [backward planning, JDM](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/16/16101/jdm16101.html)

### Inferences
- A weekly set of pre-decomposed, pre-estimated tasks applies the "unpacking" intervention for the learner. A week is near-term, which Moher's distant-future caveat suggests is the zone where unpacking works.
- Load sizing should use the product's own observed completion times (an outside view or reference class) rather than learner self-estimates. Buehler et al. show self-estimates neglect past experience, so calibrating from history is the textbook correction.
- A whole-curriculum view encourages holistic, plan-based estimates of the full journey ("I'll be done in 3 months"), which are the kind most prone to the planning fallacy. This is an inference.

### Gaps
- No study was found on planning-fallacy reduction for multi-week adult learning programs.
- Effect sizes for Kruger & Evans and Forsyth & Burt were not retrieved.

## 8. Robust vs contested: summary scorecard

### Takeaway
The best-supported pillars for "small weekly set" are implementation intentions (with the Dalton & Spiller limit on number of goals), proximal subgoals, task aversiveness and delay as procrastination drivers, the fresh start effect, and working-memory limits (as an analogy). Choice overload is moderator-dependent and contested. The Zeigarnik memory effect does not replicate. Ariely & Wertenbroch's deadline result failed a direct replication in 2026.

### Cited Findings
- **Robust or meta-analytically supported:**
  - Working-memory limit of about 3–5 chunks. The exact number is debated, but the limit itself is not. — [Cowan 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/)
  - Implementation intentions, d ≈ 0.65, across 94 tests. — [Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes)
  - Task aversiveness and delay as consistent procrastination predictors, across 691 correlations. — [Steel 2007](https://prism.ucalgary.ca/handle/1880/47914)
- **Supported but narrower or less replicated:**
  - Fresh start effect: large archival datasets plus a field RCT, but no meta-analysis or preregistered replication found. — [Beshears et al. 2021](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/using-fresh-starts-to-nudge-increased-retirement-savings/)
  - Proximal subgoals: classic study, child sample. — [Bandura & Schunk 1981](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/59faaf5b01b9500001e95457/5bc552d85141987915dab842_Bandura%20&%20Schunk,%201981.pdf)
  - Plan-making quiets unfulfilled goals: lab studies. — [Masicampo & Baumeister 2011](https://supp.apa.org:443/psycarticles/supplemental/a0024192/a0024192_supp.html)
  - Too many implementation intentions backfire: two studies. — [Dalton & Spiller 2012](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-664500.html)
  - Unpacking reduces planning fallacy: conditional effect. — [Forsyth & Burt 2008](https://dx.doi.org/10.3758/MC.36.4.791)
- **Contested:**
  - Choice overload: mean effect about zero in one meta-analysis; moderated effects in another. — [Scheibehenne et al. 2010](https://abcwest.cogs.indiana.edu/pmwiki/pdf/scheibehenne.options.2010.pdf) vs [Chernev et al. 2015](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual)
  - Cognitive load theory measurement: de Jong 2010 critique. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-009-9110-0)
- **Not supported or failed replication:**
  - Zeigarnik memory advantage. — [Ghibellini & Meier 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)
  - Ariely & Wertenbroch Study 2 deadline effect. — [Hyndman & Bisin 2026](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/09567976261460772/)

### Inferences
- The strongest single argument for the product design is a combination: a few tasks (Dalton & Spiller; CLT), each with a suggested when-and-where cue (Gollwitzer & Sheeran), framed as manageable and attainable (Bandura & Schunk), reset at a week landmark framed as a new beginning (Dai et al.), with future work held by the system so it does not intrude (Masicampo & Baumeister).
- Avoid leaning on the jam study, the Zeigarnik effect, or "deadlines boost performance" as headline rationale.
- Counter-consideration: hiding the curriculum could reduce perceived autonomy or the sense of a meaningful long-term goal. The design should keep the distal goal visible (why this week matters) while limiting the *actionable* set. Bandura & Schunk's distal-goal group did worse, but that group had *only* a distal goal, not a distal goal plus proximal steps.

### Gaps
- No study was found comparing "week view only" vs "full curriculum visible" in adult online learning.
- No autonomy or self-determination evidence on locked curricula was gathered in this session; that is a separate question worth researching.
