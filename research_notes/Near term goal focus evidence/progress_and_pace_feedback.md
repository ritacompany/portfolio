# Progress and pace feedback: how perceived progress drives motivation (applied to "2 of 4 done this week" + ahead/behind pace for the current Pathway)

Verification legend: [V] = confirmed against an abstract, publisher/repository page or multiple consistent secondary sources in this session. [U] = from prior knowledge or a single secondary source; not checked against the primary text in this session. Treat [U] items as leads to verify before quoting.

## Goal gradient effect (Hull 1932/1934; Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng 2006) and replications

### Takeaway
Effort rises as a reward gets closer, and perceived (even illusory) progress produces the same acceleration; the effect is well documented in field data but is not strictly monotonic, so a short, completable window (one week of 4 tasks) puts more of a learner's time near a "goal end" than a multi-month total does.

### Cited Findings
- Hull, C. L. (1932). "The goal gradient hypothesis and maze learning." Psychological Review, 39, 25–43. Proposed that animals move progressively faster as the goal is approached. [V citation; content via secondary sources] — [Springer reference list](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-94-009-3531-0_3); [FS blog summary](https://fs.blog/2016/10/goal-gradient-hypothesis/)
- Hull, C. L. (1934). "The rat's speed of locomotion gradient in the approach to food." Journal of Comparative Psychology, 17, 392–422. Measured the running-speed gradient directly; methods/results not checked in this session. [V citation only] — [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03327985)
- Fried (1966) found Hull-like locomotor gradients shifted when a light cue's location changed, concluding gradients index perception of relative position, i.e. perceived distance, not physical distance, drives the gradient. [V via abstract record] — [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03327985)
- Kivetz, R., Urminsky, O., & Zheng, Y. (2006). "The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected: Purchase Acceleration, Illusionary Goal Progress, and Customer Retention." Journal of Marketing Research, 43(1), 39–58. DOI 10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39. [V] — [Columbia Business School page](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal); [PDF](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1200/goalgradient.pdf)
  - Field setting 1: café "buy 10, get 1 free" card. Customers bought coffee more frequently the closer they were to the free coffee. [V] — [Columbia page](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal)
  - Field setting 2: online song-rating site. Users visited more often, rated more songs per visit and were less likely to end a session as they approached the reward threshold. [V] — same source
  - Illusory progress: a 12-stamp card with 2 pre-filled "bonus" stamps was completed faster than a plain 10-stamp card, though both required 10 purchases. [V] — same source
  - Stronger acceleration predicted greater retention and faster re-engagement in the program. [V] — same source
  - Authors rule out habituation, expiration concerns, other time trends and heterogeneity bias via statistical and experimental controls. [V] — same source
  - Exact effect sizes (e.g., inter-purchase time reductions) not extracted in this session. [Gap]
- Nunes, J. C., & Drèze, X. (2006). Endowed progress effect (Journal of Consumer Research, 32(4), 504–512 [U for volume/pages]). Car-wash field experiment, 300 cards: 8-space empty card vs 10-space card with 2 stamps pre-attached (same 8 washes needed). Over ~9 months, 34% vs 19% redeemed (≈79% relative increase; some blogs misreport "82%"). Effect stronger when a reason for the endowment is given, even a weak one. [V for figures via multiple secondary sources] — [Wharton Knowledge](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/?p=5630); [Coglode summary](https://coglode.com/nuggets/endowed-progress-effect)
- Counter-evidence on shape: Bonezzi, A., Brendl, C. M., & De Angelis, M. (2011). "Stuck in the Middle: The Psychophysics of Goal Pursuit." Psychological Science, 22(5), 607–612. Motivation can peak near the start and near the end and dip around the halfway point; which gradient appears depends on whether people use the start (completed) or the end (remaining) as reference point, because perceived marginal value of each step follows a diminishing-sensitivity curve. [V abstract] — [Kellogg page](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2011/stuck-in-the-middle-the-psychophysics-of-goal-pursuit); [APS journal page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797611404899/)
- Counter-evidence on "closer = always more effort": Louro, M. J., Pieters, R., & Zeelenberg, M. (2007). "Dynamics of multiple-goal pursuit." JPSP, 93(2), 174–193. Diary study plus two experiments: positive goal emotions from fast progress led to less effort on that goal when it was close (coasting), with emotions pushing behavior in opposite directions depending on proximity; authors frame this as a revision of the goal-gradient hypothesis. [V abstract] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645394/); [Tilburg](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/dynamics-of-multiple-goal-pursuit/)

### Inferences
- A 4-task weekly window keeps the learner on the steep end of the gradient more often than a months-long journey bar, where most time is spent in the "middle" Bonezzi et al. identify as the motivational trough.
- Endowed/illusory progress findings suggest that how the window is drawn (e.g., counting an already-done onboarding step) changes behavior even when the real work is unchanged; this is a lever, but it also carries a manipulation/trust risk if the learner notices.
- Loyalty-card evidence concerns discrete, extrinsic rewards (free coffee) with low-effort repeat actions; career-change tasks are effortful and self-set, so transfer is plausible but not demonstrated.

### Gaps
- No preregistered or direct replication of Kivetz et al. (2006) or Nunes & Drèze (2006) was found in this session; a search returned only the originals and secondary write-ups. Do not claim "widely replicated" without locating one.
- No meta-analysis of the goal-gradient effect in humans was found.

## Small-area hypothesis (Koo & Fishbach 2012) and framing a short window vs a long journey

### Takeaway
People are more motivated when attention is on whichever is smaller, completed or remaining, because each action then feels like a bigger proportional step; early in a pursuit show "done", late in it show "left".

### Cited Findings
- Koo, M., & Fishbach, A. (2012). "The Small-Area Hypothesis: Effects of Progress Monitoring on Goal Adherence." Journal of Consumer Research, 39(3), 493–509. DOI 10.1086/663827. [V citation] — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-663827.html); [OUP issue](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/issue/39/3)
  - Hypothesis: motivation is greater when attention is directed to the smaller of accumulated vs remaining progress (e.g., "20% done" beats "80% left" early; "20% left" beats "80% done" late). [V via press release] — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/04/120416113113.htm)
  - Field/loyalty-card studies (coffee shop and bagel/sushi-type purchase cards; the specific "sushi" detail is [U]): customers far from the reward were more motivated by cards highlighting completed purchases; those near the reward by cards highlighting remaining purchases. [V] — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/04/120416113113.htm); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/sticking-to-our-goals-whats-the-best-approach-for-success/)
  - Mechanism: a small area makes each action's marginal impact look larger, creating an illusion of fast progress (Study 3). [V via secondary summary] — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/sticking-to-our-goals-whats-the-best-approach-for-success/)
  - Boundary (Study 4): when people want to prolong a pursuit and avoid reaching the end state (e.g., an enjoyable activity), small-area focus slows them down. [V via secondary summary] — same source
  - Sample sizes/effect sizes per study not extracted. [Gap]
- Extension: a natural experiment on badges in a German Q&A community found small-area effects that nearly doubled user contributions near badge thresholds (ECIS 2015 / Business & Information Systems Engineering 2017). [V abstract-level] — [AIS eLibrary](https://aisel.aisnet.org/ecis2015_cr/137); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/binfse/v59y2017i4d10.1007_s12599-016-0441-5.html)
- Bonezzi et al. (2011) make a closely related reference-point argument (start vs end as anchor) and predict the mid-point slump. [V] — [Kellogg page](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2011/stuck-in-the-middle-the-psychophysics-of-goal-pursuit)

### Inferences
- On a 4-task week, both areas are always small (0–4 tasks), so the week frame avoids the worst case of a long journey where, for months, both "done" and "remaining" are large. This is the strongest theoretical argument for leading with the weekly count rather than the journey total.
- Per the hypothesis, "2 of 4 done" is neutral at the midpoint; at 1 of 4 the completed framing is optimal, and at 3 of 4 a remaining framing ("1 left") should be stronger. A dynamic label that switches framing at the midpoint is consistent with Koo & Fishbach, though not directly tested in a product UI.
- For the separate journey-progress page, the long frame will often sit in the "large-both" middle; splitting it into Pathways (subgoals) recreates small areas.

### Gaps
- No direct test of small-area framing on weekly task lists or learning products was found.
- Independent replications of Koo & Fishbach beyond the badge study were not located.

## Progress principle (Amabile & Kramer) and small wins: strength of evidence

### Takeaway
Daily diaries strongly associate making progress in meaningful work with better same-day emotion and motivation, and setbacks with worse; the data are large but correlational, from creative knowledge workers, and published mainly as a book and HBR articles rather than a peer-reviewed effect-size paper.

### Cited Findings
- Design: 238 professionals on 26 creative project teams in 7 companies across 3 industries; daily end-of-day diary emails with an open-ended "notable event" prompt plus ratings, for the length of each project (up to ~9 months); ~12,000 diary entries analyzed. [V] — [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-small-wins-unleash-creativity); [HBSWK item](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/6685.html)
- Finding: of all positive events, progress in meaningful work was the most powerful influence on "inner work life" (perceptions, emotions, motivations); setbacks were the most powerful negative event. [V] — same sources
- Asymmetry: setbacks' effect was reported as 2–3 times stronger than progress's effect. [V as reported by authors] — [HBSWK](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/6685.html)
- Even small wins counted; tangible incentives (pay, bonuses) barely appeared in diaries. [V as reported] — same source
- Publications: Amabile, T. M., & Kramer, S. J. (2007). "Inner Work Life: Understanding the Subtext of Business Performance." Harvard Business Review, May 2007 [U for exact issue]; Amabile & Kramer (2011). "The Power of Small Wins." HBR, May 2011 [U]; book: The Progress Principle (Harvard Business Review Press, 2011). [V book] — [O'Reilly listing](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/-/9781422142738)
- Peer-reviewed outputs from the same dataset exist (e.g., Amabile, Barsade, Mueller & Staw 2005, "Affect and creativity at work," Administrative Science Quarterly) [U], but no peer-reviewed paper reporting the "progress principle" effect sizes was found in this session. [Gap]

### Inferences
- Evidence quality: large N of observations, ecologically valid, but correlational and self-reported (progress and mood rated by the same person on the same day, so reverse causation and common-method bias are possible), and drawn from creative-team employees, not career changers or learners.
- The 2–3x setback asymmetry argues for designing the "behind" state carefully: a visible setback may weigh more than an equal visible win.
- "Meaningful" is a condition: ticking trivial tasks may not produce the effect, so the weekly tasks need to be recognisably meaningful to the learner's goal.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes and statistical model for the progress-to-inner-work-life association were not found in a peer-reviewed source.
- No independent replication of the progress principle as a named effect was located.

## Control theory / discrepancy reduction (Carver & Scheier 1990) and feedback interventions (Kluger & DeNisi 1996)

### Takeaway
Affect tracks the rate of progress relative to an expected rate, not position alone, which is exactly what an ahead/behind pace signal reports; but feedback is not reliably beneficial: about a third of feedback interventions reduce performance, especially when feedback pulls attention onto the self.

### Cited Findings
- Carver, C. S., & Scheier, M. F. (1990). "Origins and functions of positive and negative affect: A control-process view." Psychological Review, 97(1), 19–35. [V citation] — [World Database of Happiness](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/origins-and-functions-of-positive-and-negative-affect-a-control-process-view-9023/)
  - A second-order "meta-monitoring" loop compares rate of progress against a reference rate; faster than expected yields positive affect, slower yields negative affect. [V via Carver's later descriptions] — [Carnegie Mellon/KiltHub](https://kilthub.cmu.edu/articles/journal_contribution/Cybernetic_Control_Processes_and_the_Self-Regulation_of_Behavior/6614210); [OUP chapter](https://academic.oup.com/book/8733/chapter/154781788)
  - Prediction: positive affect (exceeding expected rate) leads to reduced subsequent effort, "coasting," which helps reallocate effort across multiple goals. [V via later descriptions] — same sources
  - Empirical support for coasting: Louro, Pieters & Zeelenberg (2007), above. [V] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645394/)
- Kluger, A. N., & DeNisi, A. (1996). "The effects of feedback interventions on performance: A historical review, a meta-analysis, and a preliminary feedback intervention theory." Psychological Bulletin, 119(2), 254–284. [V] — [Abstract (HUJI)](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/)
  - 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations (131 papers, ~12,652 participants per CEBMa's description). Mean d = .41, but over one third of feedback interventions decreased performance; not explained by sampling error or feedback sign. [V] — [Abstract](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/); [CEBMa REA](https://cebma.org/assets/Uploads/Files/REA-Feedback-Main-Findings.pdf)
  - Feedback Intervention Theory: effectiveness declines as feedback directs attention up the hierarchy toward the self (meta-task, self-esteem) and away from the task; moderated by task characteristics. [V] — [Abstract](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/); [Sage encyclopedia entry](https://methods.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/sage-encyclopedia-of-educational-research-measurement-evaluation/chpt/feedback-intervention-theory)
  - Positive and negative feedback both showed the harmful subset; sign was not the explanation. [V via Sage entry] — same source
  - Specific moderators reported in the paper (from prior knowledge): feedback that included goal-setting and that conveyed change from prior trials tended to help; feedback that threatened self-esteem or included praise/discouragement tended to hurt. [U]

### Inferences
- Pace (ahead/behind against a target date) is a near-direct operationalisation of Carver & Scheier's rate-of-progress signal, so it should carry more affective weight than a static "% complete."
- Two risks follow from the same theory: "ahead" can license coasting; "behind" generates negative affect. Whether "behind" mobilises or demoralises depends on whether the gap looks closable (expectancy), which Carver & Scheier also stress [U for exact framing].
- Per FIT, pace copy should point at the task ("1 task this week gets you back on pace") rather than the person ("you're falling behind"), keeping attention at the task level.

### Gaps
- No study was found that tests an "ahead/behind schedule" indicator specifically in learning or career-transition products.
- Kluger & DeNisi's moderator table values were not extracted.

## Monitoring goal progress (Harkin et al. 2016)

### Takeaway
Experimentally prompting people to monitor progress improves goal attainment (d ≈ 0.40), with larger effects when progress is physically recorded or reported; most evidence is from health behaviour.

### Cited Findings
- Harkin, B., Webb, T. L., Chang, B. P. I., Prestwich, A., Conner, M., Kellar, I., Benn, Y., & Sheeran, P. (2016). "Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence." Psychological Bulletin, 142(2), 198–229. DOI 10.1037/bul0000025. [V] — [White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)
  - 138 randomized studies, N = 19,951. Interventions increased monitoring frequency (d+ = 1.98) and goal attainment (d+ = 0.40, 95% CI 0.32–0.48). Change in monitoring frequency mediated the effect. [V] — same source
  - Larger effects when outcomes were reported to someone or made public, and when information was physically recorded. [V] — same source
  - Secondary subgroup figures (reported private d = 0.47 vs unreported 0.19; public d = 0.55 on ~3 studies with wide CI) not verified in the primary text. [U] — [Science for Work](https://scienceforwork.com/blog/goal-monitoring/)
  - Studies were mostly health goals (weight, smoking, diet, blood pressure); monitoring worked best combined with goal setting, action planning and feedback. [V via secondary summary] — same source

### Inferences
- Showing the weekly count supports monitoring, but the evidence is for active monitoring (the person records/checks); a passively displayed number may produce less than d = 0.40.
- "Physically recorded" and "reported" moderators favour having the learner mark tasks done themselves, and optionally share progress with a coach/peer.

### Gaps
- No moderator analysis on the time horizon of monitored goals (weekly vs long-term) was found.
- Generalisation to career/learning goals is untested within this meta-analysis.

## Self-efficacy from mastery experiences (Bandura 1977, 1997) and proximal subgoals

### Takeaway
Successful performance is the strongest source of self-efficacy, and proximal subgoals produce more of those successes than a distal goal: Bandura & Schunk (1981) found proximal subgoals beat distal goals on mastery, self-efficacy and intrinsic interest.

### Cited Findings
- Bandura, A. (1977). "Self-efficacy: Toward a unifying theory of behavioral change." Psychological Review, 84(2), 191–215. Four sources of efficacy: performance accomplishments (mastery), vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, physiological states; mastery is the most dependable. [U, not fetched; standard citation]
- Bandura, A. (1997). Self-Efficacy: The Exercise of Control. New York: W. H. Freeman. [U, not fetched]
- Bandura, A., & Schunk, D. H. (1981). "Cultivating competence, self-efficacy, and intrinsic interest through proximal self-motivation." JPSP, 41(3), 586–598. Children with marked deficits and disinterest in maths did self-directed learning under proximal subgoals, a distal goal, or no goal. Proximal subgoals produced rapid progress, substantial mastery, and higher self-efficacy and intrinsic interest than the other conditions. Rationale: proximal goals give immediate incentives and direction; distal goals are too far off to steer present effort. [V abstract] — [PDF](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/59faaf5b01b9500001e95457/5bc552d85141987915dab842_Bandura%20&%20Schunk,%201981.pdf); [ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED199284.pdf)
  - Sample size (n = 40 children is commonly cited) and the subtraction-task detail are [U].
- Counterpoint [U, not fetched]: Vancouver and colleagues (e.g., Vancouver, Thompson & Williams 2001, J. Applied Psychology; Vancouver et al. 2002) found within-person self-efficacy can be unrelated or negatively related to subsequent performance, consistent with control-theory coasting; Sitzmann & Yeo (2013, Personnel Psychology) meta-analysis reported within-person self-efficacy is largely a product of past performance rather than a strong cause of future performance. Verify before citing.

### Inferences
- The weekly task count is a structure for frequent mastery experiences; each completed task is evidence of capability, which matters for career changers who often doubt they "can" do the new field.
- Bandura & Schunk is the closest direct evidence for the product decision: proximal subgoals beat a distal goal on both performance and interest. It is a small, single study with children, so cite it as supportive, not decisive.

### Gaps
- No modern replication of Bandura & Schunk (1981) with adults was checked in this session.
- Effect sizes for proximal vs distal conditions not extracted.

## Fishbach & Dhar: progress vs commitment framing (when progress licenses disengagement)

### Takeaway
When people read progress as "I've done enough," it licenses goal-inconsistent choices; when they read the same progress as evidence of commitment ("this matters to me"), it sustains effort. Novices respond better to positive feedback, experts to negative.

### Cited Findings
- Fishbach, A., & Dhar, R. (2005). "Goals as Excuses or Guides: The Liberating Effect of Perceived Goal Progress on Choice." Journal of Consumer Research, 32(3), 370–377. DOI 10.1086/497548. Four studies: perceived progress on a goal liberated people to pursue inconsistent goals; merely planning future progress also licensed incongruent immediate choices. Illustrative study: dieters reminded of their progress toward ideal weight were more likely to choose a chocolate bar over an apple as a gift [chocolate-choice result direction from secondary sources]. [V abstract; details via secondary] — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v32y2005i3p370-377.html); [Psychology Today (McGonigal)](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-science-willpower/201112/the-problem-progress-why-succeeding-your-goals-can-sabotage-your)
- Fishbach, A., Eyal, T., & Finkelstein, S. R. (2010). "How positive and negative feedback motivate goal pursuit." Social and Personality Psychology Compass, 4(8), 517–530. Review: positive feedback motivates when it signals commitment; negative feedback motivates when it signals insufficient progress. Novices (uncertain commitment) respond to positive feedback; experts (committed) monitor rate of progress and respond to negative feedback. [V abstract] — [BGU research portal](https://cris.bgu.ac.il/en/publications/how-positive-and-negative-feedback-motivate-goal-pursuit/)
- Finkelstein, S. R., & Fishbach, A. (2012). "Tell Me What I Did Wrong: Experts Seek and Respond to Negative Feedback." Journal of Consumer Research, 39(1), 22–38 [U pages]. Across domains, novices sought and responded to positive feedback; experts to negative. [V finding] — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-661934.html)
- Related: Fishbach & Dhar's commitment/progress distinction is also framed as "highlighting commitment rather than progress" in later work (Koo & Fishbach 2008 JPSP "Dynamics of self-regulation") [U].

### Inferences
- Career changers are novices in the target field and early in a transition, uncertain of commitment; positive, commitment-affirming progress messages ("you've shown up 3 weeks running") should suit them better than shortfall messages.
- "Ahead of pace" risks a progress reading ("I can ease off"); pairing it with a commitment reading ("you're building the habit") is the Fishbach-consistent mitigation.
- Completing all 4 tasks early in the week may license disengagement for the rest of the week; this is acceptable if the weekly set is the intended dose, but not if the product wants continued engagement.

### Gaps
- Fishbach & Dhar sample sizes and effect sizes not extracted.
- No study found testing commitment vs progress framing on a learning-app progress display.

## Negative feedback, falling behind, "what-the-hell" effect, and non-judgmental framing

### Takeaway
After a perceived lapse, people with rigid all-or-nothing standards tend to abandon the goal for the rest of the period (counterregulation / "what-the-hell"); self-compassionate framing after a lapse reduced this in a lab study, supporting a non-judgmental, recoverable "behind" message.

### Cited Findings
- Herman, C. P., & Mack, D. (1975). Restrained eaters given a milkshake preload (one or two) ate more ice cream afterwards than restrained eaters with no preload; unrestrained eaters showed the opposite (compensation). Framed as counterregulation/disinhibition. Journal of Personality, 43, 647–660 [U citation details]. [V finding via secondary] — [SSIB Ingestive Classics](https://www.ssib.org/web/classic17.php)
- Boundary model (Herman & Polivy): a lapse pushes restrained eaters past a self-imposed diet boundary, after which they eat toward satiety. Limits: counterregulation is seen mainly in normal-weight restrained eaters and people high in disinhibition; overweight restrained eaters have not reliably counterregulated. [V via secondary] — [SSIB](https://www.ssib.org/web/classic17.php); [Penn State record](https://healthyaging.psu.edu/research/publications/counter-regulation-among-restrained-eaters-result-motivated-overeating)
- The "what-the-hell effect" label is attributed to Polivy & Herman (e.g., Breaking the Diet Habit, 1983) [U].
- Cochran, W., & Tesser, A. (1996). "The 'What the Hell' Effect: Some Effects of Goal Proximity and Goal Framing on Performance." In L. L. Martin & A. Tesser (Eds.), Striving and Feeling: Interactions Among Goals, Affect, and Self-Regulation (pp. 99–120 [U]). Lawrence Erlbaum. Citation confirmed; methods/results not retrieved. Reported (from prior knowledge, [U]): when performance was framed as needing to avoid any failure ("prevention"/perfect-record framing), an early failure led to greater disengagement than when framed as accumulating successes, especially early in the task. Verify before citing. — [Routledge book page](https://www.routledge.com/Striving-and-Feeling-Interactions-Among-Goals-Affect-and-Self-regulation/Martin-Tesser/p/book/9780805820393)
- Marlatt's abstinence violation effect (relapse prevention) is the addiction-literature analogue: a single lapse, attributed to stable personal failure, escalates into full relapse [U, not fetched].
- Adams, C. E., & Leary, M. R. (2007). "Promoting self-compassionate attitudes toward eating among restrictive and guilty eaters." Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 26(10), 1120–1144. DOI 10.1521/jscp.2007.26.10.1120. College women; after an unhealthy preload, a self-compassion induction reduced distress and subsequent eating, but only among the most restrictive eaters. Doughnut/candy protocol details [U]. [V abstract] — [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/782823); [PMC review citing it](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4249830/)
- Breines, J. G., & Chen, S. (2012). "Self-compassion increases self-improvement motivation." Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 38(9), 1133–1143: self-compassion after a failure increased effort to improve (e.g., more study time after a failed test) [U, not fetched].
- Amabile & Kramer's 2–3x setback asymmetry (above) also implies "behind" states weigh heavily. [V as reported] — [HBSWK](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/6685.html)

### Inferences
- A week marked "0 of 4" or a stage marked "behind" can act like a broken diet boundary; recoverable framing (what it takes to get back on pace, a fresh-start at the next week) should reduce abandonment. Weekly resets themselves function as boundaries that limit how long a lapse lasts.
- The pace signal should avoid rigid perfect-record framing (streaks that zero out) given Cochran & Tesser and counterregulation evidence.
- Direct evidence that "non-judgmental pace framing" helps in a product context was not found; the support is analogical (self-compassion and lapse studies in eating/academic settings).

### Gaps
- Primary results of Cochran & Tesser (1996) not retrieved.
- No experiment found that compares judgmental vs neutral "behind schedule" messages on persistence in learning or work apps.

## Robust vs contested: summary judgement

### Takeaway
Robust: monitoring helps on average (Harkin), feedback helps on average but often backfires (Kluger & DeNisi), mastery and proximal goals build efficacy (Bandura), and perceived (not only real) progress shifts effort. Contested or fragile: the monotonic goal gradient, the size and causal status of the progress principle, whether positive affect from being ahead sustains or reduces effort, and the generality of single-lab framing effects.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analytic and well-established: Harkin et al. 2016 (d+ = 0.40, 138 RCTs) — [White Rose](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/); Kluger & DeNisi 1996 (d = .41; >1/3 negative) — [HUJI](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/)
- Field-tested, not found replicated: Kivetz et al. 2006 — [Columbia](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal); Nunes & Drèze 2006 — [Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/?p=5630)
- Contradicting a simple gradient: Bonezzi et al. 2011 mid-point slump — [Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2011/stuck-in-the-middle-the-psychophysics-of-goal-pursuit); Louro et al. 2007 coasting near goal — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645394/)
- Correlational/practitioner-published: Amabile & Kramer diary study — [HBSWK](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/6685.html)
- Conditional (direction depends on interpretation): Fishbach & Dhar 2005; Fishbach, Eyal & Finkelstein 2010 — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v32y2005i3p370-377.html); [BGU](https://cris.bgu.ac.il/en/publications/how-positive-and-negative-feedback-motivate-goal-pursuit/)
- Counterregulation limited to subgroups (normal-weight, high-disinhibition restrained eaters) — [SSIB](https://www.ssib.org/web/classic17.php)

### Inferences
- The design choice (weekly count on main screen, stage pace signal, journey total elsewhere) is well aligned with: proximal goals (Bandura & Schunk), small-area framing (Koo & Fishbach), rate-of-progress affect (Carver & Scheier) and monitoring (Harkin). It is weakly supported by the progress principle (correlational) and loyalty-card goal-gradient work (different domain).
- Main risks the literature flags: coasting/licensing when "ahead" or after finishing the week early (Carver & Scheier; Fishbach & Dhar; Louro et al.); demoralisation and abandonment when "behind" (Amabile & Kramer asymmetry; counterregulation; Kluger & DeNisi self-focus); and many of the framing effects come from marketing labs and loyalty programmes, not effortful learning.
- Hiding the journey total entirely is not supported by any evidence found; Bandura & Schunk and Koo & Fishbach argue for foregrounding the proximal frame, not removing the distal one. Keeping it on a separate page matches that.

### Gaps
- No study found that directly compares "weekly progress + pace" vs "progress toward a distant total" in a learning or career product.
- Large-scale replication status of the consumer-psychology framing effects (endowed progress, small-area, goals-as-excuses) is unknown from this session's searches; the replication-crisis literature on licensing effects (e.g., moral licensing meta-analyses showing small effects) was not checked.
