## The route existed, but nothing said how to move through it

Every career in the product came with a route: a Growth Journey of five Pathways, stages that run in a
fixed order. Around it sat an onboarding quiz, a way to pick a career and a plan to connect people's
calendars.

Each piece had been designed on its own. None of them said what someone sees first or what comes after
that. New features kept getting bent to fit around the gaps, and each one made the next harder to build.

## It had to keep the order and still fit around a life

The Pathways run in order, so the product had to carry something like a curriculum. It was also meant
to guide people through a career change, not hold them to a course schedule. Doing both left questions
nobody had answered yet.

How much of the route belongs in one week? What happens when someone falls behind, or gets ahead? What
does it do for someone who doesn't plan in a calendar, or doesn't want to connect one? And what happens
when someone steps away for a while and comes back?

## The closest products each solved half of it

CareerFoundry runs a fixed curriculum at a fixed pace, and if you fall behind the course doesn't move
for you. I kept its order and left the pacing. Duolingo shows a missed day without punishing it. Your
streak resets and you carry on, which is how falling behind should feel here too. Notion and Todoist
are flexible with no structure at all. They tell you what's due and nothing about where it leads, so I
took the calendar from them as a planning layer and left the blank page.

The career products missed from the other side. One offered so many paths that it was hard to know
where to start. Another stacked courses into a path with no sense of how they got you to a job.

None of them held structure and room to move at once, so the framework had to be worked out piece by
piece.
