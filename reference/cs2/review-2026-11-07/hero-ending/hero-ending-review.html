What this round is

The hero and the ending, worked as blocks for the first time. Until now both were only drafted inside the copy review. Three versions of each, built differently rather than reworded, in your voice from docs/VOICE-draft.md: the user as subject, the reason inside the sentence, your casing and effort, never person, people or someone.

Hero. Modelled on how Haven's hero works, not on its words: the left line says who Mondai is for and what it is, then the right column gives the user's situation and the project's context. The left line is shared by every Mondai case study, so each version's left line says nothing about the framework. The hero comes first, so it uses no product name the page introduces later (no Growth Journey, Pathway, Action Item or capacity), and none of them previews the mechanics the page explains. It says once that Mondai hasn't launched, which is why no ending says it again.

Ending. It closes a first build with no metrics and no before and after. Each version turns the missing result into something a reader can check: A names what will be counted and where you'd look if it comes back low, B is the hardest call and the test it still needs, C is the one rule the archive marks as unsettled and what would move it. All three come from the research on how founding designers end first build case studies (reports/How first build case studies end.md); the weak endings there, funding, colleague quotes and words like improved, are ruled out.

My picks: hero A and ending C. Hero A because it does Haven's job in your own Background words and doesn't preview in a list what the page goes on to show. Ending C because it names the one rule nobody can know is right yet and how real weeks would prove it wrong, and it passed the reader check in both rounds. Ending A, the one you accepted on 7 Nov, failed the re-check: all three readers found no design decision in it. Its fix is one line, written in its note, and gets checked before it goes into final-copy.md if you pick it. What would change my picks: for the hero that sounds most like you, B, since its left line is your own frame wording; if the hardest call in ending B is true for you, B is the warmer ending.

Hero, three versions

The current text in final-copy.md

Left: Designed as an AI guide for people from underrepresented backgrounds working their way into a tech career.

Right 1: Mondai builds each user a curriculum for the tech career they choose, and it can take months to finish. Most users don’t have the same hours free every week, so a plan that expects a steady pace would have them falling behind from the first week.

Right 2: So I designed a weekly framework that turns that curriculum into weeks the user can finish, planned around the hours they say they can give each week. Mondai hasn’t launched, so this case study shows the reasoning rather than results: how much lands in each week, how it fits around their calendar and what happens when a week runs short or ends early.

B: what existed, the question it left, then the answer
LeftDesigned as an AI career navigator for the next generation of hustlers from underrepresented backgrounds.
Right 1When I started on Mondai’s MVP, the curriculum its AI writes for each user already had a fixed order, and calendar integration was planned. There was no way yet to decide how much of it belongs in a single week, and the whole curriculum can take months.
Right 2The answer depends on the user, because most of them don’t have the same hours free every week. So I designed a weekly framework that plans each week around the hours they say they can give and still holds when a week runs short. Opens on the project instead of the user: what was there when you started and the one question it left open, so the reader meets the design problem as a question before the answer. The left line is your own frame wording (AI tech career navigator, the next generation of hustlers) with the audience added, so it keeps your voice on the line every Mondai case study shares. Weaker than A because the user's situation arrives second, after the reader has already been told about a fixed order and calendar plans they have no reason to care about yet.

C: two users, one curriculum
LeftDesigned as a way for newcomers from underrepresented backgrounds to find a path into tech that fits around their life.
Right 1Two users can choose the same tech career and have very different weeks. One can give 5 hours a week and the other 15, and either can have a week where almost nothing gets done. Mondai’s AI writes each of them a curriculum that can take months, so one framework has to work for both.
Right 2I designed the weekly framework that turns it into weeks they can finish, planned from the hours they say they can give. Mondai hasn’t launched, so this case study shows the reasoning: how much lands in a week and what happens when a week runs short.

A worked contrast first, your habit of real numbers: 5 hours against 15 is the archive's own example of why the plan has to come from the user. The left line copies Haven's Designed as a way for form word for word in shape. The most memorable opening of the three, but it keeps the three topic preview at the end, which the page then delivers anyway, and the left line runs longest.

Ending, three versions

The current text in final-copy.md

Eyebrow: Where it stands

Headline: The framework is built, so the question left is whether it works for the users it was made for.

Body 1: The framework is meant to give the user weeks they can actually finish, so the measure I’m watching is how many planned weeks close with every Action Item done, and how often Rollover Action Items clear in the week they land in.

Body 2: A user whose work keeps rolling over is the sign the plan doesn’t fit their life, which is the case Recalibration was built for. Testing with the first users comes next, and these are the two numbers I’ll count from it.

A: what success is, the two counts and where I'd look first
EyebrowWhere it stands
HeadlineThe framework is meant to give each user weeks they can finish, so the first thing I’ll count is how many they do.
Body 1Testing with the first users comes next, and I’ll count two things: how many planned weeks close with every Action Item done, and how often a Rollover Action Item gets done in the week it lands in.
Body 2Those counts become the baseline. If they’re low for most users, the first place I’d look is how much effort each Action Item is given, since that decides how many fit into a week.

Failed the reader check re-check: all three readers said it states no design decision (in round 1 it passed 2 of 3), because two counts read as a measure, not a choice with a reason. That is why it is not my pick any more. If you want it, the fix is one line: Body 1 says I chose those two counts because each shows whether a week actually fit; I run it through the check before it goes into final-copy.md. What it is: the version you accepted on 7 Nov, rebuilt: the headline now says what the framework is for, instead of telling the reader it has not launched again (the hero already does), and the second paragraph swaps the line that re-explained Recalibration for what the counts would tell you and where you would look first if they come back low. That turns a missing result into a test with a next step. It closes the whole case study, since finishing weeks is what every block was for. When counts exist, Body 2's first sentence becomes x of n and nothing else moves.

B: the hardest call and the test it still needs
EyebrowLooking back
HeadlineThe hardest call was what to do when a user can’t finish a week, because every answer costs them something.
Body 1I chose to move the work forward and keep the target where it was, and it’s the decision I most want real weeks to test.
Body 2What I’ll be looking for is whether Rollover helps a user recover or slowly piles up on them: whether a user whose work rolls over finishes the next week, or rolls it over again.

The reflection shape: the hard part, what to validate and how you would know. It reads the most like you talking. The catch: hardest call is your judgment, not something in the record, so it ships only if it is true for you. If another decision was harder, the headline changes and the rest holds.

C: the least settled number and what would move itMy pick
EyebrowWhat’s still open
HeadlineThe least settled rule is the one for when Recalibration steps in, because no user had lived a week with the plan yet.
Body 1Right now it steps in after three weeks in a row where a user finishes less than about half the hours they set. That line is a starting point, and the first users’ weeks will move it.
Body 2It comes too early if most users who see it skip it and catch up anyway, and too late if users stop coming back before it ever shows.

My pick. The most specific of the three: a real rule from the archive that the page never states (three weeks under about half the hours set, marked directional there), with a test that can go wrong in both directions, each one something you can watch happen. It is the most honest way to close a first build: it names what you don't know yet instead of what you hope. It passed the reader check in both rounds, where A did not, and unlike B it claims nothing that is only your judgment. The risk: it closes on one rule rather than the whole case study, so it reads like a last word on Recalibration. What would change it: if you want the ending to speak for the whole page, A with its one line fix.
