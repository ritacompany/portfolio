Hiring bars in tech keep rising, and access has not kept up. Mondai is a career navigator built for the people the map leaves out: it turns a person's goals into a plan they can follow.

I met our founder when he was my bootcamp mentor, and the mentorship outlasted the program. One day he asked me to design a product he had been building in his head for years, and I took the role. I still consider him my mentor, but I now sit beside him as one of four voices deciding what Mondai becomes.

At the start I was the only designer, so every decision ran through me. That was fine until the product needed more design than I had hours for. Adding designers gave us the hours, but the reasoning behind everything we had already decided still only existed in my head, so I gave them the work with fewer dependencies and took the more complex pieces myself. Getting it out of my head and into something the team could use is what the job turned into.

The product outgrew how we were building it

This was the team's first time building around AI, so one of our early research goals was simply to learn how people felt about it. They did not trust it. They wanted a tool, not an answer. I argued that was the opportunity rather than the obstacle: a product that shows its reasoning is how you earn trust, and people should feel in control of the AI, not the other way around.

Our task at the start was to design the set of features the founder laid out, so that is what we did. Each designer owned a piece and we stitched them together, which was reasonable for an MVP. The gaps between those pieces got harder to reconcile the more we built, because nothing underneath held them together. A product whose behavior adapts to each person, and stays legible while it does, needs a structure that can absorb that variation. We did not have one, so every change rippled.

I brought it to the whole team and made the case that structure had to come before more features. They got behind it, and it went on the roadmap ahead of new work. From then on my job was not just building screens. It was building the foundation the screens could stand on.

Arguing the calls that shaped it

Calendar integration was one of the founder's original ideas, a way to help someone find room for a new career inside a life that was already full. Designing it feature first buried the real question: how much structure helps someone build a habit, and when does it start to feel like the traditional education path this product exists to be an alternative to.

That question turned into one of the most productive stretches of dialogue I have had with our founder. He believed in structure, the kind that gets someone to actually show up week over week. I believed the moment a schedule marks someone as behind, it starts recreating the rigid education system this product exists to replace. Over a handful of long conversations we were each holding a real piece of the problem the other one had not seen yet. He was thinking about consistency and follow-through. I was thinking about how one missed day can make someone feel like they are already behind, and how that feeling is usually what makes someone quietly stop trying. What came out of those conversations was not either of our starting positions. He told me afterward that he had been trying to figure out how to give the user more freedom for a while and kept hitting a wall, and this finally showed him a way through. I had been circling it too, from the other side.

I took what came out of that conversation and designed a weekly framework: a set of tasks free to move across the week instead of built around the calendar. I brought it back to him before calling it done, and when we put it in front of people it barely needed explaining. That was the version that stuck.

Building systems instead of being one

The pattern underneath all of this: the more the product grew, the more every decision still routed back through me. The fix was never another answer from me. It was building the things that let other people arrive at the answer without me in the room.

A shared board for what design could not decide alone. Plenty of design work stalls waiting on someone else, a strategy call, a stakeholder answer. I built a tracked board that carried a status for exactly that, so anything blocked was visible and owned rather than quietly waiting. Strategy could see what sat on them without being chased, and work stopped stalling in silence.

A review surface for the founder. He told me plainly he was getting lost in the design files and wanted one place to see finished work with the reasoning beside it. Rather than treat that as his problem, I built that surface and blocked time in our weekly to walk it together. Approvals stopped being the thing everything waited on.

The weekly meetings are the rhythm all of this runs on. When I shipped the roadmap across the org I had each sub-team stand up its own weekly, so the cadence did not depend on me convening it. I show up to mine with an agenda and a position, not just attendance.

Growing designers instead of assigning them

The designers I have worked with have been volunteers with anywhere from a few hours a week to twenty, at very different points in their careers. Managing that like a staffed team does not work, and I stopped trying early.

Reviews are recorded and built around the criteria rather than the fixes, because a fix improves one screen and a criterion improves every screen after it. When I ask for changes I say why, and I make a point of saying when a change is not a correction, because people quietly assume it is. My standing ask in meetings is that designers explain the why behind their ideas, their research, their tradeoffs. I have built more than one designer's training around how they told me they learn best, which is the same courtesy we extend to users. One was matched to motion work because that is what she is strongest at and what she wanted for her own portfolio, so the assignment served her and the product at once.

Some of it has to be taught directly. At one point nobody on the team felt confident with design systems, components, variables or auto layout, which was quietly slowing everything down and turning my approvals into rework. So I ran a workshop on all four. Not because teaching is my favourite use of an evening, but because the alternative was correcting the same things forever.

I also pass on what I find. When Figma released Make I put it in front of the whole design team the same week, with a note about the free tier limits so people would write thorough prompts instead of burning their attempts. Two of them were using it on live tasks within a fortnight.

The line I have used with the team is that I am not the teacher handing out tasks. What I want is people who own a problem, and that has taken deliberate work to build rather than announce.

In 2026 I put three of them forward for promotion. Two to product designer two, and one to senior accessibility designer, a discipline she had grown into from a standing start and now owns across the product. All three came in as volunteers with no formal design role. That is the outcome I am proudest of here, and it is the one that outlasts anything I designed.

Making the work move without me in the room

By mid 2025 we were delivering against a hard date while a restructure moved engineering onto its own team, which risked cutting design out of delivery entirely. I made the case for keeping the pipeline I had built intact, spanning design, strategy and development, before that meeting rather than after it.

Delivering against it exposed the real problem. Work was arriving built on different assumptions about layout, components and responsiveness, which meant it got reconciled after the fact, by hand, at a cost of weeks. That was not a performance problem on anyone's part. The agreement had never been written down before the work started, so we were paying for it afterward every time.

So the agreement moved earlier:

  • A documented layout standard, written in far more detail than I wanted to write it, living inside the design file where designers actually are.
  • Exploration separated from delivery, because they are different rooms with different rules.
  • Review states between design and development, so status is a fact rather than a feeling.
  • An audit before anything ships, including an accessibility pass, because a product built for people who get excluded by default cannot treat that as a later problem.
  • Named ownership on sign-off.

Language got the same treatment. A team working separately will invent four names for the same object and all four will end up somewhere, so I set up a shared terminology board with additions subject to approval. Once it existed and was being used, it moved to content, where it belonged. Building something and handing it to the right owner is usually the end state I want.

The hard date held. Engineering built from the handoff file without a meeting to decode it, and the two heaviest delivery months in the product's history ran through that pipeline.

Deciding what gets built next

The last thing still being argued rather than decided was priority. Everything felt urgent to whoever raised it, and design and engineering were sizing the same work from opposite sides with no shared way to compare.

So I built a scoring model in Notion, and wrote the guide for using it, which mattered more than the model itself. Engineering rates effort, design rates value, both on a plain T-shirt scale with definitions anyone can apply without asking:

  • XS, tiny polish. Nice to see, but nobody misses it if it is gone.
  • M, meaningful fix. Solves a specific flow or a common friction point.
  • XL, the human priority. Without it the user is blocked or the product fails at what it exists to do.

The pair of scores lands each item in a Must, Should, Could or Won't bucket. High value against low effort is a no-brainer. Low value against high effort goes to the backlog and we stop discussing it.

What it cut is the part I would point to. A fix design had flagged in the password field turned out to be behaviour engineering had disabled deliberately for security, which the board caught before anyone spent a day on it. A demographic question got taken out of onboarding entirely. One item that scored its way into the backlog was an idea of mine, redundant against the effort it needed, which is a reasonable outcome for a system nobody gets to overrule.

It also quietly became somewhere decisions go to stay decided. Every row carries the reasoning beside it, so a question settled in March is still settled in September, and whoever raises it again finds the answer already sitting there.

How my practice changed while I was doing this

I started experimenting with AI in my process in 2023, before it was standard practice, using it to sharpen research questions on an early project. Since then it has moved from something I used occasionally to something the operation runs on, and the useful version arrived later than the hype did.

The turn came when I hit a real limit. I was working long documents through a model that quietly lost information from earlier in the conversation, so outputs started dropping things I had already settled and I would not notice until much later. I researched which tools held context best, moved, and immediately put the difference to work.

The clearest example is the design handbooks. Three designers were each about to start on a different part of a complex feature. Previously that meant a verbal briefing and a week of clarifying questions coming back at me one at a time. Instead I spent an evening producing a written handbook for each of them, covering their part in real detail, and told them up front that the first pass would not be perfect and we would walk it together. They arrived at the next meeting already oriented, and the questions I got were about the design rather than about what the task was.

It also made research affordable at a scale I could not otherwise reach. Integrating calendar colors meant working out how two platforms handle them differently and which colors have no equivalent across them, and I brought a comparison and a recommendation to that conversation instead of a hunch. That is a week of work I do not have, done in an afternoon.

The honest part: it is not always faster. There are times I have gone a long way down a course I did not need because the tool was confidently wrong, and catching that is its own skill. The boundary I set early still holds. I do not use AI to decide what good looks like. I use it to apply what we already decided good looks like, and I throw out the passes that miss.

What it adds up to

Design at Mondai went from one person making screens to a function that sets direction. The thing I flagged alone in the first year, that structure has to come before features, is now the default the leadership group builds from. I came in through a bootcamp mentorship and now sit as one of four people deciding what the product becomes.

What comes next is the same operation pointed at the mobile product. Most of that build is moving to engineering, and design owns the decisions that actually need a designer, starting with navigation. The point of everything above is that this no longer depends on me being in every room. The systems decide, the team builds, and I get to work on what only a designer should.
