## Chadwick's own rewrite of the "arguing the calls" beat (his voice, use as the base for beat 2, still needs condensing and a wow moment)

"Calendar integration was one of the founder's original ideas, a way to help someone find room for a new career inside a life that was already full. This was during an early design phase, when the design team consisted of me and two other designers all working on a part-time volunteer basis. It was about turning the founder's ideas directly into designs. It was an idea that lacked a foundation to operate within. So I brought this up to the founder which led to a series of ideation sessions between the two of us revolving primarily around two conflicting but important perspectives. He stressed the importance of structure, the kind that gets someone to consistently show up for work. Structure that creates a much needed sense of accountability. I expressed concern about how an overly rigid system would lead to cognitive consequences that would lead to issues with retention and add unnecessary stress for a tool that is supposed to make the user's already busy lives less complicated. A tool that is supposed to guide their careers, rather than provide a traditional curriculum. These series of meetings provided clarity to a wall that both of us kept hitting individually. It allowed us to move forward and push developing a framework that provided structure, but also maintained its role as a guide. A trajectory that could fit perfectly into user's often chaotic lives. I brought it back to him before calling it done, and when we put it in front of people it barely needed explaining. That was the version that stuck."

## Chadwick's own draft material for the systems beat (use as real evidence for beat 3, in his voice)

On the approval pipeline change (design to Chadwick to founder to dev):
"With unpredictable hours and levels of dedication, progress on a part-time team needs careful tracking. Which means the approval pipeline needs to be tight, but flexible. This is particularly true when it comes to design hand-offs. Originally the next step was getting the founder's final approval and once that was set, the designs were ready for hand-offs. However, a pattern started emerging where things started to slowly fall behind during the pipeline. A reliance on multiple individuals with part-time schedules getting to their own approvals on time became difficult to manage. After multiple discussions between myself, our lead developer and our founder, the founder vocalized that he trusted my design decisions and very infrequently did anything need modified once it got to him. So we decided to make a modification to the preexisting pipeline. I could hand things off to dev, before the founder was able to give final approval. This decision created a rapid increase in our ability to push things forward and cut the hand-off process time in half. Meaning the product shipped faster and by the time designs got to the founder they were already half developed."

On the review system:
"All designs that each designer worked on went through me. We had a team of 5-6 designers. Volunteers with anywhere from a few hours a week to twenty, at very different points in their careers. Managing that like a staffed team does not work. So I built a review system that was recorded based on criteria rather than fixes, because a fix improves one screen and a criteria improves every screen after it. Often when their designs got to me they needed tweaking, so I would walk them through any of the changes I needed to make so there was never a question as to why. The most important part of this process is that I have a clear understanding of what led to their decisions, and what research was conducted to support those decisions."

On the decision tracker:
"The more the product grew, the more we needed a way to keep track of decisions. It was quickly becoming a larger team of individuals making separate decisions and we needed a way to keep aligned. As a design team, we needed a way to flag any decisions that needed more input from team members outside of design before moving forward. I built a tracked board that carried a status for exactly that, so anything blocked was visible and owned rather than quietly waiting. The tracker also provided a way for the design team to communicate any blockers that physically held up design."

On ceremonies:
"We had two recurring team meetings per week: a full team meeting and a focus team meeting. Full team meetings spanned around 25-30 people across engineering, product, and strategy. I served as the primary design voice, presenting progress, surfacing blockers and advocating for design decisions at the team level. Our weekly design meetings structured the team's sprint planning. For our design team meetings I developed a format of 10-15 minutes of stand up followed by whatever core topic was most relevant, which often meant workshops. At one point nobody on the team felt confident with design systems, components, variables or auto layout, which was quietly slowing everything down and turning my approvals into hours of rework. So I ran workshops on all four."
