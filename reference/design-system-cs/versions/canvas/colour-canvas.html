Colour · Version A · T3I

the audit as the method. Told in the order the work happened: count, rule, write the rules down, clean the pickers, then the test.

Covers: P1, P2, P3, P4, P5, P6, P7, P8

Colour

Green labels across the screens came in five different greens, and the card outlines came in four greys, with nothing saying which one was right.

Before choosing anything I counted. Every colour in the library (3,555 paints) and about 40,000 on the MVP screens, grouped by the job it does instead of by its value. Each job then got one token, set at the colour the screens already used most for it.

The pickers were their own problem. Raw values were 65% to 78% of every colour picker, so designers and agents mostly saw colours with no job attached, and I found it confusing myself. I took the raw values out so everyone picks roles, and renamed three roles for what they paint. Text/Placeholder became Text/Meta, since 2 of its 213 layers were placeholders. Nothing changed colour. A leaner public palette, such as Radix or Material 3, would have recoloured the screens.

Gold is not in the system. It never had a value or a variable, and it spread only because it had been written down and repeated. The product is dark only, one mode, so nothing waits on a light theme.

Later, fresh agents rebuilt a known screen from the library alone. Every colour they placed sat on a published role. They mostly skipped Text/Display, the brightest text, for names and titles, because the name reads as a size.

Colour · Version B · T2I

the rejected option first. The tempting fix was to merge colours that share a value; the section argues against it, then shows what judging by purpose produced and how it was tested.

Covers: P1, P2, P3, P4, P5, P6, P7, P8

Colour

Cards, fields and buttons all had outlines in a handful of greys, and the shared values hid which of them were doing the same job.

The quick fix was to merge every colour that shares a value into one token. I ruled it out. A button and a field both have an outline and still do different jobs, so every element was compared by what it does and how it is used.

First I counted. I grouped the library's 3,555 paints and about 40,000 on the MVP screens by job and gave each job one token, at the colour the screens already used most for it. Then 11 sheets, one kind of element each, ruled one at a time. Accent text went to one green across 551 layers; 6 went back because pathway progress is not accent text.

I wrote one call as arithmetic so nobody has to judge it by feel. An icon beside text is one step darker than the text. An agent can check that as easily as I can.

Card outlines came in four greys. I gave cards the grey the navigation already uses and raised areas inside a card a slightly lighter grey. States keep their own: a finished item is darker on purpose.

Then I turned to the colour pickers. Raw values were 65% to 78% of every colour picker, and I found the system confusing to use myself. I took them out, so people and agents pick roles only, and renamed three roles for what they paint, with no layer changing colour. Moving to a leaner public palette, such as Radix or Material 3, would have recoloured the screens.

Fresh agents later rebuilt a known screen from the library alone. Every colour on the layers they made sat on a published role. They mostly skipped Text/Display, the brightest text, for names and titles, because the name reads as a size.

Gold is not part of the system. It never had a value or a variable, and it spread because it was written down and repeated. The product is dark only, one mode, so no work waits on a light theme.

Colour · Version C · T1I

the reader's seat. Starts from what anyone picking a colour saw, leads with the agent test as the image, then explains the decisions that made the result possible.

Covers: P1, P2, P3, P4, P5, P6, P7, P8

Colour

Anyone picking a colour, a designer or an agent, saw mostly raw values with no job attached and had to guess which green meant what.

Raw values were 65% to 78% of every colour picker, and I found it confusing myself. So I started where the colours were used: the library's 3,555 paints and about 40,000 on the MVP screens, grouped by the job each one does. Every job got one token, at the colour the screens already used most for it. Then I ruled 11 sheets by purpose, never by shared value. A button and a field both have outlines and still differ in what they do.

An icon beside text is one step darker than its text, so that call works like arithmetic. An agent can check it as easily as a person.

Then I took the raw values out of the pickers and renamed three roles for what they paint. No layer changed colour. A leaner public palette, such as Radix or Material 3, would have recoloured the screens.

Gold is not in the system; it spread only because it was written down. The product is dark only, one mode.
