Hero and about — three structures — exhibit 04

Three ways this could be built

Not three wordings. Three different information architectures, each derived from one of the sites you sent. Hero and About together, because they have to be decided together.

Why the three lines keep stalling

They can't be settled before the structure is, because structure decides what job they do. In model 1 they are the centrepiece and have to carry the page. In model 2 they sit in a personal block and can be looser. In model 3 they are evidence for a claim and have to be concrete. Same three slots, three different writing problems. Pick a structure and the lines get much easier.

The Goodside shape. A one-line positioning statement sits small next to the wordmark, then the About page opens on belief and lets everything else follow from it. Closest to what you already have. The belief is the thesis and the three lines are the proof.

Hero — small text block

I design the systems underneath products, mostly for people the tech industry has never really designed for. Currently at Mondai, occasionally in Figma at 2am.

What's working

  • #06 Rule of three broken. Two straight clauses, then a third that undercuts. The 2am line is the deflation.
  • #39 Names the actual thing. Figma, Mondai, not "design tools" and "a startup."
  • Cost: the 2am joke is the only personality in the hero. If it doesn't land there's nothing else holding it.

About — role

I lead design at Mondai, a career tool for people the tech industry usually overlooks. Two years, unpaid, one of four people deciding what it becomes. I met the founder when he was my mentor. Now I'm one of the voices he argues with.

What's working

  • #38 Oddly precise numbers. Two, four. Counted, not estimated.
  • #14 Costly admission. "Unpaid" works against you, which is exactly why it reads as honest.
  • #19 Peak-end. The mentor reversal is the last thing read and the best line in the block.

Verdict

Lowest risk, highest dependency. It preserves everything you've already written and the sequence you said felt right. But it puts the entire weight of the page on three lines that aren't settled, which is why this model has felt stuck.

Pick this if you want to protect the existing opening and are willing to spend more time on the lines.

The Jon Howell shape. His profile page keeps two registers in separate adjacent blocks and never blends them: a straight career paragraph, then a personal one that mentions he is a lifelong Jimmy Buffett fan. The separation is what lets both be fully themselves.

Hero — small text block

Product designer. I lead design at Mondai, which is a career tool for people the industry skips, and I have been doing it unpaid for two years, which tells you something.

What's working

  • #45 Serious content, flat delivery. A real fact stated without weight.
  • #43 The unexplained reference. "Tells you something" refuses to say what. The reader finishes it.
  • Cost: spends the strongest fact in the hero, so About has to open with something else.
About — block one, professional register

Two years ago the founder of Mondai asked me to design a product he had been building in his head for a long time. He had been my mentor. I said yes, and I have been one of four people deciding what it becomes ever since. In that time it went from a set of screens to a system, and from one designer to five.

What's working

  • #30 Scene, not summary. Opens on an actual moment rather than a characterisation.
  • #41 Sentence length variance. Long, short, long.
  • Cost: more words before the reader learns anything about who you are.

What's working

  • #24 Says the quiet part. "This is the part I actually believe" names the register change out loud, which is what makes the switch legible instead of jarring.
  • #13 Pratfall ordering. Competence is fully established first, so warmth reads as secure rather than soft.
  • Structural payoff: the lines no longer have to carry the page, so weaker lines survive here.

Verdict

This is the one that solves your actual blocker. Because credibility is established before the belief block, the three lines stop being load-bearing. They become the warm close rather than the whole argument.

Cost: your belief opening no longer opens. You said you preferred belief first, and this contradicts that directly. Worth knowing that preference is what makes model 1 hard.

The Monolog shape. Opens on a problem in the world rather than a fact about you, then positions you as the person who noticed it. The founder statement is signed at the bottom, which turns the whole thing into an argument with an author.

Hero — small text block

The people who most need a way into tech are the ones the industry writes the least software for. I design for them. Currently at Mondai, where that is the entire point.

What's working

  • #30 Leads with the problem, so the reader agrees with something before being asked to be interested in you.
  • Cost: mission-forward openings can read as worthy. It needs the About page to get personal fast or the whole page stays cold.
About — the claim

Most career products assume you already know the unwritten rules. Ours cannot, because our users mostly don't, and that single fact changes almost every decision underneath the interface. I have spent two years working out what it changes.

What's working

  • #15 Preempts the objection. Names the assumption most products make before a reader can call it obvious.
  • #43 Withholds. It says a fact changes everything and refuses to list what. That gap pulls the reader down the page.

Verdict

Highest ceiling, least like a portfolio. It is the only model where a hiring manager reads an argument rather than a description, and the signature is a move nobody else makes.

Cost: it is the furthest from what you have, and it makes the belief block share the page with a thesis rather than owning it.
