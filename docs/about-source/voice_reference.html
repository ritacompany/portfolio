The real principle, from your own note on Craig Jackson

Good personal copy names a specific, private, maybe-weird thing — a quirk, a habit, a thought you assumed was just yours — and someone reading it goes “wait, same.” The connection isn't the detail itself. It's the recognition. That's a sharper design principle than anything in the 7 examples below, and it's yours, not mine.

The mechanic that showed up in every good one — “the deflating third item”

State real credentials, then let one item break the pattern on purpose. Sloan: writer, printer, & manufacturer. Appleton: designer, anthropologist, and mediocre developer. Baz: cook, video host, recipe developer cookbook author, weenie lover and caesar salad enthusiast. Trixie Mattel: singer, actor, drag queen and recognized “Skinny Legend.”

And this answers the doubt you raised on Kicks Condor. Baz and Mattel both have real commercial careers with brand deals and national press, and both put an absurd item directly inside the credential list rather than quarantining it in a fun section at the bottom. The move doesn’t require being an indie-web hobbyist with nothing to lose. It survives professionalization. What Kicks Condor shows is only the far end of the dial, not the price of admission.

The rubric — what “fit” actually measures 1–4 scale, forced choice, no safe middle

01Specificity

1 = trait-claim anyone could write (“proven track record”). 4 = a fact only this person could produce.

02Recognition, not detail

1 = detail admired from a distance. 4 = names something private enough that a stranger thinks “wait, same.”

03Register fit

1 = warm/cute or corporate-clean. 4 = reads as weird, high fashion, editorial — your stated lane, not a nearby one.

04Economy

1 = explains the joke or over-hedges. 4 = confident, says the thing once, stops.

05Unfakeable voice

1 = could be swapped onto any bio with a name-and-replace. 4 = the sentence dies if you change a single specific in it.

Correction to an earlier claim on this page. An earlier version asserted that writing for hiring managers caps how weird you can go. That was inferred from eight product designers, all in one conservative slice of tech, none of whom reported any hiring outcome. It was not supportable and it was wrong to narrow the search around it. Molly Baz and Trixie Mattel are direct counter-evidence.

What holds up. The anti-design/brutalist lane rarely writes About copy at all — it carries personality visually and withholds prose. And within SF product design specifically, flat credential bios are near-universal. Outside that lane, in food, drag and illustration, the same people with equally serious careers write with real voice. That’s a culture of one industry, not a constraint on what works.
